The Diode

The first component in this compilation that Ohm's law cannot solve. It is also the cheapest place to learn the method that every remaining part of Electronics depends on.

1.1 What changes when a component is not linear

Every element so far — resistor, capacitor, inductor — obeys a straight-line relationship between voltage and current. That is why node analysis worked: a linear circuit is a set of simultaneous linear equations, and Mathematics Part 5 solves those.

A diode does not. Its current rises exponentially with voltage:

A decade per 60 millivolts

Because VT ≈ 26 mV, multiplying the current by ten needs only VT ln 10 = 60 mV more. A diode carrying 1 mA sits at about 0.7 V; at 10 mA it sits at 0.76 V; at 100 mA, 0.82 V. Three orders of magnitude in current for a fifth of a volt. That is why "a diode drops 0.7 V" is a usable statement at all.

1.2 Three models, and when each is honest

Nobody solves the exponential by hand under exam pressure. Three approximations exist, and choosing between them is a real engineering decision:

  • Ideal. A perfect switch: zero volts when forward, zero current when reverse. Use it when the supply is tens of volts and 0.7 is noise.
  • Constant drop. A perfect switch in series with 0.7 V. The default for this exam.
  • Exponential. The real thing. Needed only when the question is about the diode equation — thermal voltage, saturation current, or a ratio of two currents.
Trap · when "ideal" is not close enough

With a 5 V supply the ideal model overstates the current by 16% — enough to pick the wrong option. With a 1 V supply it overstates it by 170%. The rule that actually works: if the supply is less than about ten times 0.7 V, keep the drop. Distractors on this exam are routinely built from exactly this omission.

1.3 The method: assume, solve, check

A diode has two states, and which one it is in depends on the answer you have not computed yet. That circularity is broken the same way every time:

  1. Assume a state — say, the diode is conducting.
  2. Solve the circuit as a linear one, replacing the diode with a 0.7 V source (or a short, or an open if you assumed off).
  3. Check the assumption against the result. A conducting diode must have positive current through it; a non-conducting one must have negative voltage across it. If the check fails, the other assumption was right.

Learn this here. Part 3 uses the identical procedure to decide whether a transistor is cut off, active or saturated — a three-state version of the same idea — and that is where most Electronics marks are won or lost.

Worked · a diode that turns out to be off

A 5 V source drives a 1 kΩ resistor to ground. A diode is connected from the resistor's midpoint to a 6 V rail, cathode at the midpoint. Is it conducting?

  1. Assume off. Then no current flows through the diode, and the midpoint sits at whatever the rest of the circuit says — here 5 V.
  2. Check. The anode is at 6 V and the cathode at 5 V, so the diode is forward-biased by 1 V. The assumption fails.
  3. Assume on. The cathode is then at 6 − 0.7 = 5.3 V, and current flows from the 6 V rail into the node.
  4. Check. Current through the diode is positive, so this assumption holds.

Two assumptions, two checks, thirty seconds. Never guess a diode's state from the picture — the whole point of the check is that intuition is unreliable once there is more than one source.

1.4 Reverse breakdown, put to work

Push a diode backwards hard enough and it conducts anyway, at a sharply defined voltage. In an ordinary diode that is a failure. In a Zener diode it is the product: the breakdown voltage is manufactured to a tolerance and the device is used deliberately in reverse.

Two opposite worst cases

This is the insight the plate is built to give. The Zener's power is worst when the load takes least, because everything Rs delivers has to go somewhere. Regulation is worst when the load takes most, because there may be nothing left over to keep the Zener in breakdown. A design has to survive both corners at once, which is why Rs has an upper and a lower bound rather than a single right value.

1.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • VT ≈ 26 mV at room temperature, and 60 mV per decade of current.
  • I = IS(e^(VD/nVT) − 1), with the −1 droppable once forward-biased.
  • Silicon drops 0.7 V forward; germanium and Schottky about 0.3 V.
  • Assume, solve, check. A conducting diode needs positive current; an off diode needs reverse voltage.
  • Zener: IZ = (Vin − VZ)/RS − VZ/RL, and the limit is PZ = VZ IZ.

A diode's exponential characteristic makes its forward voltage almost constant over decades of current, which is what makes the 0.7 V model both crude and excellent. The ideal model is not — at supply voltages of a few volts it is wrong by tens of percent.

Because the device has states, solving its circuit means assuming one, solving linearly, and checking. That loop is the single most transferable thing in this module: it is how transistors are solved too, and it is why Electronics questions reward care over speed.
