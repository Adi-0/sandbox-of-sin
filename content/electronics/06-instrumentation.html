Instrumentation

Two problems that every measurement has: reading a thing without changing it, and turning what the world does into a number. Neither is difficult, and both are routinely got wrong.

6.1 Every measurement is a load

A voltmeter is a resistor. Connecting it across a node puts that resistor in parallel with whatever was already there — so the act of measuring changes the circuit being measured.

Whether that matters is not a question about the meter alone. It is a ratio, and the thing it must be compared against is the source's Thévenin resistance from Circuit Analysis Part 4:

Trap · "10 MΩ is high impedance"

Ten megohms is a large number and it is not the answer to anything by itself. Loading error is a ratio. On a 50 kΩ divider a 10 MΩ meter is 200 times stiffer and reads 0.5% low — excellent. On a pH electrode with a source resistance of 100 MΩ, that same meter reads 9% of the true value. The instrument did not change; the source did.

6.2 Turning the world into volts

A transducer converts a physical quantity into an electrical one. Most of the ones this exam names change resistance, which means they need a circuit to turn that into a voltage.

  • Strain gauge — a foil grid whose resistance rises as it stretches. Fractional changes are tiny, around 0.1%.
  • RTD — a platinum resistance that rises with temperature, very linearly.
  • Thermistor — a semiconductor whose resistance falls sharply with temperature. Sensitive, and markedly non-linear.
  • Thermocouple — two dissimilar metals producing a voltage directly, tens of microvolts per degree.
Why a bridge and not just an amplifier

A strain gauge at 350 Ω changing by 0.1% becomes 350.35 Ω. Put it in a plain divider and the output moves from 2.500 V to 2.501 V — a 1 mV signal sitting on 2.5 V of offset. Amplify that by 1000 and the offset saturates the op-amp long before the signal is visible. The bridge subtracts the 2.5 V away first, leaving only the millivolt to be amplified. Subtract, then amplify — never the other way round.

6.3 Turning volts into numbers

Worked · specifying a data acquisition channel

A strain gauge bridge outputs ±20 mV full scale. It must be digitised to a resolution of 1 microstrain, where the gauge factor is 2 and full scale is 2000 microstrain. What amplifier gain and how many bits?

  1. The converter's input range is usually ±5 V or 0–10 V. Take ±5 V, so the amplifier must lift 20 mV to 5 V: A = 250
  2. Resolution of 1 in 2000 needs at least 2000 distinguishable levels: n ≥ 11 bits
  3. Nobody ships an 11-bit part, so specify 12 bits, giving 4096 levels and about half a microstrain per step.
  4. Check the noise floor: a 12-bit converter has 74 dB of SNR. The amplifier must be quieter than that, or the extra bits are measuring its noise rather than the strain.

That last step is the one that separates a specification from a wish. Resolution you cannot distinguish from noise is not resolution — it is just more digits.

6.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Voltmeter needs Rm ≫ Rth; ammeter needs Rm ≪ R. Loading error ≈ Rth/Rm.
  • Bridge balance: R1/R2 = R3/R4. One active arm gives Vout ≈ (VS/4)(ΔR/R).
  • LSB = FSR/2ⁿ.
  • SNR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB — about 6 dB per bit.
  • fs > 2 fmax, or aliasing is permanent.
  • Subtract the offset before amplifying, not after.

Instruments are circuit elements. A voltmeter loads what it reads and an ammeter obstructs what it passes, and in both cases the question is a ratio against the Thévenin resistance of the thing being measured — not the raw specification of the instrument.

Getting a physical quantity into a computer means a transducer, a bridge to remove the offset, an amplifier to fill the converter's range and a converter with enough bits to resolve what matters. Each stage has one number, and the chain is only as good as the noisiest link in it.
