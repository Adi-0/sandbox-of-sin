Operational Amplifiers

Part 4 ended on a bargain: trade raw gain for a gain you can predict. An op-amp has so much raw gain that the trade becomes free — and the result is the easiest arithmetic in this module.

5.1 Two rules, and everything follows

An ideal op-amp has infinite gain, infinite input impedance, zero output impedance and infinite bandwidth. Wire negative feedback around it and those assumptions collapse into two statements that solve every circuit in this part:

Why the rules work

They look like cheating. They are not — they are the limit of Part 4's argument. A real op-amp has an open-loop gain around 100 000. To produce a 10 V output it needs an input difference of 0.1 mV. Compared with the volts everywhere else in the circuit, that is zero, and calling it zero introduces an error of about 0.001%. Rule 2 is not an idealisation so much as a rounding.

5.2 Deriving one, to show they are all the same

Worked · the inverting amplifier, from the rules only

Input Vin through Ri to the − input; Rf from the − input to the output; + input grounded.

  1. Rule 2: the + input is at 0 V, so the − input is too. Virtual ground.
  2. The current arriving through Ri is therefore I = Vin / Ri
  3. Rule 1: none of it enters the op-amp, so all of it continues through Rf.
  4. Walking from the virtual ground to the output through Rf: Vout = −(Rf/Ri) Vin

Four lines, and no property of the op-amp appears in the answer. Every other configuration is the same four lines with a different node grounded — which is why this topic is worth far more marks per minute than any other in Electronics.

Trap · the non-inverting +1

With Rf = 4 kΩ and Ri = 1 kΩ, the inverting circuit gives −4 and the non-inverting one gives +5. They differ by exactly one because the non-inverting input arrives at the output as well as being amplified. Both −5 and +4 will be on offer, and only one reading of the circuit tells you which terminal the signal entered.

5.3 Where the ideal model stops

The specification says "ideal, nonideal", and three departures matter:

Check the rails before you answer

An op-amp question that supplies a rail voltage is usually testing this. Compute the ideal output, then compare it with the supply. If the arithmetic says 40 V and the part runs from ±15 V, the answer is the rail — about 14 V, since real outputs do not quite reach the supply. This is the most frequently missed op-amp question on the exam, and it is missed by people whose arithmetic was perfect.

5.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • The two rules: no input current, and the inputs match.
  • Inverting −Rf/Ri; non-inverting 1 + Rf/Ri; follower +1.
  • Summing: −Rf Σ(Vk/Rk). Difference: (Rf/Ri)(V2 − V1).
  • Integrator −(1/RC)∫, differentiator −RC d/dt.
  • GBW = A × BW, a constant.
  • Always check the output against the supply rails.

Two rules — no input current, and the inputs equal — solve every standard op-amp circuit in four lines, and no device parameter survives into the answer. That is the payoff of the trade Part 4 introduced: with enough raw gain, feedback buys total predictability for nothing.

What the ideal model cannot tell you is where it stops. Gain and bandwidth are one fixed budget, slew rate limits large fast signals independently, and the output can never leave the supply rails — which is the limit that decides more exam questions than the other two together.
