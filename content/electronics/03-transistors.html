Transistors

A diode is a valve that opens by itself. A transistor is a valve with a handle — and almost every question about one is really the question "which region is it in?"

3.1 Two families, one job

Both device families do the same thing: a small signal at one terminal controls a much larger current between the other two. They differ in what the control input actually is.

  • Bipolar (BJT) — a base current controls the collector current. Three terminals: base, collector, emitter.
  • Field-effect (FET) — a gate voltage controls the drain current, and the gate draws essentially no current at all. Three terminals: gate, drain, source.

That difference decides where each is used. A FET's gate is an insulator or a reverse-biased junction, so it loads whatever drives it almost not at all — which is why every integrated circuit with millions of devices is built from MOSFETs.

3.2 The BJT in three regions

The three regions, in one sentence each
  • Cutoff — base-emitter below 0.7 V. No base current, no collector current. The switch is open.
  • Active — base-emitter forward, base-collector reverse. IC = βIB and the collector voltage does not matter. This is the amplifier region.
  • Saturation — both junctions forward. The collector current is limited by the external circuit, not by β, and VCE collapses to about 0.2 V. The switch is closed.

3.3 Deciding the region — the method from Part 1

A transistor's region depends on quantities you have not computed yet, which is exactly the circularity Part 1's diode had. The resolution is the same, with three states instead of two:

  1. Assume active. Take VBE = 0.7 V, find IB, and compute IC = βIB.
  2. Find VCE from the collector loop.
  3. Check. If VCE > 0.2 V, the assumption holds. If it came out below 0.2 V — or negative, which is impossible — the transistor is saturated, and you redo it with VCE = 0.2 V and IC = (VCC − 0.2)/RC.
Trap · using β when it does not apply

A saturated transistor still has base current and still has a β on its datasheet, but IC ≠ βIB — the collector current is whatever the supply and the collector resistor allow, and no more. A negative VCE is the tell. If your arithmetic produces one, you have not made an error in the arithmetic; you have made an error in the assumption.

3.4 The FET, and the square law

A FET has three regions too, under different names: cutoff, triode (also called ohmic or linear), and saturation. Note the vocabulary collision — saturation in a FET is the useful amplifying region, the opposite of what the word means for a BJT.

Worked · a MOSFET's gate voltage

An n-channel enhancement MOSFET has a conductivity factor of 4 mA/V² and a threshold of 0.7 V. It is operating in saturation. What gate-to-source voltage produces 23.5 mA of drain current?

  1. Start from the square law and solve for the overdrive: VGS − Vt = √(ID/k)
  2. Substituting, in consistent units of mA and V: VGS − 0.7 = 2.42 V
  3. So VGS = 3.1 V

Do not forget to add the threshold back. The square law is written in terms of the overdrive VGS − Vt, and the answer 2.42 V will be one of the options.

Trap · "saturation" means opposite things

In a BJT, saturation is the fully-on switch state where VCE has collapsed and gain is lost. In a FET, saturation is the flat, high-output-impedance region where the device is a well-behaved current source and amplification happens. A question that says "the FET operates in the saturation region" is telling you the square law applies — it is a hint, not a warning.

3.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • IE = IB + IC, IC = βIB, IE = (β+1)IB, α = β/(β+1).
  • VBE = 0.7 V on, VCE(sat) = 0.2 V.
  • Assume active → compute → check VCE. Negative means saturated.
  • JFET: ID = IDSS(1 − VGS/VP)². MOSFET: ID = k(VGS − Vt)².
  • gm = 2√(k ID), the slope of the square law.
  • Saturation is the good region for a FET and the switch region for a BJT.

A BJT is current-controlled and a FET is voltage-controlled, but both are valves whose behaviour depends on which region they occupy — and the region is never given to you. It has to be assumed, computed and checked, exactly as a diode's state was in Part 1.

Both FET families follow the same square law, so transconductance is simply its derivative and therefore rises with bias current. That single fact is what Part 4 turns into voltage gain.
