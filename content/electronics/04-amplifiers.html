Amplifiers

Part 3 gave a device that turns a small current into a large one. Turning that into a usable amplifier takes two more things: a bias point that stays where you put it, and a gain that does not depend on the transistor.

4.1 Bias first, signal second

An amplifier does two jobs at once, and they are analysed separately:

  • DC analysis sets the quiescent operating point — the Q-point — with the signal set to zero. Capacitors are open circuits.
  • AC analysis finds the gain for small variations about that point. Capacitors are short circuits and DC supplies are grounds.
Why supplies become grounds in AC analysis

A 12 V rail is a constant 12 V. Constant means it does not vary, and AC analysis deals only in variations — so as far as the signal is concerned, the rail and ground are the same node. This is not a trick; it is what "small-signal" means. It is also why RC appears to connect the collector to ground in the AC model.

4.2 The Q-point problem

The obvious way to bias a transistor is a single resistor from the supply to the base — fixed bias. It sets IB directly, so IC = βIB.

And that is exactly what is wrong with it. β is not a design parameter. It is whatever the part happens to have, it varies with temperature, and a single part number is routinely specified anywhere from 50 to 300. A bias scheme that multiplies by β inherits all of that.

How the emitter resistor does it

It is negative feedback, and the loop is short enough to say in one sentence: collector current rises → emitter voltage rises → the base-emitter drive falls → collector current comes back down. The base is held near a fixed 2.4 V by the divider, so anything that lifts the emitter necessarily reduces VBE. The transistor is correcting its own drift, which is why β can wander by a factor of six and the current barely moves.

4.3 Where the gain comes from

For small signals a BJT looks like a resistance re at its emitter, and that resistance is set entirely by the bias current:

Trap · quoting the bypassed gain

A fully bypassed stage's gain of 133 is a real measurement and a useless specification. It is RC/re, and re is 26 mV/IE — which changes with temperature, with supply voltage, and with the resistor tolerances that set the bias. Two boards off the same reel would not have the same gain. Exam questions ask for both forms; be sure which one the stem wants.

4.4 The three configurations

What an emitter follower is for

A stage with a gain of exactly one sounds pointless until you remember Circuit Analysis Part 4. A source with a high Thévenin resistance collapses when you load it. An emitter follower presents (β+1)RE to that source — so it barely loads it — and offers re + Rs/(β+1) to whatever comes next. It buys impedance, not voltage, and that is exactly what the op-amp in Part 5 is doing when it is wired as a follower.

4.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • re = 26 mV / IE.
  • Divider bias: Thévenin the base, then IB = (VTH − 0.7)/(RTH + (β+1)RE).
  • VCE = VCC − IC(RC + RE) — both resistors.
  • Av = −RC/(re + RE′), using only the unbypassed emitter resistance.
  • Common emitter inverts and amplifies; the follower has Av ≈ 1 and exists for impedance.
  • DC and AC analyses are separate. In AC, supplies are grounds.

An amplifier is a bias point with a signal riding on it, and the two are designed separately. The bias must survive a transistor parameter nobody controls, which is why every practical stage uses an emitter resistor and negative feedback rather than trusting β.

The same feedback that stabilises the bias also stabilises the gain, and at the same price. Trading raw gain for a gain that is merely a ratio of two resistors is the central bargain of analogue design — and Part 5 is what happens when a device has so much raw gain that the trade becomes free.
