Rectifiers and Power Conversion

The diode's main job in the world. This part also collects a debt: the arithmetic it needs was already done in Circuit Analysis Part 5, from the definition, and has been waiting ever since.

2.1 The chain, end to end

Almost every mains-powered device contains the same four stages, and by now three of them have already been taught:

  1. Transform — 120 V down to something safe. Power Systems Part 4: a turns ratio of about 9.5 : 1 gives 12.6 V RMS.
  2. Rectify — diodes turn alternating into one-directional. This part.
  3. Filter — a capacitor smooths the lumps into near-DC. This part.
  4. Regulate — a Zener or an IC pins the output exactly. Part 1.

So the only genuinely new content here is stages 2 and 3, and half of stage 2 is already on a plate the reader has seen.

What actually distinguishes the three

The bridge and the centre-tap produce an identical waveform, so no formula can tell them apart. The differences are practical and they are what gets examined:

  • Bridge — four diodes, ordinary transformer, two drops in the path, PIV = Vm.
  • Centre-tap — two diodes, centre-tapped transformer, one drop, PIV = 2Vm.
  • Half-wave — one diode, half the output, and ripple at the line frequency instead of twice it, which makes it the hardest to filter.
Trap · peak inverse voltage

When a diode is off, it has to withstand the reverse voltage across it, and exceeding that rating destroys it. For a centre-tapped rectifier the non-conducting diode sees the full secondary — both halves — so PIV = 2Vm, twice what the bridge's diodes endure. This is the most commonly asked and most commonly missed fact about rectifiers, because the two circuits look so similar on paper.

2.3 The capacitor, and where ripple comes from

A rectified sinusoid is one-directional but it is not DC — it drops to zero twice per cycle. Adding a capacitor across the load changes the mechanism completely: the capacitor charges to the peak, and then it supplies the load while the input falls away.

So ripple is not a filter response to be computed with impedances. It is a discharge, and the arithmetic is charge conservation:

Worked · designing the filter

A bridge rectifier runs from a 12.6 V RMS, 60 Hz secondary and supplies 100 mA. What capacitor keeps the ripple below 1 V peak-to-peak, and what is the resulting DC output?

  1. Peak at the secondary, then subtract the bridge's two drops: Vm = 17.82 V, Vpk = 16.42 V
  2. A bridge is full-wave, so the ripple frequency is 120 Hz — not 60.
  3. Rearrange the ripple formula for C: C = 833 µF so a standard 1000 µF part will do.
  4. At 1000 µF the actual ripple is Vr = 0.833 V and the DC output sits halfway down it: VDC = 16.0 V

Note how far this is from the unfiltered average of 10.45 V. A capacitor does not smooth the average — it raises the output towards the peak, because the load now sees the peak minus a small sag rather than the mean of a lumpy waveform. Students who filter and then apply 2Vm/π get an answer 35% low.

2.4 The rest of power electronics

Rectifiers are 9.E's main content, but the subtopic also names inverters and converters. In outline, and at the depth this exam asks:

  • Inverter — DC to AC, by switching the polarity across the load at the wanted frequency. Output is a square or stepped wave, filtered towards a sine. This is what a solar installation or a motor drive contains.
  • Buck converter — DC to lower DC by switching, with Vout = D·Vin where D is the duty cycle.
  • Boost converter — DC to higher DC, with Vout = Vin/(1 − D).
  • Controlled rectifier — a thyristor (SCR) instead of a diode, fired partway through each half cycle so the average output is adjustable.
Why switching beat the Zener

A Zener regulator or a linear IC drops the excess voltage across a resistance and turns it into heat: taking 16 V down to 5 V at 1 A wastes 11 watts. A buck converter with a 31% duty cycle does the same job by switching, and wastes a fraction of a watt. Efficiency is why every modern supply switches — and switching is why they also emit interference and need filtering.

2.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Half-wave: Vavg = Vm/π, Vrms = Vm/2. Full-wave: 2Vm/π and Vm/√2.
  • PIV: Vm for half-wave and bridge, 2Vm for centre-tap.
  • Drops in the path: 2 for a bridge, 1 for the other two.
  • Vr = IL/(fr C), with fr = 2f for full-wave.
  • With a filter capacitor the output is near the peak, not near 2Vm/π.
  • Buck: DVin. Boost: Vin/(1 − D).

A rectifier's output values were already determined in Circuit Analysis Part 5 by the RMS integral; this part only says which circuit makes which shape, and adds the two practical facts formulas cannot give — how many diode drops sit in the load path, and how much reverse voltage the off diodes must survive.

The smoothing capacitor changes the question entirely. Once it is present the load runs off a discharging capacitor between peaks, so the ripple is set by charge, and the DC output climbs from the rectified average up to nearly the peak.
