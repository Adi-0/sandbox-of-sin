Synthesis and the Mixed Bench

Five subtopics, one procedure. What looked like a catalogue of components was the same question asked six times, of progressively more capable devices.

7.1 The module was one method

A diode was chosen to open this module for one reason: it is the cheapest device that has states. Once a component's behaviour depends on a condition you have not evaluated yet, Ohm's law alone stops working, and the only way through is to assume, solve and check.

  • Part 1 taught it on two states.
  • Part 3 reused it verbatim on three.
  • Part 4 spent an entire part making sure the state you chose stays chosen, against a β nobody controls.
  • Part 5 is what happens when a device has so much surplus gain that feedback pins the state for free — and the assumption disappears entirely.

Four debts collected

7.4 The five mistakes that cost the most

Worth one last look before the bench
  1. Not checking the rails. An op-amp output of 40 V from a ±15 V supply is 14 V and clipped. This decides more marks than any other single check in area 9.
  2. Not checking VCE. A negative answer means saturated, not a slip in the arithmetic.
  3. The non-inverting +1. Same two resistors, −4 one way and +5 the other.
  4. fr = 2f for full-wave ripple, and PIV = 2Vm only for the centre-tap.
  5. Adding the threshold back. The FET square law is written in overdrive, so VGS = √(ID/k) + Vt.

7.5 Reading a stem quickly

Almost every Electronics question announces its method in the first line. The classification worth practising:

  1. Is there a triangle in the figure? Then it is two rules and four lines. Compute, then check the rails.
  2. Does the stem give β and a base resistor? Assume active, find IB, multiply, and check VCE.
  3. Does it name a saturation region and give IDSS or k? That is the FET square law, and the phrase is a hint that it applies.
  4. Are there diodes and a sinusoid? Convert to peak, count the drops, then use the average or RMS factor for the shape.
  5. Is a meter, a bridge or a converter mentioned? Instrumentation — and the answer is a ratio or a power of two.
Where the marks actually are

Area 9 is 7 to 11 questions, and they are not spread evenly. Op-amps and diodes together are usually about half of them, and both are the fastest topics in the module — an op-amp question is often under a minute. Transistor biasing is slower and less frequent. If time is short on exam day, that ordering is worth knowing before you open the section.

7.6 Under exam conditions

Eight questions across all five subtopics, at the real pace of 2 minutes 50 seconds each.

Electronics is one procedure applied to a sequence of devices that each have more states than the last — until the op-amp, where enough surplus gain makes the states irrelevant and two rules replace the whole method. Recognising which stage of that sequence a question belongs to is most of the work.

Very little of the machinery is new. Thévenin, the RMS integral, the exponential and reflected impedance all arrived from earlier modules and do most of the lifting here. What Electronics adds is the habit of checking the answer against a physical limit — a rail, a saturation voltage, a supply — before believing the arithmetic.
