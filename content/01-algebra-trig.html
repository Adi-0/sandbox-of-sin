Algebra and Trigonometry

Two tools. One compresses enormous ranges into readable numbers. The other converts an angle into a ratio. Almost everything else in this guide is built on them.

Engineering algebra is not the algebra of school, where the point was to solve for x. Here the point is that certain shapes of expression recur so often that recognising them is most of the work. This part covers the four shapes the FE exam returns to again and again — the exponential, the quadratic, the progression, and the triangle — and it introduces the object that will follow us through all eight subtopics.

1.1 Why logarithms exist

A pin dropping and a jet engine at takeoff differ in acoustic power by a factor of about 10¹². A copper wire and window glass differ in resistivity by a factor of about 10²². No axis, no table, and no human intuition handles numbers like that side by side.

So engineers stopped writing the numbers and started writing their exponents. A logarithm is the exponent you would have had to write. log₁₀ 1000 = 3 says nothing more than "1000 is 10 raised to the 3."

That single move turns the pin-drop-to-jet-engine ratio from 10¹² into the number 120 — which is exactly why sound is measured in decibels, why Bode plots in Part 12 of the exam have logarithmic axes, and why gain in Electronics is quoted in dB. The whole audible range of human hearing fits in two digits.

The killer number

Amplifier A has a power gain of 1000. Amplifier B has a gain of 100. Chain them and the gain is 100 000 — you multiplied. In decibels they are 30 dB and 20 dB, and the chain is 50 dB — you added. Every logarithm rule below is a restatement of that one trade: multiplication becomes addition.

Plate 2 is that trade, made physical. Drag the slider and watch two lengths add up while two numbers multiply.

Because a logarithm is an exponent, every logarithm rule is an exponent rule read backwards. You do not have two lists to learn. You have one list, seen from two sides.

Worked · a real FE-shaped log question

Evaluate log₃(3/2) + log₃12 − log₃2.

  1. Two of the three rules from Form 1.1 collapse this into one logarithm: addition means multiply, subtraction means divide. log₃ of ((3/2)(12)/2)
  2. The inside is 18/2 = 9. So we want log₃9.
  3. Read it as the question it is: 3 to what power is 9? Two. The answer is 2.00.

Notice you never touched a calculator. That is the design of these questions — they look like arithmetic and they are actually about recognising that three log terms with the same base always want to become one.

1.2 The quadratic, and the number that tells you the answer's shape

A quadratic is any equation of the form ax² + bx + c = 0. You know the formula. What matters on this exam is the piece under the radical.

Also worth carrying, because it is faster than the formula when it applies: the roots of ax²+bx+c satisfy x₁+x₂ = −b/a and x₁x₂ = c/a. If a multiple-choice question offers you four root-pairs, adding them is often quicker than solving.

1.3 Progressions

The FE exam asks about sequences more often than its syllabus suggests, because they are quick to write and quick to mark. There are two, and they differ in one word: an arithmetic progression adds a constant each step; a geometric progression multiplies by one.

Trap · the off-by-one

It is rⁿ⁻¹, not rⁿ. The first term has taken zero steps. Every wrong answer offered on a progression question is built from exactly this slip, so if your result matches a listed option exactly, that is not proof you were right.

Worked · finding a first term backwards

The 2nd and 6th terms of a geometric progression are 3/10 and 243/160. What is the first term?

  1. Going from term 2 to term 6 is four steps, so the ratio is applied four times: a6/a2 = r⁴ = 81/16
  2. r = 3/2, since 3⁴=81 and 2⁴=16. Recognising fourth powers of small integers is worth more on this exam than it sounds.
  3. Step back one from the second term: a1 = 1/5 The first term is 1/5.

1.4 Trigonometry is one picture

Everything in trigonometry is a single diagram: a point walking around a circle of radius 1.

Put the point at angle θ from the positive horizontal axis. Its horizontal position is defined to be cos θ and its vertical position is defined to be sin θ. That is not a consequence of anything — it is the definition, and every identity you will ever use falls out of the picture.

Turn the knob in Plate 3 and watch the two coloured lengths. The horizontal one is cosine. The vertical one is sine. They are the same length measured in two directions, which is why swapping them just shifts the angle by 90°.

Two consequences are free from the drawing, and they are the two you use most:

  • The point is always distance 1 from the centre. By Pythagoras, its coordinates satisfy cos²θ + sin²θ = 1. Memorise this one — it is the most-used identity in engineering and you should never spend a second looking it up.
  • Sine and cosine can never leave [−1, 1], because a coordinate of a point on the unit circle cannot. If an answer claims sin θ = 1.4, it is wrong, and you knew that without checking.

1.5 Meet the cast: the 3-4-5 triangle

The unit circle is honest but inconvenient — its side lengths are irrational almost everywhere. There is one famous escape, and it is the object that will follow us for the rest of this guide.

Scale the circle to radius 5, and the point at coordinates (3, 4) sits exactly on it, because 3² + 4² = 5². That gives a right triangle with whole-number sides and, therefore, clean ratios.

Which angle is which

53.13° and 36.87° both belong to this triangle; they are the two acute angles and they add to 90°. Throughout this guide θ = 53.13° — the angle at the origin when the point sits at (3,4), measured up from the horizontal, so tan θ = 4/3. Keep that fixed and the cast stays consistent all the way to Part 7.

1.6 The identities worth carrying

The handbook lists perhaps forty trigonometric identities. Four of them do almost all the work on this exam.

Check the second one against the cast. With θ = 53.13°, sin 2θ = 2(0.8)(0.6) = 0.96, and 2θ = 106.26°. Sure enough sin 106.26° = 0.96, not 1.2 — which would have been impossible anyway, as we established one section ago.

1.7 Triangles that are not right triangles

Right triangles are solved by ratios. Any other triangle needs one of two laws, and the entire skill is picking the correct one in about three seconds.

Plate 5 makes the choice for you once and then you will not need it again. Press each scenario button and read which law survives.

Trap · degrees and radians

This costs more marks on the FE exam than any other single mistake in mathematics, and it is invisible — you get a plausible number, not an error.

The rule: geometry is in degrees, calculus is in radians. If an angle appears inside a derivative, an integral, a series, or an exponential, it must be in radians, because d/dx sin x = cos x is only true in radians. Convert with π rad = 180°, and check your calculator's mode before the exam starts, not during it.

1.8 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • sin²θ + cos²θ = 1 — and that sine and cosine live in [−1,1].
  • The 3-4-5 triangle: sin θ = 0.8, cos θ = 0.6, tan θ = 4/3, θ = 53.13°.
  • log(xy) = log x + log y and log xⁿ = n log x. You will use these faster than you can search for them.
  • Change of base: log_b x = ln x / ln b.
  • π rad = 180°, and that calculus always means radians.
  • The discriminant's three cases. Not the quadratic formula itself — that one is in the handbook and you have time to look it up.

A logarithm is an exponent, so every log rule is an exponent rule read backwards, and its whole purpose is to turn multiplying into adding.

Trigonometry is one point walking around a circle: cosine is where it is horizontally, sine is where it is vertically, and sin² + cos² = 1 is just Pythagoras applied to that point. The 3-4-5 triangle is the case where all of it is whole numbers, and we are going to keep using it.
