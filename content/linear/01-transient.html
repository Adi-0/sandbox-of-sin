Transient Response

Circuit Analysis stopped at steady state and said so. This is the part it deferred: what a circuit does in the moments after something changes, before it settles into the answer that module taught you to find.

1.1 The debt Circuit Analysis named

Every result in Circuit Analysis assumed the circuit had already settled — that the DC values were constant, or that the sinusoid had been running forever. That assumption is what let a capacitor be an open circuit and an inductor be a short. Take it away and neither is true.

The reason is one property each, and they are mirror images:

Trap · which way the resistor acts

τ = RC and τ = L/R look like a symmetry and are not. A bigger resistor limits the current that charges a capacitor, so the RC circuit gets slower. The same resistor forces an inductor's current to change faster, because it takes a larger voltage to push that current through it — so the RL circuit gets quicker. If you only remember the algebra you will eventually write L·R; if you remember the physical reading you cannot.

1.2 Why the curve is an exponential

Take an RC circuit charging. The current through the resistor is whatever voltage is left over divided by R, and that current is exactly what raises the capacitor's voltage. So the rate of climb is proportional to how far there still is to climb — and a quantity whose rate of change is proportional to what remains is the definition of exponential decay.

That is not a fact about capacitors. It is a fact about the differential equation, which is why all four first-order transients on the exam are the same drawing:

Five numbers worth having on sight

After 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5 time constants a first-order transient is 63.2%, 86.5%, 95.0%, 98.2%, 99.3% of the way there. Each time constant removes the same fraction of what is left — 63.2% of it — which is what "exponential" means and why the sequence is not evenly spaced. Five time constants is conventionally settled, and if a question mentions a duration much longer than that, it is a steady-state problem with no exponentials in it at all.

1.3 The three-step method

Almost no first-order exam question needs a differential equation solved. It needs three numbers, and then one line that is the same line every time:

Worked · a transient with nothing at zero

A 12 V source behind 4 kΩ feeds a 100 µF capacitor with 8 kΩ across it. The capacitor starts at 2 V. Find v(t).

  1. Where it starts. Given: 2 V. And it does not jump, so v(0⁺) = 2 V.
  2. Where it ends. Capacitor open, so it is a divider: v(∞) = 8 V
  3. How fast. Kill the source and look back from the capacitor's terminals: R₁ and R₂ are in parallel. τ = 0.27 s
  4. Substitute into the general form: v(t) = 8 − 6e^(−t/0.27)

Step 3 is where the marks are lost. The obvious wrong answer uses R₁ alone, because that is the resistor the charging current flows through — but the capacitor discharges through R₂ at the same time, and τ is set by what the capacitor sees, not by what charges it. Move R₂ on the plate above and watch the final value and the time constant move together, because R₂ is in both.

1.4 Reading the circuit at t = 0⁺ and t = ∞

Two substitutions do all the work in steps 1 and 2, and they are worth holding as pictures rather than rules:

at t = 0⁺, starting from restat t = ∞, on DC
Capacitora short — holding 0 Van open circuit — no current can flow into a constant voltage
Inductoran open circuit — enforcing 0 Aa short — no voltage across a constant current
"Starting from rest" is doing work in that table

A capacitor already charged to 5 V is not a short at t = 0⁺ — it is a 5 V source, for that instant. An inductor already carrying 2 A is a 2 A source. The short and the open circuit are just the special case where the stored value happens to be zero, and a question that describes a switch moving from one position to another is usually arranging for it not to be. Read the circuit before the switch moves; that is where step 1 actually comes from.

1.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • vC cannot jump; iL cannot jump. Nothing else in the circuit is protected.
  • τ = RC and τ = L/R. kΩ × µF = ms; mH ÷ kΩ = µs.
  • The general form x = x∞ + (x₀ − x∞)e^(−t/τ), and the three steps that fill it in.
  • τ uses Rth — what the element sees, sources killed.
  • 63.2, 86.5, 95.0, 98.2, 99.3 per cent. 5τ is settled.
  • 0.69τ to half, 2.3τ to 90%, 4.6τ to 99%.
  • At t = 0⁺ from rest: C is a short, L is open. At t = ∞ on DC: C is open, L is a short.

A capacitor defends its voltage and an inductor defends its current. That is the only new physics in this part, and everything else follows: the continuity conditions that give step 1, the steady-state substitutions that give step 2, and the differential equation whose solution is the curve.

The curve itself is not a fact about components. A rate of change proportional to what remains produces an exponential, and that describes a cooling cup of coffee and a decaying isotope as readily as a charging capacitor — which is why the same five percentages turn up everywhere. What Part 2 adds is a second store of energy, so that the two can hand energy back and forth instead of quietly running down. That is when the circuit starts to ring, and when Mathematics Part 7's characteristic roots arrive as hardware.
