Transfer Functions and the s-Plane

One ratio holds everything a linear system can do. And once you have it, the behaviour is not in the algebra — it is in the positions of a handful of points on a plane.

4.1 The definition, and why it is worth having

H(s) = Y(s)/X(s), initial conditions zero

The "initial conditions zero" is not a limitation, it is the point: H(s) describes the system, with nothing of the particular situation in it. Whatever you drive it with, the output is one multiplication: Y(s) = H(s)X(s).

Why swapping the components does not move the pole

An RC low-pass and a CR high-pass have exactly the same pole, at −1/RC. The pole comes from the loop, and there is one loop either way. What changes is the numerator: the high-pass has a zero at the origin, which is what kills the response at DC. That division of labour is worth carrying: poles are what the circuit does on its own, zeros are what your particular choice of output does to it. Stability depends only on poles, for exactly this reason.

4.2 The s-plane

Every claim this module has made now becomes a statement about where a point is:

Trap · a pole on the axis is not stable

"Negative real part" means strictly negative. A pole at exactly s = 0 is a pure integrator whose output accumulates without limit; a pair at exactly ±jω is an oscillation that never dies. Both are marginally stable, which on this exam counts as not stable. And a pole with a very small negative real part — −0.1 ± j50, say — is stable, however long it rings. Slow is not unstable.

Memorise the standard second-order form

H(s) = ωn² / (s² + 2ζωn s + ωn²) is worth knowing on sight, because exam questions hand you a denominator and expect ζ and ωn back. Match coefficients: the constant term is ωn², and the s coefficient is 2ζωn. Two lines, no derivation, and it works for any second-order system — mechanical, thermal or electrical — because none of them knows what its own components are.

4.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • ZR = R, ZL = sL, ZC = 1/sC. Then it is Circuit Analysis, unchanged.
  • Order = number of storage elements. Resistors add none.
  • Stable ⟺ every pole strictly in the left half-plane. Zeros never affect it; a pole on the axis is not stable.
  • Real pole at −a: τ = 1/a.
  • Complex pair: distance is ωn, cosine of the angle from the negative real axis is ζ, imaginary part is ωd, real part sets ts.
  • standard second-order form — match coefficients to read off ζ and ωn.
  • Poles come from the loop; zeros come from where you take the output.

A transfer function is the system with the situation taken out of it, and the price of that abstraction is nothing: three impedance substitutions and every technique from Circuit Analysis still applies. What you get back is that a circuit's entire behaviour has collapsed into the positions of a few points.

And those positions are readable. Left is decay, up is ringing, distance is ωn, angle is ζ — so a specification written as "under 5% overshoot, settled in 20 ms" is a region: to the left of a vertical line, and inside a wedge from the origin. That is the picture Control Systems is built on entirely, and Part 5 opens the door to it by asking what happens when s is not any old complex number but specifically jω.
