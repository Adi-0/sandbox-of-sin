Resonance

One frequency where two opposite reactances happen to be equal. Nothing new is introduced — but the consequences are large enough that the spec gives it a subtopic of its own.

6.1 The cancellation

Inductive reactance X_L = ωL rises with frequency; capacitive reactance X_C = 1/ωC falls. Two curves going opposite ways must cross, and because they enter the impedance with opposite signs, at the crossing they cancel exactly.

Trap · series is a minimum, parallel is a maximum

This inversion catches people every time, and it is the same inversion as Part 2's damping ratio. In series the resistor sits in the current path, so it is what limits the current when the reactances get out of the way. In parallel it is a bypass, so getting the reactances out of the way means the source stops supplying current at all. Read the topology before writing anything down — and note that both answers are "R", so the number alone will not save you.

6.2 Q — how sharp, and how large

Why the voltage rise is real and the cancellation is not a trick

At resonance the current is V_s/R, which is large because nothing but R is opposing it. That current flows through an inductor whose reactance is ω₀L — and (V_s/R)(ω₀L) = QV_s. The capacitor carries the same magnitude with the opposite sign, so their sum is zero and the source sees only R. The two voltages cancel; they do not fail to exist. A high-Q circuit routinely destroys its own capacitor from a modest supply, and this is how.

Worked · a tuned circuit, end to end

A series RLC has R = 20 Ω, L = 10 mH, C = 1 µF, driven by 10 V.

  1. Where. ω₀ = 10⁴ rad/s — that is 1.59 kHz.
  2. How sharp. The characteristic impedance is √(L/C) = 100 Ω, so Q = 5.
  3. How wide. BW = 2000 rad/s
  4. What it does to the components. V_L = V_C = 50 V
  5. And in the time domain. ζ = 0.1 — so a step would overshoot by 73% and ring for a long time. Same circuit, same number, other domain.

Five answers from three components, and the last one is the point of the module: Q and ζ are one property. A circuit that selects a narrow band of frequencies is a circuit that rings for a long time, because both are statements about how slowly it loses stored energy.

6.3 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • ω₀ = 1/√(LC). R is not in it.
  • Series: |Z| = R, a minimum. Parallel: |Z| = R, a maximum.
  • Series Q = (1/R)√(L/C); parallel inverts it.
  • BW = ω₀/Q, between the half-power points.
  • V_L = V_C = QV_s at resonance — and the components must survive it.
  • Q = 1/2ζ. Q = 0.5 is critical damping.
  • Below ω₀ capacitive, above ω₀ inductive.

Resonance is the frequency at which a circuit's two opposite reactances are equal. That is all it is — and everything else follows: the impedance collapses to R (or rises to it), the current peaks (or vanishes), and the circuit stops being reactive at exactly one point on the axis.

What makes it worth a subtopic is Q, and the fact that Q means two things at once. How narrow the band is, and how much the voltage inside the circuit exceeds the source — the same number, because both measure how much energy the circuit is storing against how little it is losing. And that is also Part 2's damping ratio upside down, which closes the module: a circuit that rings for a long time and a circuit that is selective in frequency are the same circuit, described from the two ends of the same transform.
