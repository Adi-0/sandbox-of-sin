Frequency Response

Let s be jω and the transfer function stops describing transients and starts describing a filter. The substitution is one line; what it buys is a way of reading a circuit's whole behaviour off a straight-line sketch.

5.1 One substitution

Feed a linear system a sinusoid and — once the transient has died — the output is a sinusoid at the same frequency, changed only in amplitude and phase. That is Circuit Analysis Part 6's phasor argument, and it means the entire steady-state response at frequency ω is one complex number:

H(jω) = H(s) with s = jω

Its magnitude is the gain and its angle is the phase shift. Nothing new has been defined — jω is simply the imaginary axis of the plane Part 4 drew, and asking for the frequency response is asking what happens along that one line.

The Bode plot's shape is one triangle

For a single pole at −a, the gain is a / |jω + a| — which is a over the distance from the pole to the point you are standing at. Below the corner the vertical leg is small, so the distance barely changes and the response is flat. Above it the vertical leg dominates, so the distance is essentially ω and the gain goes as 1/ω. That is what −20 dB per decade is: ten times the frequency, one tenth the gain. And level with the pole the two legs are equal, so the distance is √2·a — gain 1/√2, −3.01 dB, phase exactly −45°.

5.3 The straight-line sketch

The skill worth having under time pressure is not evaluating H(jω) at a frequency — a calculator does that. It is being able to draw the shape in fifteen seconds and read the answer off it.

Worked · sketching from a transfer function

Sketch H(s) = 100 / [(1 + s/10)(1 + s/1000)].

  1. DC gain. Set s = 0: H = 100, which is 40 dB. Start the sketch flat at 40 dB.
  2. Find the corners. Each factor (1 + s/ωc) is a pole at its own ωc: here 10 and 1000 rad/s.
  3. Break at each one. At 10 rad/s the slope goes to −20 dB/decade; at 1000 rad/s it goes to −40.
  4. Read anything you need off the lines. From 10 to 1000 is two decades at −20 dB/decade, so the gain falls 40 dB and the plot crosses 0 dB right at 1000 rad/s.

Four steps and no arithmetic beyond a logarithm. The phase goes with it: starting at 0°, heading for −90° through the first corner and −180° through the second, passing −45° at 10 rad/s and −135° at 1000. A question asking for the gain at 100 rad/s is now a reading, not a calculation — one decade past the first corner, so 40 − 20 = 20 dB.

Trap · the corner is a pole, not a coefficient

A factor written (1 + s/10) has its corner at 10. A factor written (s + 10) has its corner at 10 as well — but its DC value is 10, not 1, so it carries a gain you must account for. Always put each factor into the form (1 + s/ωc) before reading a corner off it, and collect the leftover constants into the DC gain. Skipping that step is how a sketch ends up the right shape at the wrong height.

5.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • s → jω: magnitude is gain, angle is phase.
  • ωc = 1/RC — corner, pole location and 1/τ are one number. Divide by 2π for hertz.
  • 20 log for voltage, 10 log for power. ×2 → 6 dB, ×10 → 20 dB, 1/√2 → −3 dB.
  • Pole: −20 dB/decade and −90°. Zero: +20 and +90°. Slopes add.
  • At a corner: −3.01 dB and −45°, always.
  • Phase swings over about a decade either side of the corner; the magnitude turns at a point.
  • Put every factor into (1 + s/ωc) form before reading corners.

Frequency response is the s-plane restricted to one line. Walk up the imaginary axis and the gain at each point is set by how far you are from the poles — near one, the response is large; far from all of them, it is small. Every feature of a Bode plot is that distance changing, which is why the shape can be sketched from pole positions alone.

What makes it a tool rather than a picture is the logarithm. Multiplying transfer functions becomes adding decibels, and adding slopes becomes drawing straight lines — so a cascade of stages is sketched by superposition and read by eye. Control Systems is built almost entirely on that convenience, and Part 6 finishes this module by looking at what happens when a pole pair sits so close to the imaginary axis that the gain at one frequency dwarfs everything else.
