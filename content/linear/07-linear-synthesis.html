Synthesis and the Mixed Bench

Four subtopics, six parts, one object. A pole pair is a step response, a Bode plot, a resonance curve and a pair of characteristic roots — and none of those is a metaphor for the others.

7.1 One point, four questions

Every panel on that plate is drawn from the same two numbers, so moving ζ moves all four at once. That is not a nice coincidence to notice at the end — it is what the module is:

AskAnd the answer isPart

Where the two domains disagree, and both are right

Set ζ between 0.707 and 1 on the plate. The step response still overshoots — so in the time domain the system plainly rings. But the Bode peak has vanished and the magnitude response is monotonic, so in the frequency domain it does not resonate at all. Neither reading is wrong: overshoot appears below ζ = 1 and a frequency peak appears below ζ = 0.707, and between those two values a system does one and not the other. ζ = 0.707 is exactly the maximally-flat, or Butterworth, condition, which is why filter design keeps landing on it.

7.4 The five mistakes that cost the most

Worth one last look before the bench
  1. τ = RC but L/R. A bigger resistor slows an RC circuit and speeds up an RL one. The physical reading fixes it; the algebra alone does not.
  2. Using R instead of Rth. The time constant uses the resistance the storage element sees, not the one it charges through.
  3. Series and parallel invert. ζ inverts, Q inverts, and resonant impedance goes from a minimum to a maximum. Both answers are "R", so the number will not warn you.
  4. ωn is not ωd. On the s-plane ωn is the pole's distance and ωd its height. Reading the imaginary part as ωn is the commonest slip in Part 4.
  5. 20 log for voltage, 10 log for power. Getting it wrong doubles or halves every decibel answer.

7.5 Reading a stem quickly

  1. A switch, and "at t = 0"? Transient. Count the storage elements: one means the three-step method, two means ζ and ωn.
  2. "Most nearly, after N time constants"? Pure recall — 63.2, 86.5, 95.0, 98.2, 99.3.
  3. Overshoot, settling or peak time given? Work back to ζ first; overshoot identifies it uniquely.
  4. An s in the expression? Transfer function. Factor the denominator — the poles are the answer to almost anything else asked.
  5. Decibels, corners or "per decade"? Frequency response. Put every factor into (1 + s/ωc) form and sketch.
  6. "Resonant", "tuned", "bandwidth" or "Q"? Part 6, and check series or parallel before writing anything.
Where the marks actually are

Linear Systems is 5 to 8 questions, and they cluster. Time constants, the five percentages, ω₀ = 1/√(LC), corner frequencies and the dB anchors are pure recall and appear constantly — that is most of the area's marks for a few hours of work. Laplace questions are usually table lookups rather than derivations, and pole-location questions are geometry. The genuinely slow ones are full partial-fraction inversions, and they are also the rarest; if you meet one late in the exam, flag it.

7.6 A set under exam conditions

Ten questions drawn from all six parts, at the real pace of 2 minutes 50 seconds each.

Six parts, and one object underneath all of them. A circuit with two energy stores has a pair of poles; where that pair sits decides how quickly the circuit settles, whether it overshoots, what it does to each frequency, and how sharply it selects one. Those are not four related facts — they are four questions asked of one point on a plane.

The transform is what makes the point visible. Before it, each of those questions needed its own differential equation; after it, they need a factored denominator and a picture. That is why this module sits where it does in the compilation: Circuit Analysis could describe a circuit only once it had settled, and Linear Systems describes what it does on the way — which is the same thing Control Systems will then try to change on purpose.
