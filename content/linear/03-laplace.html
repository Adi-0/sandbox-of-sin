The Laplace Transform

Parts 1 and 2 solved differential equations by recognising them. That stops working as soon as the input is anything but a step. The transform replaces the recognition with a procedure — and the procedure has no calculus in it.

3.1 What it is for

The definition is one line and you will not use it:

F(s) = ∫₀^∞ f(t)e^(−st) dt

Nobody evaluates that integral on an exam, and the handbook does not expect you to. What matters is the property it buys, which is this: because e^(−st) differentiates to a multiple of itself, integrating by parts turns f′(t) into sF(s). Differentiation becomes multiplication. A differential equation becomes a polynomial one.

Where the initial conditions go

The full statement is ℒ{f′} = sF(s) − f(0⁻), and that trailing term is the reason engineers use this and mathematicians argue about it. The initial condition enters the algebra at the transform step. Solve the ordinary way and you get a general solution with two arbitrary constants, then set up and solve two simultaneous equations to pin them down. In s, the constants were never arbitrary — they walked in with the problem.

3.2 The table

The handbook has a long transform table. Five entries carry nearly every FE question, and they are worth reading as one idea rather than five.

Read the right-hand column, not the left

Every entry above is characterised by where its denominator is zero. A pole on the negative real axis is a decaying exponential. A pair on the imaginary axis is an oscillation that never dies. A pair to the left of the imaginary axis is a decaying oscillation. That is the entire table in three sentences, and it is why Part 4 can throw the table away and just look at a picture.

Apply it to F(s) = 1/[s(s−2)] and it cheerfully returns −0.5. The actual response grows without limit, because of that pole at +2. The theorem is only valid when every pole of sF(s) lies strictly in the left half-plane — so check where the poles are before using it, which takes two seconds and is the difference between a right answer and a confidently wrong one.

Sanity-check it against the theorems without inverting: initial value sF → 0 ✓, final value sF → 3 ✓. And check it against Part 1: this is a rise from 0 to 3 with τ = ¼ s, which is the three-step method's answer. Two routes, one answer — and the transform did not need to know it was a first-order circuit.

3.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • ℒ{f′} = sF(s) − f(0⁻). The reason the whole method exists.
  • e^(−at) ↔ 1/(s+a) — a pole at −a is a decay at rate a.
  • Step is 1/s; a step input always puts a pole at the origin.
  • Sinusoid poles sit at ±jω; damped ones at −a ± jω.
  • Shift: e^(−at)f(t) ↔ F(s+a).
  • Final value lim s→0 sF(s), only if the poles are all in the left half-plane.
  • Partial fractions by the cover-up rule.

The transform is a detour that turns out to be shorter. Three sides of the rectangle — transform, rearrange, invert — and none of them is calculus, against one side that is. The initial conditions come along for free, which is what turns a convenience into a method.

But the real payoff is not the arithmetic. It is that a system's behaviour has become a set of numbers: the values of s where a denominator is zero. A pole on the negative real axis is a decay, a pair on the imaginary axis is an oscillation, a pair to the left is Part 2's ringing. Part 4 stops inverting anything and reads the answer straight off a picture of where those numbers are — and that picture is the single most useful object in this module and the next one.
