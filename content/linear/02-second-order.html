Second-Order Response

One store of energy runs down. Two stores can hand energy back and forth — and a circuit that does that rings. This is where Mathematics Part 7's characteristic roots stop being algebra and become components you could buy.

2.1 Why a second store changes everything

An RC circuit has one place to put energy, so the only thing it can do is fill up or empty. Add an inductor and there are two, with opposite habits: the capacitor wants to hold its voltage and the inductor wants to hold its current. When the capacitor reaches its target the inductor is still pushing current into it, so it overshoots — and then the capacitor pushes back.

Whether that argument settles quietly or rings depends on one thing: how much resistance there is to take energy out of the exchange.

Trap · series and parallel invert ζ

In a series RLC the resistor is in the path of the circulating current, so more resistance means more damping. In a parallel RLC it is a bypass, so more resistance means less damping — an open circuit removes it entirely. The formulas are reciprocals of each other and questions are built on exactly this. Read which topology you have been handed before writing anything down.

Mathematics Part 7 solved y″ + 6y′ + 25y = 0 and got roots −3 ± 4j: ωn = 5, ωd = 4, ζ = cos 53.13° = 0.6. Multiply everything by 2000 and it is R = 120 Ω, L = 10 mH, C = 1 µF — roots at −6000 ± j8000, ωn = 10 krad/s, and the same 3-4-5 triangle. Set the plate above to 120 Ω to see it. The characteristic equation you solved as algebra was this circuit all along, and the ringing on the screen is what those imaginary parts are.

More damping is not more safety

Past ζ = 1, adding resistance makes the response slower and buys nothing — there is no overshoot left to remove. People reliably expect heavily overdamped to be the "safe" choice, and it is simply the sluggish one. Critical damping is a boundary, not a compromise: it is the fastest response that never overshoots, and it exists at exactly one value of R.

2.3 The two numbers a specification is written in

Nobody specifies a circuit by its damping ratio. They specify how far it may overshoot and how quickly it must settle — and both come off the same decaying envelope.

Worked · designing to a specification

A system must overshoot by no more than 5% and settle within 20 ms. What ζ and ωn are needed?

  1. Overshoot fixes ζ, and only ζ. Solving 0.05 = e^(−πζ/√(1−ζ²)) gives ζ = 0.69 — so take the familiar 0.707, which gives 4.3% and leaves margin.
  2. Settling then fixes ωn. ωn ≥ 283 rad/s
  3. Check: at ζ = 0.707 and ωn = 283, the roots are −200 ± j200 and the overshoot is 4.3%. Both specifications met.

The two requirements never fight, because they depend on different things — which is the whole reason second-order systems are described by ζ and ωn rather than by R, L and C. Note also that the answer is an inequality: any faster system also meets it, so the real limit is whatever the hardware can do.

2.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • ωn = 1/√(LC), and R is not in it.
  • Series: ζ = (R/2)√(C/L). Parallel inverts it.
  • Critical resistance R = 2√(L/C) — compare R against it to name the case on sight.
  • Roots −ζωn ± jωd, with ωd = ωn√(1−ζ²).
  • %OS depends on ζ alone: 0.5 → 16.3%, 0.6 → 9.5%, 0.707 → 4.3%.
  • tp = π/ωd, ts ≈ 4/ζωn.
  • ζ = 1 is the fastest with no overshoot; beyond it is slower for nothing.

Two energy stores with opposite habits produce an argument, and the resistance decides whether it is settled quietly or has to be rung out. All of the behaviour is in two numbers — ζ for the shape, ωn for the speed — and the fact that they are separable is what makes design possible: overshoot is a function of ζ alone, so the two specifications a system is written to never compete.

Underneath, this is Mathematics Part 7 with units attached. The roots are the same roots, ζ is the same cosine of the same angle, and the reason a circuit rings at ωd rather than ωn is the same reason e^((−3+4j)t) oscillates at 4 and decays at 3.
