Analytic Geometry

Descartes' one idea: a shape and an equation are the same object. Once you accept that, geometry becomes algebra, and algebra you can do under time pressure.

3.1 Lines, and the two numbers that fix one

A line is fixed by a direction and a point it passes through. The direction is the slope — how much vertical you get per unit of horizontal:

Two relationships between lines come up constantly, and only one of them is worth memorising:

  • Parallel lines have equal slopes. Obvious enough not to need a note.
  • Perpendicular lines have slopes whose product is −1 — each is the negative reciprocal of the other. Know cold. A slope of 4/3 is perpendicular to a slope of −3/4, and those two numbers are about to reappear.

3.2 The circle, and the trick the exam is really testing

A circle is every point at a fixed distance from a centre. Write that sentence with the distance formula and you have the equation, no derivation needed:

Worked · completing the square, the FE way

Find the centre of the circle x² + 4x + y² + 8y = 0.

  1. Take the x terms alone: x² + 4x. Halve the coefficient of x — that gives 2 — and you know the square is (x+2)², which is x² + 4x + 4. You added 4, so subtract it back.
  2. Same for y: half of 8 is 4, so (y+4)² = y² + 8y + 16. Subtract 16.
  3. Reassemble: (x+2)² + (y+4)² = 20 Centre (−2, −4), radius √20 ≈ 4.47.

The shortcut, once you trust it: the centre is always (−D/2, −E/2). Halve each linear coefficient and flip the sign. Ten seconds, no algebra — but do the long version at least once so you know why it works.

3.3 The cast, as a circle

Take r = 5 centred at the origin: x² + y² = 25. The point (3, 4) lies on it, which we already knew from Part 1 — the radius to that point is the hypotenuse of the 3-4-5 triangle.

Now the useful part. The radius to (3,4) has slope 4/3. A tangent line touches the circle without crossing it, and a tangent is always perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact. So the tangent's slope must be −3/4, by the negative-reciprocal rule two sections back — and now you have the tangent line with no calculus at all:

y − 4 = −(3/4)(x − 3)

Drag the point around Plate 9 and watch the two slopes multiply to −1 at every position.

Where this is going

In Part 6 we will differentiate x² + y² = 25 implicitly and get dy/dx = −x/y, which at (3,4) is −3/4. The same number, by a completely different route. When two methods that share no machinery agree, you have understood something rather than remembered it.

3.4 The conic sections are one family

Circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas look like four separate topics with four separate formula sets. They are one shape with one dial.

Fix a point (the focus) and a line (the directrix). Now trace every point whose distance to the focus is e times its distance to the directrix. That single number e — the eccentricity — decides which conic you get.

Trap · reading the equation at a glance

Before computing anything, look at the squared terms. Both squared and added, equal coefficients → circle. Both squared and added, unequal coefficients → ellipse. Both squared but subtracted → hyperbola. Only one squared → parabola. That classification takes two seconds and eliminates two of the four options on most questions in this topic.

3.5 Polar and parametric, briefly

Two alternative ways to name a point, both of which you have already met.

Polar coordinates give a point as (r, θ) instead of (x, y) — which is precisely the polar form of a complex number from Part 2, with the same conversions: x = r cos θ, y = r sin θ, r² = x² + y². Nothing new to learn.

Parametric equations give x and y each as a function of a third variable, usually time. To find the path, eliminate the parameter. The FE exam asks this in almost exactly one form:

Worked · eliminating the parameter

A particle has x = 8 sin t and y = 6 cos t. What path does it trace?

  1. Isolate the trig functions: sin t = x/8 and cos t = y/6.
  2. The Pythagorean identity from Part 1 is what kills the parameter, because it is true for every t: (x/8)² + (y/6)² = 1
  3. That is an ellipse with semi-axes 8 and 6. Multiply through by 2304 if the answer options are in general form: 36x² + 64y² = 2304.

Whenever a parametric problem contains a sine and a cosine of the same argument, the Pythagorean identity is the elimination. That pattern covers nearly every version of this question you will see.

3.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Slope is Δy/Δx, and perpendicular slopes multiply to −1.
  • Point-slope form. It is faster to write than to look up.
  • A circle's centre from general form: (−D/2, −E/2).
  • The four-way classification: added and equal → circle, added and unequal → ellipse, subtracted → hyperbola, one square → parabola.
  • A tangent is perpendicular to the radius at the point of contact.
  • Sine and cosine of the same argument in a parametric pair means the Pythagorean identity eliminates the parameter.

Every shape in this part is the distance formula in disguise, which is Pythagoras, which is the triangle from Part 1.

The four conic sections are one family separated by one number. And the tangent to a circle is perpendicular to its radius — a fact that gives you tangent lines with no calculus, and that Part 6 will confirm with calculus.
