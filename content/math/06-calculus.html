Calculus

Two questions, and everything else is technique. How fast is this changing right now? And how much has piled up so far? The astonishing part is that those two questions turn out to be inverses of each other.

6.1 The derivative is a slope you cannot measure directly

Slope is easy when you have two points: rise over run, as in Part 3. But "how fast is this changing right now" asks for the slope at a single point, and one point has no run.

The way out is to measure the slope between your point and a nearby one, then move the nearby one closer and watch what the answer settles down to. That settling-down value is the limit, and the limit of the slope is the derivative.

Plate 17 does exactly that, on a curve you already know: the top half of the circle x² + y² = 25 from Part 3. Slide h towards zero and watch the number the slope settles on.

Third time, same number

It settles on −0.750. In Part 3 we got that tangent slope from the negative-reciprocal rule. In Part 4 the gradient gave the same perpendicular direction. Now a limit of secant slopes produces it again, with no geometry involved at all. Three routes sharing no machinery, arriving at −3/4. That is what it feels like when a thing is true rather than merely remembered.

6.2 The rules that replace the definition

Four rules cover essentially every derivative on this exam. Two of them you should know without thinking; the other two are in the handbook and worth recognising rather than reciting.

And the short list of specific derivatives worth having in memory:

Worked · implicit differentiation, and the cast one more time

Find dy/dx for x² + y² = 25 at the point (3, 4).

  1. You could solve for y first, but you do not have to. Differentiate both sides with respect to x, treating y as a function of x — which means the y² term needs the chain rule: 2x + 2y dy/dx = 0 The dy/dx is the derivative of the inside. Leaving it out is the error this technique exists to catch.
  2. Solve: dy/dx = −x/y
  3. At (3, 4): −3/4 = −0.75. The tangent slope from Part 3, derived from scratch.

Notice what dy/dx = −x/y says in general: the tangent slope is the negative reciprocal of y/x, which is the slope of the radius. Calculus rediscovered the perpendicularity rule without being told it.

6.3 What derivatives are for: maxima, minima, and rates

At a peak or a valley of a smooth curve, the tangent is horizontal. So the recipe for optimisation is short: set the derivative to zero and solve. Those solutions are the critical points.

To tell a peak from a valley, look at the second derivative — the rate at which the slope itself is changing.

6.4 The integral is an accumulation

Now the second question. Given a rate, how much has accumulated? If a tap runs at a varying rate, how much water is in the bucket?

Slice the time into thin strips, multiply rate by width for each strip to get a small amount, and add them all up. Make the strips infinitely thin and the sum becomes the definite integral — which is why the integral sign is a stretched letter S, for sum.

Geometrically: the definite integral is the area under the curve.

The killer comparison

Adding up ten thousand rectangles to get an area is an afternoon of arithmetic and still only an approximation. The Fundamental Theorem replaces the whole procedure with: find any function whose derivative is your curve, evaluate it at the two ends, subtract. Two evaluations instead of ten thousand rectangles, and the answer is exact.

Plate 18 shows the two halves of that claim agreeing. Drag the upper limit and watch the shaded area grow while a second curve records the running total.

Trap · area between two curves

To find the area between two curves, integrate upper minus lower between their intersection points. Two things go wrong: people forget to find the intersections first, and people subtract in the wrong order and report a negative area. If your area comes out negative, you subtracted backwards — the magnitude is still right.

Worked · area between two curves

Find the area bounded by y = 8 − x² and y = −2 + x².

  1. Find where they meet — you cannot integrate before you know the limits: x = ±√5
  2. Between those points the first curve is on top. Integrate the difference: ∫(10 − 2x²)dx
  3. Antidifferentiate and evaluate. The integrand is even and the limits are symmetric, so integrate from 0 and double — half the arithmetic and half the chances of a sign error: ≈ 29.8 About 30.

6.5 More than one variable

A function can depend on several things at once — temperature depends on position in all three directions. A partial derivative asks how the function changes with one variable while every other variable is held still. It is written ∂f/∂x, and computing one is easier than it sounds: treat the other variables as constants and differentiate normally.

You have already used these. The gradient in Part 4 is nothing but the three partial derivatives collected into a vector — which is why a scalar field goes in and a vector comes out.

Trap · the three that cost the most marks

1. The chain rule's inner derivative. d/dx sin3x = 3cos3x, not cos3x. Every missing factor of 3 is this.

2. Degrees. d/dx sinx = cosx is only true in radians. Calculus never uses degrees.

3. Endpoints. A question asking for the maximum on an interval wants you to check the ends as well as the critical points. The answer is frequently at an end.

6.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • The power rule, forwards and backwards — differentiating and integrating xⁿ.
  • The chain rule, and the habit of asking "is there a function inside another function?" before differentiating anything.
  • d/dx sin x = cos x, d/dx cos x = −sin x, d/dx eˣ = eˣ, d/dx ln x = 1/x.
  • f′=0 locates critical points; f″ tells you which kind; endpoints must be checked separately.
  • A definite integral is an area, and is computed as F(b) − F(a).
  • Area between curves is upper minus lower, between the intersections.
  • A partial derivative holds the other variables still. Nothing more.

A derivative is the limiting slope of a shrinking secant — the answer to "how fast, right now." An integral is the limiting sum of shrinking strips — the answer to "how much, in total." The Fundamental Theorem says they undo each other, which converts an infinite sum into two evaluations and a subtraction.

Set the derivative to zero to find peaks and valleys, use the second derivative to tell them apart, and check the endpoints because the exam is counting on you not to.
