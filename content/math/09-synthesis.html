Synthesis, and the Mixed Bench

Eight subtopics, twenty-four plates, and a suspiciously small number of actual ideas. Here is the count.

9.1 How few things were really doing the work

Read back over the eight parts and the same handful of moves keeps reappearing in different costumes.

Pythagoras. It is the distance formula in Part 3, the magnitude of a vector in Part 4, the magnitude of a complex number in Part 2, and sin² + cos² = 1 in Part 1. Four topics, one theorem.

Perpendicularity. A tangent is perpendicular to its radius (Part 3). The gradient is perpendicular to the level curve (Part 4). Two vectors are perpendicular when their dot product is zero (Part 4). Perpendicular slopes multiply to −1 (Part 3). Implicit differentiation reproduces the first of these without being told it (Part 6). These are one fact seen five ways, and it is why the same −3/4 kept turning up.

The quadratic discriminant. Introduced in Part 1 as a curiosity about root types, it returns in Part 7 to decide whether a physical system rings, crawls, or sits exactly on the boundary. The sign of b² − 4ac is a design parameter, not a piece of algebra trivia.

Rotation as multiplication. Complex multiplication adds angles (Part 2). A matrix can rotate the plane (Part 5). A phasor is a rotation running at constant speed (Part 2), and the characteristic roots of a second-order system are where that rotation meets that decay (Part 7).

Linearity. Superposition is why matrices work, why the general solution of a differential equation is a sum of pieces, and why any of this reduces to arithmetic in the first place. Nothing in this guide would survive a genuinely nonlinear problem — which is exactly what the FE exam avoids.

9.4 On exam day

  • Classify before you compute. Fifteen seconds spent naming the tool saves two minutes of solving the wrong problem. That is the entire purpose of the Handbook Reflex drill.
  • Sketch it. Quadrants, signs, and rough magnitudes are free from a five-second drawing, and they catch the errors arithmetic will not.
  • Use the answers. They are four data points. Eliminate on units, on sign, on order of magnitude, on "sine cannot exceed 1." Sometimes substituting the options is faster than solving.
  • Move on. Every question is worth the same. A question you skip and return to costs you nothing; a question you fight for six minutes costs you two other questions.
  • Trust the handbook for formulas and your memory for recognition. That is the trade this whole guide was built around.

9.5 Where to go next

The mathematics is done, and almost all of it is about to be used somewhere else on the exam.

  • Circuit Analysis (11–17 questions) is Part 2 doing real work. Every impedance is a complex number and every AC solution is one division in polar form.
  • Linear Systems and Control Systems (11–17 between them) are Part 7. Transfer functions are characteristic equations, and a Bode plot is Part 1's logarithm plotted against Part 2's phase.
  • Signal Processing and Communications (10–16) are Part 2 and Part 6: Fourier analysis is the claim that any signal is a sum of the rotating arrows from Plate 8.
  • Digital Systems (8–12) is Part 8. Boolean algebra is the propositional logic in Form 8.3, and Karnaugh maps are De Morgan applied systematically.
  • Probability and Statistics (4–6) leans on Part 8's counting and Part 6's integrals — a continuous distribution is an area under a curve.

Each of those is its own module in this compilation. They reuse the same plates, the same stamps, and the same rule: memorise what the handbook will not give you, and practise recognising everything else.

Five ideas — Pythagoras, perpendicularity, the discriminant, rotation as multiplication, and linearity — carried all eight subtopics. The rest is notation, and the notation is in the handbook.

You do not need to be fast at mathematics to pass this exam. You need to be fast at recognising mathematics, and then careful for two minutes.

The mixed bench

Ten problems drawn from all eight subtopics in no announced order — which is the one thing the per-part benches could not simulate. Classification is now part of the work, exactly as on exam day.
