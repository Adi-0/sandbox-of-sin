Complex Numbers

Not "imaginary." A complex number is a number that carries two facts at once — how big, and how turned. Electrical engineering needs exactly that, which is why this topic is not optional here.

2.1 The problem complex numbers were hired to solve

In a DC circuit, Ohm's law is arithmetic: divide volts by ohms, get amps. In an AC circuit, the voltage and the current are both sinusoids, and the current generally lags or leads the voltage. So a full answer needs two numbers: an amplitude and a phase shift.

You can do that with trigonometry. Here is what it costs. To add two sinusoids of the same frequency you expand both with the angle-sum identity, collect the sine terms, collect the cosine terms, then convert the pair back into a single amplitude and phase. Half a page, three chances to drop a sign, once per element in the circuit.

The killer number

A series circuit with resistance R = 3 Ω and inductive reactance X_L = 4 Ω, driven at 100 V. The trigonometric route is that half-page. The complex route is one division:

I = 100∠0° / 5∠53.13° = 20∠−53.13°

Look at the impedance in that denominator: 3 + 4j, magnitude 5, angle 53.13°. That is the triangle from Part 1, unchanged, now doing a job. This is not a coincidence dressed up for the guide — the impedance triangle really is the 3-4-5 triangle, and that is why every FE textbook uses these numbers.

2.2 What the object is

Define one new symbol: j, the number whose square is −1. Electrical engineering writes j rather than i because i was already taken by current, and the exam follows that convention throughout.

A complex number is then anything of the form z = a + bj. Plot a horizontally and b vertically and you have a point on the complex plane — which means every complex number is also an arrow from the origin, with a length and a direction.

Trap · the quadrant

Your calculator's arctan only ever returns an angle between −90° and +90°. For z = −3 − 4j it will happily say 53.13°, which is the wrong quadrant by 180°. Always sketch the point first. Two seconds of drawing beats a plausible wrong answer, and this exact trap turns up in Part 7 when we take roots.

2.3 Arithmetic: use the form that makes it easy

This is the whole practical content of the topic. Addition is easy in rectangular form and horrible in polar. Multiplication is easy in polar and tedious in rectangular. So convert, operate, convert back.

Turn the knob in Plate 7. It multiplies the cast by a number of length 1 at whatever angle you choose. Watch the arrow swing without ever changing length — because multiplying by something of length 1 is a rotation.

Worked · squaring the cast

Find (3 + 4j)² both ways, and check they agree.

  1. Rectangular. Multiply it out and remember j² = −1: = −7 + 24j
  2. Polar. 3+4j = 5∠53.13°. Squaring squares the length and doubles the angle: = 25∠106.26°
  3. Check. 25 cos106.26° = −7 and 25 sin106.26° = 24. Agreed. And note √(49+576) = 25 — the cast keeps producing whole numbers even when squared.

2.4 The conjugate, and how to divide

The conjugate of z = a + bj is z̄ = a − bj: the same point reflected across the horizontal axis. It exists for one reason, and it is a good one.

On the cast: 1/(3+4j) = (3−4j)/25 = 0.12 − 0.16j. Its magnitude is 0.2 and its angle is −53.13°, which is exactly what the polar division rule predicted. Two routes, one answer — that is worth confirming once, so you trust the shortcut afterwards.

2.5 Powers and roots

Once you accept "lengths multiply, angles add," powers are free. Multiplying z by itself n times raises the length to the nth and multiplies the angle by n.

The cleanest example: the cube roots of 1. In polar form 1 = 1∠0°, so the roots have length 1 and angles 0°, 120°, 240° — three points evenly spaced round the unit circle. One of them is the boring answer 1. The other two are complex, and they are the reason a cubic can have one real root and still have three roots in total.

2.6 Euler's formula, and why any of this is about circuits

Everything so far has been geometry. Here is the bridge to engineering.

Now let θ grow steadily with time — θ = ωt — and e^{jωt} is an arrow spinning at constant speed. A phasor is that spinning arrow, and the voltage you would actually measure is its shadow: the vertical projection, traced against time.

That is the whole idea, and Plate 8 is the whole idea drawn. Watch the arrow spin on the left and the wave it writes on the right.

The payoff is this: because every phasor in a circuit spins at the same frequency, you can stop drawing the spinning and just record where each arrow started. What is left is a static complex number — and Ohm's law works on those directly. That single simplification is why AC circuit analysis on this exam is arithmetic rather than calculus.

Honest simplification

Phasors work only when every source in the circuit is a sinusoid at the same frequency and the circuit is in steady state. Switch something on and the transient that follows is not a phasor problem — it is a differential equation, which is exactly where Part 7 picks the story up.

2.7 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • j² = −1, and that j means what i means everywhere else.
  • Add in rectangular. Multiply and divide in polar. Convert freely.
  • Lengths multiply, angles add. If you remember one sentence from this part, this is it.
  • e^{jθ} = cos θ + j sin θ.
  • Divide by multiplying top and bottom by the conjugate.
  • The cast: 3 + 4j = 5∠53.13°, and (3+4j)² = −7 + 24j.
  • Check the quadrant before you trust an arctangent.

A complex number is a length and an angle wearing a coordinate disguise. Adding uses the coordinates; multiplying uses the length and angle, because multiplying multiplies lengths and adds angles.

A phasor is a complex number spinning at the circuit's frequency. Since everything spins together, you can throw the spinning away and keep the starting arrow — and that is why AC circuits reduce to Ohm's law.
