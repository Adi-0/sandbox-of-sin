A neuron has exactly one way to say anything: emit a spike. The spikes are all identical, so it cannot vary the message. It cannot make one louder. It cannot attach a number to it.

All it can vary is when.

This looks like a crippling restriction and turns out to be a generous one, because "when" is a continuous quantity that costs nothing to transmit. The question this part answers is: given a stream of identical events, what can a receiver actually extract — and how fast?

Two ways to read a spike train

There are two answers, and the difference between them decides what kind of machine you end up building.

The first is rate coding: the message is the number of spikes in a window. Fast firing means a big number, slow firing means a small one. It is robust — a lost spike barely changes a count — and it is the assumption underneath most of what people call a spiking neural network.

The second is latency coding: the message is how soon the first spike arrives. Strong stimulus, early spike. Weak stimulus, late spike. Nothing else needs to be transmitted at all.

Reader A, the counter, has to wait for the shaded window to close before it can report anything. That window is 100 ms — chosen because that is roughly the shortest window in which a rate estimate is worth having. Reader B, the stopwatch, is finished as soon as the first spike lands, which at 0.4 nA is 13.9 ms in.

7 × sooner, at the default setting, and the gap widens as the stimulus gets stronger — exactly the situation where being quick matters. Drag the slider right and watch the latency readout collapse toward 5 ms while the counter's 100 ms never budges.

This is not an academic distinction. It is why you flinch. The pathway from your retina to a startle response does not have time to average anything; the first spikes to arrive are the message. Rate coding is what you use when you have time. Latency coding is what you use when you do not.

Real cortex almost certainly uses both, mixed, along with codes based on which neurons fire together and on spikes locked to ongoing rhythms. The strict either-or above is a teaching simplification. What is not a simplification is the timing arithmetic: a rate estimate genuinely needs a window, and a first-spike latency genuinely does not.

The consequence: a neuron is a coincidence detector

Now put the leak from Part 2 together with timing, and something falls out that neither idea has on its own.

Give a neuron two inputs, each worth 12 mV. Threshold is 20 mV. Neither input can fire the cell alone — 12 is not 20. Together they easily could: 24 clears it. But "together" has to mean something precise, and the leak is what defines it. The first input's 12 mV starts draining the instant it lands. By the time the second arrives, the first is worth 12·e−Δt/20ms, and the sum either clears threshold or it does not.

Drag Figure 7 and find the edge.

The edge sits at 8.1 ms. Inside it, the neuron fires. Outside it, nothing happens at all — not a weaker output, nothing, because spikes are all-or-none. You have built a logic gate whose truth table depends on time, using one neuron and no clock:

Δtwindow = τ · ln( w / (Vth − w) ) = 20 ms · ln(12/8) = 8.1 ms
Why it looks like this. You need the first input to still be worth at least Vth − w = 8 mV when the second lands. It started at w = 12 mV and decays exponentially, so you are asking how long 12 takes to fall to 8 — a ratio of 1.5, and the logarithm of a ratio is what an exponential decay charges you in time. The only free parameter is τ. The leak is the clock.

This is the load-bearing idea of the whole guide. A leaky neuron with a high threshold is a device that asks one question — did enough of my inputs arrive close together in time? — and answers it with a single event, using no clock, no comparator, no arithmetic, and no energy when the answer is no.

You want to build a detector with a wider tolerance — one that says yes even when the two inputs are 30 ms apart. Which change gets you there?
Increase τ. The window is proportional to τ, so a neuron that forgets more slowly tolerates a bigger gap. Raising the threshold or shrinking the weights does the opposite — it demands the two inputs arrive even closer together, and past a point makes the detector impossible to trigger at all. In a neuromorphic chip, τ is the knob you tune to set the timescale a circuit cares about, and it is why programmable time constants are a headline feature on every chip in Part 6.

Why this is worth building hardware for

Count the work in that detector. Two events arrived. One event left. In between, a capacitor discharged, which is something a capacitor does for free whether you ask it to or not.

To do the same job on a conventional processor you would sample both inputs on a clock, timestamp them, subtract the timestamps, compare against a constant, and branch — several instructions, each fetching operands, running at least a thousand times a second whether or not anything arrived. The neuron does it with a leak.

That is the trade being made across this entire field: replace explicit computation with physics that happens anyway. A leak is not simulated in a neuromorphic chip. It is a real current draining a real capacitor, and it costs nothing because it is what capacitors do.

Spikes are identical, so all information lives in their timing. Counting them is robust but slow; timing the first one is fast and nearly free.

A leaky neuron with a high threshold is a coincidence detector whose tolerance window is set by τ — it fires only when enough input arrives within about one time constant, and does no work at all otherwise.
