Spikes

How to compute with events instead of numbers — and why a fly does it better than a data centre.

Start at Part 1 Skip to the bench

A fruit fly has roughly 140,000 neurons. With them it holds altitude in a crosswind, tracks a moving target, learns which smells preceded a shock, and gets out of the way of your hand before you have finished deciding to move it. It does all of this on a power budget too small to measure comfortably, using a design principle no computer you own follows: it only does work when something happens.

This guide is about the machines being built on that principle. You will not be asked to take anything on faith. Every claim here is attached to a figure you can turn a knob on, and every figure is running the same simulated neuron underneath — the same twenty milliseconds, the same twenty millivolts — so that by Part 5 the numbers feel like old friends rather than fresh notation.

What you need to know already

No background is assumed beyond: arithmetic, the idea that electricity flows through wires, and the willingness to drag a slider. No calculus, no neuroscience, no circuit theory, no machine learning. Three exponential curves appear; each is explained in a sentence when it shows up. If you have never heard the word "synapse" outside a documentary, you are exactly the reader this was written for.

The whole story in one paragraph

Part 1 shows you the wall every conventional computer is standing against: the energy cost of moving numbers, and the cost of checking on a world where nothing is happening. Part 2 introduces the one object that gets around it — a leaky bucket that tips over when it fills — and turns it into a neuron with four numbers attached. Part 3 asks what such a thing can possibly say, and finds the answer is timing. Part 4 connects two of them, then three, and gets a decision out of a circuit with no comparator in it. Part 5 lets the connections change themselves using nothing but the order events arrived in, and watches a synapse discover a pattern nobody labelled. Part 6 puts all of that into silicon, where refusing to move numbers turns out to be the entire trick. Part 7 is the scorecard — what actually ships, and what is still an open problem in 2026. Part 8 hands you the bench and gets out of the way.

Read them in order. Each part uses the machinery the last one built, and Part 5 will be incomprehensible without Part 3.

Every figure is a live simulation, not a recording. Progress is stored in your browser only. Colour is used as vocabulary throughout: amber is voltage, magenta is a spike, teal is excitation, indigo is inhibition. Those four meanings never change.
