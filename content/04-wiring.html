One neuron is a detector. It answers a single yes-or-no question about its inputs. To get a computer you need to connect them, and the connection is where all the interesting freedom lives.

A synapse is the junction where one neuron's output becomes another's input. It has exactly one important property, and Part 2 already told you why: since every spike is identical, the only thing a connection can decide is how much that spike counts for. That number is the synaptic weight, written w, and in this guide it is measured in millivolts — the size of the bump it puts on the receiving neuron's level.

In the bucket picture, the weight is the width of the pipe.

Figure 8 shows three pipes and one drain, with the voltage bump each one produces. A positive weight is an excitatory postsynaptic potential — an EPSP, teal, a bump upward. A negative weight is an inhibitory postsynaptic potential — an IPSP, indigo, a dip downward. Both decay with the same 20 ms time constant, because both are just changes to the level in the same leaky bucket.

Note the shape. The bump appears instantly and then bleeds away over about 20 ms. That decay is not a property of the synapse — it is the neuron forgetting, exactly as in Figure 4. There is only one leak in this system, and everything inherits it.

The cliff

Now connect two neurons and turn the weight up from nothing. You would expect the output to grow smoothly with w. It does not.

The sending neuron in Figure 9 fires at a constant 100 Hz throughout — the input never changes. Below w ≈ 7.9 mV, the receiving neuron does not fire at all. Not weakly. At all. Above it, the output rate climbs almost vertically and then flattens.

The arithmetic is the same as the coincidence window, run in reverse. Bumps arriving every 10 ms into a bucket with a 20 ms leak settle at a level of w ÷ (1 − e−10/20) = 2.54·w. Set that equal to the 20 mV threshold and you get w = 7.87 mV. Below that number the pile-up plateaus under the mark and stays there for eternity.

0.4 % At 7.87 mV this link is dead forever. At 7.90 mV it works. A 0.4 % change in one weight is the difference between a channel that carries the message and one that swallows it — while the same change anywhere else on the slider is barely visible.

This nonlinearity is the reason a spiking network is not just a matrix multiply in a funny hat. A weight near the cliff is a decision; a weight far from it is a formality. Training such a network means finding cliffs, and Part 7 explains why that is genuinely difficult.

Inhibition earns its keep

Excitation alone gives you detectors and relays. To get a decision — a circuit that picks one option and suppresses the rest — you need inhibition, and you need it wired in a particular way.

Take three neurons receiving almost the same input: 0.24, 0.23 and 0.22 nA. A spread of ±4 %, and deliberately close to the rheobase, where small differences still matter. They fire at roughly 40, 34 and 29 Hz — three similar numbers that a downstream reader would have to work hard to tell apart.

Now wire each one to inhibit the other two, and — this part matters — let each one also excite itself. The mutual inhibition is called lateral inhibition; the self-connection is recurrent excitation. Figure 10 runs the pair of them live. Start with the slider at zero, press play, then push the inhibition up.

Watch B and C get strangled. A fires slightly more often, so it inhibits the others slightly more often, so they fire less, so they inhibit A less, so A fires more still — and its own self-excitation banks the gain. That last part is not decoration. Take the self-connections away and the three neurons simply throttle each other down in lockstep, all landing at the same suppressed rate: inhibition alone produces a fair fight, not a winner. You need positive feedback on the leader for the lead to run away.

4 % → 20 × A ±4 % difference at the input becomes around 38 Hz for the winner against a couple of spikes a second for the losers. There is no comparator in this circuit. There is no clock. There are three neurons and nine wires.

The name for the result is a winner-take-all circuit, and it is one of the most reused motifs in both cortex and neuromorphic hardware. It does classification: whichever input is strongest, its neuron is the only one still speaking. It does normalisation: total activity stays roughly constant however many inputs are active. And it does it in whatever time the neurons take to settle — a few tens of milliseconds here — rather than in a fixed number of clock cycles.

In Figure 10, push the inhibition slider all the way up and leave it. What happens if neuron A's input is briefly interrupted?
B takes over — and with strong enough inhibition it can hold the win even once A recovers, because A now has to climb back out from under B's suppression. Strong lateral inhibition gives the circuit hysteresis: it does not just choose, it commits. That is a feature when you want a stable decision and a bug when you want a responsive one, and the inhibition strength is the dial between the two. You can try exactly this on the bench in Part 8.

What you can build with these three pieces

Everything in the rest of this guide is assembled from three motifs you have now seen working:

Relay

One neuron drives another above the cliff. Signals travel. Figure 9.

Coincidence

Several weak inputs converge; only simultaneity fires the cell. Figure 7.

Competition

Mutual inhibition turns a graded comparison into a single winner. Figure 10.

Two of those three do their work with silence — the coincidence detector says nothing unless its condition is met, and the losers of a competition say nothing at all. On a clocked machine, silence costs exactly as much as activity. On an event-driven one, silence is free. That is the connection between the circuits in this part and the energy argument in Part 1, and it is why the two belong in the same guide.

What we do not yet have is any way for the weights to get set. So far you have been turning them by hand. Part 5 takes the slider away from you and gives it to the network.

A synapse's weight is how many millivolts one arriving spike is worth. Excitation adds, inhibition subtracts, and both decay with the receiving neuron's 20 ms leak.

Weights do not act smoothly: below a critical weight nothing gets through at all. And mutual inhibition converts a small difference between inputs into an all-or-nothing decision, with no comparator anywhere in the circuit.
