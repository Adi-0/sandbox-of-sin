Vectors and Vector Analysis

Some quantities need a direction to be meaningful. This part gives you arithmetic that respects direction — and then two products that are worth more than the notation makes them look.

4.1 Why a second kind of number

Temperature is a number. Mass is a number. But a force is not: pushing north with 10 N and pushing east with 10 N do not add to 20 N of anything. A vector is a quantity that carries a magnitude and a direction, and it needs its own arithmetic because the ordinary kind gives wrong answers.

We write vectors in components along the three axis directions, using the unit vectors i, j, k — each of length 1, pointing along x, y and z.

4.2 The cast in three dimensions

Take w = 3i + 4j + 12k. Its magnitude is √169 = 13 — a whole number again, because 3-4-5 and 5-12-13 nest inside each other. Plate 11 shows why, and it is worth seeing once: the magnitude is two applications of Pythagoras, not one exotic three-dimensional formula.

4.3 The dot product: how much do these agree?

Two vectors can point the same way, opposite ways, or somewhere in between. The dot product measures that, and returns an ordinary number — no direction attached.

The killer number

You push a crate 10 m with a force of 100 N, but your arms are at 60° to the floor. Work is F·d, so W = 500 J — not 1000 J. Half your effort went into pressing the crate into the floor, which moves nothing. The cosine is not a correction factor bolted on; it is the fraction of your push that points where the crate is going.

4.4 The cross product: how much do these disagree?

The cross product asks the opposite question, and answers with a vector rather than a number. It points perpendicular to both inputs, and its length is the area of the parallelogram they span.

Turn the knob in Plate 12 and watch the two products trade off. There is only one thing to notice, and it takes about five seconds.

Trap · which product does this problem want?

Ask what kind of answer the question needs. A number — work, power, a projection, an angle, a flux through a surface — means dot. A vector or an area — torque, angular momentum, the force on a charge moving through a magnetic field, the normal to a plane — means cross. Getting this right takes two seconds and is worth more than remembering either formula.

4.5 Vector fields, and the three derivatives

Now let a vector depend on where you are. A field assigns a value to every point in space: the electric field around a charge, the velocity at every point in a river. Two kinds matter here — a scalar field assigns a number to each point (a temperature map), and a vector field assigns an arrow (a wind map).

There are exactly three derivative operators on this exam, and each answers a different question.

The gradient is the one worth seeing drawn, because it has a property that ties this part straight back to Part 3.

The loop closes

For f = x² + y², the gradient is 2xi + 2yj. At the cast's point (3,4) that is 6i + 8j — magnitude 10, pointing directly away from the origin.

Its direction is exactly the radius, so it is perpendicular to the circle through that point. Which is the same sentence as “a tangent is perpendicular to its radius” from Part 3, arrived at from a completely different direction. The gradient is always perpendicular to the level curve — always, for every scalar field, not just this one.

4.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Dot gives a number; cross gives a vector. Decide which the question needs before you compute anything.
  • A·B = 0 means perpendicular. This is how you test for a right angle without finding one.
  • A·B = AxBx + AyBy + AzBz — too short to be worth looking up.
  • The unit vector is A/|A|.
  • Gradient points uphill, steepest first, and is perpendicular to the level curve.
  • Scalar in / vector out = gradient. Vector in / scalar out = divergence. Vector in / vector out = curl.
  • The cast in 3-D: 3i + 4j + 12k has magnitude 13.

A vector is a magnitude with a direction, and its magnitude is always Pythagoras — twice, in three dimensions.

The dot product measures agreement and returns a number; the cross product measures disagreement and returns a perpendicular vector whose length is an area. The gradient points uphill and is always perpendicular to the level curve, which is why Part 3's tangent rule was never really about circles.
