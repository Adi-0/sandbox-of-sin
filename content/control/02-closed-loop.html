The Closed Loop

Closing a loop moves the poles. Where they move to is what the system does — and since the gain is the one thing you get to choose, the whole design problem is: which point on that path do I want?

2.1 One equation, and everything comes out of it

Part 1 gave the closed-loop transfer function as G/(1+GH). Its poles are wherever the denominator vanishes, so:

1 + G(s)H(s) = 0

Write GH = K·N(s)/D(s) — numerator, denominator, and the gain pulled out front — and clear the fraction:

D(s) + K·N(s) = 0

That is the characteristic equation, and it is worth staring at for a moment. At K = 0 it is D(s) = 0 — the open-loop poles. As K → ∞ it is N(s) = 0 — the open-loop zeros. So the closed-loop poles start at the open-loop poles and travel to the open-loop zeros, and the path between is fixed by the plant. The gain does not choose the path. It chooses how far along it you are.

Why the locus is a straight line for two poles

Take G = K/[s(s+6)]. The characteristic equation is s² + 6s + K = 0, so the two roots always sum to −6 — the coefficient of s never changes. Two numbers summing to −6 and complex must be −3 ± jω. The real part is pinned at −3 for every gain above 9, so raising K moves the poles straight up. Nothing was computed: the sum of the roots is the second coefficient, and that coefficient has no K in it.

2.2 The plane, read as performance

The whole point of putting the poles on a diagram is that their position is the answer. Three readings, and each of them is a piece of geometry.

Trap · those formulas assume exactly two poles

Every performance expression above is derived for a single complex pair. Hand them a third pole and they are estimates at best. The licence to use them anyway is the dominance rule: a real pole may be ignored if it sits at least five times further left than the pair's real part. At 5× its exponential has decayed by e⁻⁵ = 0.7% in the time the pair takes one time constant, so it is genuinely gone. At 1.5× it is not, and the true settling time will be longer than 4/σ says. A question that hands you three poles is usually testing whether you checked.

2.3 Choosing the gain

A specification is not a number to hit, it is a region to land in — and once it is drawn on the plane, “what gain?” becomes “which part of the locus is inside?”

Worked · picking K, and finding out what gain cannot do

A unity-feedback system has G(s) = K/[s(s+6)]. Choose K for no more than 9.5% overshoot, then check whether it settles within 1.5 s. Then check 1.0 s.

  1. Overshoot fixes ζ. 9.5% is the anchor worth memorising: ζ = 0.6.
  2. Match the characteristic equation to the standard form. s² + 6s + K = s² + 2ζωn·s + ωn² so ωn = √K and 2ζωn = 6.
  3. Solve for K. K = 25 The poles are at −3 ± 4j, ωn = 5.
  4. Settling time. ts = 4/3 = 1.33 s — inside 1.5 s, so both requirements are met at K = 25.
  5. Now 1.0 s. That needs σ ≥ 4. But σ is pinned at 3 for every gain, so no value of K meets it.

The last step is the one worth remembering. Gain slides the poles along the locus; it cannot move the locus. When a specification asks for something the path never passes through, no amount of tuning will produce it — the plant has to be changed, or a compensator added to bend the path. That is not a failure of the method; it is the method telling you the truth early.

What the exam actually asks

Constructing a root locus by hand is a drawing skill, and the FE does not have time for it. What it does ask is the reading: how many branches, where do they start, how many asymptotes and at what angles, and is this system stable at this gain. All of those are counting or one substitution. Learn the five lines of plate 12.4 and the geometry of plate 12.5 cold, and treat the sketching as a way of understanding rather than a technique to drill.

2.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Closed-loop poles are the roots of 1 + GH = 0, i.e. of D + KN = 0.
  • Branches start at the open-loop poles and end at the zeros. n of them; n − m go to infinity.
  • Asymptotes: (2q+1)180°/(n−m), meeting at (Σp − Σz)/(n−m).
  • Real-axis locus: odd count of poles and zeros to the right.
  • ζ = cos θ, ωn = |s|, ts = 4/σ. Angle, radius, horizontal distance.
  • For K/[s(s+a)] with unity feedback: ζ = a/(2√K), ωn = √K, and σ = a/2 at every gain.
  • Dominance: ignore a real pole only if it is 5× further left than the dominant pair.

The closed-loop poles are the roots of one polynomial, D + KN, and the root locus is nothing more than a plot of those roots as K sweeps. Everything else in this part is geometry laid over that plot: an angle is a damping ratio, a distance from the origin is a natural frequency, and a distance from the imaginary axis is a settling time. A specification, drawn, is a region; the design question is whether the path goes through it.

The most useful thing the picture tells you is a limit. For a two-pole plant the branches turn vertical, so gain buys speed of oscillation and nothing else — no settling time faster than the plant's own breakaway point is available at any price. For a three-pole plant the branches turn towards the imaginary axis, so there is a gain at which the loop starts oscillating on its own. Part 3 finds that gain exactly, without plotting anything, using a test that only looks at the coefficients.
