Feedback and Block Diagrams

Measure what you got, compare it with what you asked for, and act on the difference. That sentence is the entire subject; the rest of this module is working out what it costs and when it goes wrong.

1.1 Open loop, and why nobody ships it

An open-loop system acts and hopes. You set the oven dial to 200 °C, the element delivers whatever power that dial means, and if the door is open or the mains sags, the oven is simply wrong — nothing in it knows. Its accuracy is the accuracy of every component in the chain, multiplied together.

A closed-loop system measures the result and drives the difference to zero. It cannot be more accurate than its sensor, but it is almost completely indifferent to everything in front of the sensor. That trade — throw away gain, buy accuracy — is the reason essentially every amplifier, regulator, motor drive and autopilot on earth is built this way.

Worked · deriving the formula, so you never memorise it wrong

Three lines, and worth doing on scrap paper the first time a question makes you doubt the sign.

  1. The junction subtracts: E = R − HC.
  2. The forward block acts on the error: C = GE = G(R − HC).
  3. Collect the C terms: C(1 + GH) = GR

So C/R = G/(1+GH). The plus came from the minus — the C term crossed the equals sign and changed sign, which is exactly why the denominator's sign is opposite to the junction's. That is the one fact people get backwards under time pressure, and it is two lines of algebra away at all times.

Trap · the sign reads backwards

Negative feedback gives 1 + GH. Positive feedback gives 1 − GH. Every instinct says otherwise. If you have thirty seconds of doubt, sanity-check the answer instead of the algebra: negative feedback must make the gain smaller than G, and if your expression makes it bigger you have the wrong sign. And note what GH = 1 does to the positive-feedback denominator — infinite gain with no input is not an amplifier, it is an oscillator.

1.2 What 1 + GH divides

The factor 1 + GH is not just a denominator. It is the exchange rate of the whole subject: everything feedback improves, it improves by exactly this factor, and it pays for all of it out of gain.

This is the op-amp, again

Electronics Part 5 asserted two rules — no current into the inputs, and the inputs sit at the same voltage — and got the non-inverting gain 1 + Rf/R1 in one line. That second rule was never a law; it was E = R − HC with GH of about 10⁵, so the error is a few microvolts and rounds to zero. The virtual short is a large loop gain, seen from the inside. And 1/H for the non-inverting amplifier is exactly 1 + Rf/R1 — the divider that feeds the inverting input, inverted. The rule you memorised was this plate's first line all along.

More loop gain is not free

Reading the plate above, the obvious move is to make GH enormous and collect all four benefits at once. Real loops will not let you. Every extra stage of gain brings its own pole, each pole adds up to 90° of phase lag, and once the lag reaches 180° the feedback that was subtracting is adding — at which point the system does not settle, it oscillates. The entire remainder of this module exists because of that sentence: Part 2 watches the poles move as the gain rises, Part 3 decides when they cross over, and Part 4 measures how much room is left.

1.3 Reducing a diagram

Exam diagrams are drawn to look worse than they are. There are only four moves, they are all mechanical, and the order you apply them in is fixed: innermost first.

Worked · a minor loop inside a major one

A controller G₁ drives a plant G₂. The plant has its own minor feedback path H₂ — a tachometer, in a motor drive — and unity feedback is closed around the lot. Find C/R, then evaluate it for G₁ = 4, G₂ = 10, H₂ = 0.5.

  1. Innermost loop first. Ignore everything outside it: G₂/(1 + G₂H₂). It is now a single block.
  2. Cascade. The controller is in series with that block, so multiply: G₁G₂/(1 + G₂H₂). This is the forward path of what is left.
  3. Close the outer loop, with H = 1: C/R = G₁G₂ / (1 + G₂H₂ + G₁G₂)
  4. Numbers. 40/46 = 0.870

Two things to take from the shape of that answer. The denominator is 1 plus every loop gain — 5 from the minor loop, 40 from the major one — which is a reliable check on any reduction you do. And the result sits just below 1 almost regardless of what G₁ and G₂ were, because the outer loop is unity feedback: the answer is set by H, and the gains only decide how close to it you get.

Trap · reducing from the outside in

Started at the outer loop, that diagram invites you to write G₁G₂/(1 + G₁G₂) and lose H₂ entirely, or to sweep a block into a loop gain it was never inside. Work innermost-first, and after every step redraw the collapsed block — even as a scribble. The rule for what belongs in a loop gain is physical, not algebraic: trace one complete trip round the loop and multiply everything the signal passes through. A block downstream of the takeoff point is not on that trip.

1.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • C/R = G/(1+GH). Negative feedback → plus; positive feedback → minus.
  • E/R = 1/(1+GH) — same denominator, and the reason big loop gain means small error.
  • Loop gain L = GH is what one trip round multiplies by. Stability and margin are read off L, not G.
  • Large GH ⇒ C/R ≈ 1/H. Check the loop gain before you use that shortcut.
  • Sensitivity, disturbance and gain are all divided by 1 + GH; bandwidth is multiplied by it.
  • Cascade multiplies, parallel adds, and you reduce innermost first.
  • Move a takeoff past a block and the branch gains a copy of it — or a 1/G going the other way.

Feedback is a trade, and the exchange rate is 1 + GH. Spend gain, and you buy insensitivity to the plant, rejection of disturbances, and speed — in exactly that proportion, all at once. What you end up with depends on H, which you choose and can build precisely, rather than on G, which drifts with temperature and varies between units. That is why the block diagram is the right picture: it puts the thing you trust and the thing you do not in different boxes.

And here is where this module is going. Put G(s) = K/[s(s+6)] — a motor, near enough — inside a unity loop, and the closed-loop denominator is s² + 6s + K. At K = 25 the roots are −3 ± 4j: the same pair Mathematics Part 7 solved as algebra and Linear Systems Part 2 built out of a 120 Ω resistor, with ζ = 0.6, 9.5% overshoot and a 53.13° angle. The gain you turn up in a control loop is the same knob as the resistor you change in an RLC circuit — and Part 2 draws the path those roots take as you turn it.
