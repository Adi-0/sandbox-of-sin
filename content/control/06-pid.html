Controller Performance and PID

Part 5 left a plant with two requirements that one gain could not satisfy at once. The fix is not a cleverer gain: it is to stop asking a single number to do three jobs, and put three terms in front of the plant instead.

6.1 Three questions about one error

Everything a PID controller does comes from asking three different things about the same error signal, and acting on each answer:

u(t) = Kp·e + Ki∫e dt + Kd·de/dt

  • How big is the error? — proportional. Push harder when further away.
  • How long has it been there? — integral. Keep adding to the push while any error remains.
  • Which way is it heading? — derivative. Ease off early if it is closing fast.

In the s-domain those are a constant, a 1/s, and an s — and putting them over a common denominator is worth doing once, because the result says what the controller is:

Gc(s) = (Kd s² + Kp s + Ki) / s

One pole at the origin and two zeros. The pole is the integrator — it raises the system type by one, and Part 5 says that drives the steady-state error to zero. The zeros are what pull the root locus back to the left, recovering the damping the pole cost. The structure of PID is the two halves of that fraction pulling against each other, which is why the three gains are tuned together rather than one at a time.

6.2 Why the integral term works

The claim that an integrator removes steady-state error is easy to state and easy to disbelieve. The plate below is the argument: it opens the controller up and plots the three terms separately, so you can see where the control effort comes from once the error has gone.

The effort has to come from somewhere

Hold a plant's output at 1 and the plant needs a steady input — for the plate's plant, exactly 8, because G(0) = 1/8. A P controller can only produce 8 by having an error of 8/Kp sitting there permanently. An integral term produces the same 8 with no error at all: it accumulated the value during the transient and has no reason to give it back, because its input is now zero. Select PI on the plate and read the four numbers at the end — P term 0.07, D term nothing, integral 7.95, total 8.02. That is the whole mechanism, and it is also why an integrator winds up when the plant cannot deliver: the term keeps accumulating regardless.

Why real derivative terms are filtered

A pure derivative has gain that rises without limit with frequency, so it amplifies sensor noise ferociously — a clean 1 mV of hiss at 1 kHz becomes a very loud command. Real controllers therefore use Kd·s/(1 + s/N), a derivative with a pole well above the loop bandwidth, and often take the derivative of the output rather than the error so that a step change in the command does not produce an impulse. Neither refinement is on this exam, but both are the reason a real PID block has more than three settings.

6.3 The plant Part 5 could not satisfy

Worked · the same plant, three controllers

The plant is G(s) = 1/[(s+1)(s+2)(s+4)], the one where a 5% error demanded K = 152 on a loop that goes unstable at K = 90. Compare what the three controllers achieve.

  1. P, Kp = 20. Closed loop s³ + 7s² + 14s + 28. Error 28.6%, overshoot 27%, settling 4.9 s. Accurate: no.
  2. PI, Kp = 20, Ki = 10. The open loop gains a pole at the origin, so the characteristic equation becomes s⁴ + 7s³ + 14s² + 28s + 10 Error zero, exactly — but the extra 90° of lag shows up as more overshoot and a longer settle.
  3. PID, Kp = 40, Ki = 20, Kd = 15. The derivative adds Kd to the s² coefficient: s⁴ + 7s³ + 29s² + 48s + 20 Error zero, overshoot 6.6%, settling 2.5 s. Better than P on every count at once.
  4. Check it is stable, with Part 3's array on that quartic. The first column comes out 1, 7, 22.1, 41.7, 20 — all positive, so no sign changes.

Set the plate above to each preset in turn and watch the three numbers move. The point is not that PID is magic — it is that three independent terms can satisfy three independent requirements, and one gain cannot. Note also what the derivative did to the polynomial: it raised the s² coefficient from 14 to 29, and it is that coefficient the Routh array needs to be large. The derivative term is buying stability in the most literal way available.

Trap · derivative cannot fix an offset

The single commonest confusion in this topic. The derivative term acts on de/dt, and in steady state the error is constant, so de/dt is zero and the term contributes nothing. A loop sitting 8% low will sit 8% low with any Kd you like. Equally: the integral term cannot fix ringing — it makes it worse, because 90° of extra lag is exactly what a ringing loop cannot afford. Match the symptom to the term: offset is integral, ringing is derivative, sluggish is proportional.

6.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • u = Kp·e + Ki∫e + Kd·ė, and Gc = (Kd s² + Kp s + Ki)/s.
  • I adds a pole at the origin — type goes up by one, steady-state error to a step goes to zero.
  • D adds a zero, contributes +90°, damps the response, and does nothing to the steady-state error.
  • I costs −90° of phase at every frequency. That is what makes PI loops ring, and why D usually comes with it.
  • Kp: faster, less error, more overshoot, less margin. Never zero error.
  • PI for accuracy, PD for damping, PID when you need both.
  • Tune in order: stable → Kd → Ki → Kp.

A PID controller is three answers to three questions about the same signal, and its whole character is in the fraction (Kd s² + Kp s + Ki)/s: one pole that buys accuracy at the cost of phase, and two zeros that buy the phase back. Every trade in this module is present in that one expression, which is why almost every loop in industry is running some version of it.

And look at what has been assembled. Part 1 gave the loop and its algebra; Part 2 said where the poles go; Part 3 said when they cross; Part 4 measured how far from crossing they are; Part 5 said whether the loop ends up in the right place; and this part supplies the thing you actually get to change.
