Bode Plots and Margins

Part 3 answered “is it stable” with a yes. That is not enough to design with. This part measures how far from unstable a loop is, in the two units engineers actually quote — and both are distances read off a plot you already know how to sketch.

4.1 Why the open loop tells you about the closed loop

The closed-loop poles are where 1 + GH = 0. Rearranged, that is one of the most useful sentences in the subject:

GH(jω) = −1

A complex number equals −1 when its magnitude is 1 and its phase is −180°, at the same frequency. So the question “is this loop close to oscillating” becomes “is there a frequency where the open loop returns a signal the same size as it started, and exactly inverted” — and that is a question you can answer by looking at two curves you already know how to draw.

Why inverted feedback becomes reinforcing

The summing junction subtracts, which is another 180° of phase. Add the plant's own 180° of lag and the signal arriving back is in phase with the one that produced it. If it is also the same size or larger, it sustains itself with no input at all. That is the whole content of the −1 point: the loop's minus sign and the plant's lag have cancelled, and negative feedback has quietly become positive.

4.2 Two crossings, two margins

The magnitude curve crosses 0 dB somewhere; the phase curve crosses −180° somewhere else. Each margin is measured at the other one's crossing, and getting that the wrong way round is the standard mistake.

Worked · both margins, and a check against Part 3

Find the phase crossover, the critical gain and the gain margin for GH = 60/[s(s+2)(s+8)].

  1. Phase crossover. The integrator gives a flat −90°, so the two poles must supply the other 90°: arctan(ω/2) + arctan(ω/8) = 90° Two angles summing to 90° have reciprocal tangents, so ω² = 16 and ωpc = 4 rad/s — the geometric mean of the corners, which is the midpoint on a logarithmic axis.
  2. Magnitude there. |GH| = 0.375
  3. Gain margin. GM = 8.5 dB — the gain could be multiplied by 2.67.
  4. Critical gain. Kcrit = 60 × 2.67 = 160.

160 and 4 rad/s are exactly what the Routh array gave in Part 3, from the coefficients of s³ + 10s² + 16s + K, with no plot involved. The two methods have nothing in common procedurally and must agree, because both are locating the moment the closed-loop poles reach the imaginary axis. For this shape there is a formula worth carrying: ωpc = √(ab) and Kcrit = ab(a+b), which is the same c < ab cubic rule wearing different clothes.

Trap · gain moves one curve only

Multiplying K by ten shifts the whole magnitude curve up by 20 dB and leaves the phase curve completely untouched — a constant has no phase. So the phase crossover frequency never moves, and the gain margin falls by exactly the 20 dB you added. The gain crossover, on the other hand, slides right, and since the phase is still dropping there, the phase margin falls too. One knob, both margins, which is why turning the gain up to go faster always costs stability.

4.3 What a margin is worth

Margins are quoted in the frequency domain and specifications are written in the time domain, so there has to be a bridge. There is, it is one line, and it is used constantly.

Where the rule of thumb gives up

Above about 65° the exact relationship flattens and ζ ≈ PM/100 starts understating the damping badly — at 75° the true ζ is 0.95, not 0.75. It is also derived for a second-order loop with a single dominant pair, so a plant with a resonance or a right-half-plane zero can have a perfectly respectable phase margin and still behave poorly. Both margins being healthy is a good sign, not a proof. On this exam it is a good sign that is worth full marks; in practice it is where the simulation starts.

4.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Instability is GH = −1: magnitude 1 and phase −180° at the same frequency.
  • PM is read at the 0 dB crossing: 180° + ∠GH.
  • GM is read at the −180° crossing: −|GH| in dB.
  • Targets: PM 45–65°, GM 6–12 dB. Both positive means stable.
  • ζ ≈ PM/100 — and 60° ⇒ ζ 0.6 ⇒ 9.5% overshoot.
  • Gain moves the magnitude curve only. ωpc is fixed by the plant; ωgc and both margins move with K.
  • For K/[s(s+a)(s+b)]: ωpc = √(ab), Kcrit = ab(a+b).

All of this part is one substitution. The closed loop misbehaves when GH = −1, and a Bode plot shows magnitude and phase against frequency, so the distance from −1 is visible as two gaps — how far the magnitude is below unity where the phase hits −180°, and how far the phase is above −180° where the magnitude hits unity. Nothing is being computed that Routh could not compute; what the plot adds is a quantity where the array gave a verdict.

And notice what has now been established four separate ways for the same plant. The locus of plate 91 showed the branches crossing at ω = 4; the Routh array of Part 3 computed K = 160 from the coefficients; this part measured both off a Bode plot; and the step response of plate 94 heard the result. Four methods, one fact — which is what it looks like when a subject is properly joined up. Part 5 changes the question entirely: not whether the loop is stable, but whether it ends up in the right place at all.
