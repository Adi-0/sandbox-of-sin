Synthesis and the Mixed Bench

Four subtopics, six parts, one loop. The root locus, the Routh array, the Bode margins and the step response are not four related techniques — they are four questions asked of a single polynomial, and they have to agree.

7.1 One gain, four views

Every panel on that plate is drawn from s³ + 10s² + 16s + K, so moving one knob moves all four. Take K to 160 and watch them arrive at their boundaries together. That is not a tidy coincidence arranged for the last part of the module — it is what the module is:

Why the four methods have to agree

All four are asking where the roots of 1 + GH = 0 are. The locus plots them, Routh counts the bad ones, the Bode margins measure the distance to the moment one goes bad, and the step response is what those roots do. Different arithmetic, one object — which makes them a free check on each other. If your Routh answer and your Bode answer disagree, one of them is wrong, and you now know to look.

7.4 The six mistakes that cost the most

Worth one last look before the bench
  1. The sign reads backwards. Negative feedback puts a plus in the denominator. If your answer makes the gain bigger than G, you have it the wrong way round.
  2. Reducing outermost-first. It drags blocks into loop gains they were never inside. Collapse the innermost loop, redraw, repeat.
  3. Swapping the two margins. Gain margin is read where the phase crosses −180°; phase margin where the magnitude crosses 0 dB. Both are on the open loop.
  4. The 1 + on the wrong error formula. Step is 1/(1+Kp); ramp and parabola are bare 1/Kv and 1/Ka.
  5. Type confused with order. Type counts poles at the origin. A fourth-order plant with no integrator is type 0.
  6. Expecting D to fix an offset. In steady state the error is constant, so its derivative is zero and the D term contributes nothing at all. Offset is always the integral's job.

7.5 Reading a stem quickly

  1. A diagram with boxes and a circle? Reduction. Innermost first, and the answer is almost always G/(1+GH) with something substituted.
  2. “For what range of K…” or “how many roots in the right half-plane”? Routh. Check the coefficients for a missing or negative one first — it is sometimes the whole question.
  3. Decibels, degrees, or “crossover”? Margins. Identify which crossing the stem is describing before doing anything.
  4. “Steady-state”, “final value”, or a ramp input? Count the poles at the origin, pick the matching constant, apply the right formula.
  5. Overshoot, settling or ζ given? Second-order geometry. Convert to ζ first; overshoot identifies it uniquely, and 0.5/0.6/0.707 → 16/9.5/4.3% covers most of them.
  6. Kp, Ki, Kd, or “the controller”? Part 6. Match the symptom to the term and remember which one costs phase.
Where the marks actually are

Control Systems is 6 to 9 questions, and they are unusually concentrated. Block reduction and G/(1+GH) alone are worth a couple of them, and both are mechanical. Routh range questions and steady-state error questions are formula recall with one limit. Margin questions are almost always reading a plot rather than constructing one. The genuinely slow item is a full root-locus sketch — and it is also the rarest, because the exam does not have time for it either. If you learn the plate above and nothing else in this module, you will still get most of the area.

7.6 A set under exam conditions

Ten questions drawn from all six parts, at the real pace of 2 minutes 50 seconds each.

Six parts, and one polynomial underneath all of them. A loop has a characteristic equation; where its roots sit decides whether the system settles, how fast, with how much overshoot, and how much room is left before it stops settling at all. Those are not five related facts — they are five questions asked of one set of numbers, which is why the locus, the array, the margins and the response can never disagree.

What makes it engineering rather than algebra is the last part. Everything up to Part 5 describes a loop; Part 6 changes one, and it does so by adding exactly the poles and zeros the earlier parts said were missing — an integrator because Part 5 showed the error needed one, a zero because Part 4 showed the phase could not afford the integrator on its own. The design follows from the analysis, term by term. That is the whole discipline, and it fits on one drawing plate.
