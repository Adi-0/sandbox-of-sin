Stability and Routh–Hurwitz

A system is stable when every pole is in the left half-plane. Finding the poles of a fifth-order polynomial is not something you will do in two minutes — so this part is a test that counts the bad ones without finding any of them.

3.1 What the word means

Every closed-loop pole contributes a term e^(st) to the response, and with s = σ + jω that is e^(σt) times an oscillation. The sign of σ decides everything:

Pole locationIts termThe system
left half-plane, σ < 0decaysstable — the transient dies and the output settles
on the jω axis, σ = 0neither grows nor decaysmarginally stable — it rings for ever at that frequency
right half-plane, σ > 0growsunstable — the output runs away until something saturates

One pole in the right half-plane is enough. It does not matter how far left the other nine are; that single growing exponential eventually dominates everything. So the question is never “where are the poles” but “is any pole not in the left half-plane” — and that is a yes-or-no question with a yes-or-no answer, which is why a counting test can settle it.

3.2 The check that costs nothing

3.3 The array

Routh's test builds a table of numbers from the coefficients, and the number of sign changes down its first column is the number of roots in the right half-plane. That sentence is the whole method. Nothing is factored, nothing is solved, and the work grows slowly with order.

A row of zeros is not a failure — it is the answer

When an entire row comes out zero, the polynomial has roots placed symmetrically about the origin, and for a loop that was stable up to this gain, that means a conjugate pair sitting exactly on the imaginary axis. Take the row above the zeros as a polynomial — the auxiliary polynomial — and solve it. Its roots are that pair. So the array does not merely tell you the loop is on the boundary, it tells you the frequency it will oscillate at. Set the first system on the plate to K = 160 and watch it happen: the s¹ row goes to zeros and 10s² + 160 = 0 gives ω = 4 rad/s.

The other special case, briefly

Occasionally the first entry of a row is zero while the rest of the row is not. The division that builds the next row then blows up. The standard fix is to replace the zero with a small positive ε, carry on symbolically, and read the signs in the limit as ε → 0 — an entry like 2 − 3/ε is large and negative. It is worth recognising, and it is rare enough on this exam that it is not worth drilling.

3.4 The question the exam actually asks

Almost every Routh question on the FE is one of two shapes: how many roots are in the right half-plane, or for what range of K is this stable. The second is the same array with a symbol in it.

Worked · the range of gain, and the frequency at the edge

A unity-feedback system has G(s) = K/[s(s+2)(s+8)] — the plant whose locus crossed the axis in Part 2. Find the range of K for stability, and the frequency of oscillation at the boundary.

  1. Characteristic equation. s³ + 10s² + 16s + K = 0
  2. Free test. All coefficients present and positive provided K > 0. Inconclusive, so build the array.
  3. The array. s³: 1, 16 / s²: 10, K / s¹: (160−K)/10 / s⁰: K
  4. Make the first column positive. The bottom row gives K > 0; the row above gives 160 − K > 0. So 0 < K < 160.
  5. The boundary. At K = 160 the s¹ row is zero. The auxiliary polynomial is the s² row: 10s² + 160 = 0 → s = ±4j so it oscillates at 4 rad/s.

Two numbers, one array, no root-finding — and they match the locus of plate 91 exactly, which is the point: the locus showed the crossing and Routh computed it. For a cubic s³ + as² + bs + c the whole thing collapses to 0 < c < ab, with ω = √b at the boundary. Cubics come up often enough that the shortcut is worth carrying.

Trap · stable is not the same as good

Routh answers a yes-or-no question, and it is easy to leave with the impression that any gain inside the range is acceptable. Look at the response on the plate at K = 140: it is stable, and it is useless — enormous overshoot, ringing that takes an age to die, and no margin at all for a plant that drifts. A design lives well inside the stable range, not at the edge of it. Quantifying “well inside” is what gain and phase margin do, and that is Part 4.

3.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Stable ⇔ every pole strictly in the left half-plane. One bad pole is enough to ruin it.
  • Free test first: any missing or negative coefficient ⇒ unstable. All-positive proves nothing above second order.
  • First two rows are the alternating coefficients; every later entry is (b₁a₂ − a₁b₂)/b₁ from the two rows above.
  • Sign changes in the first column = roots in the right half-plane.
  • A whole row of zeros ⇒ roots on the jω axis. Auxiliary polynomial from the row above gives ω.
  • Cubic shortcut: s³+as²+bs+c is stable iff 0 < c < ab, and ω = √b at the boundary.
  • Rows may be scaled by any positive number — clear fractions freely.

Routh–Hurwitz is a counting argument dressed as arithmetic. It never locates a root; it counts how many are on the wrong side, which is all the question needs. That is why it survives into fifth and sixth order where factoring has long since become hopeless, and why the exam likes it: the answer is a couple of multiplications and a look at four signs.

What it hands you beyond a verdict is the boundary — the exact gain at which the loop tips over, and the frequency it tips over at. Both come out of the same table. But notice what the last plate showed: the response is already bad long before the boundary, so knowing where the cliff is does not tell you where to stand. Part 4 measures the distance to the cliff in the two units engineers actually quote — how much more gain the loop could take, and how much more phase lag — and both are read off a Bode plot rather than a table.
