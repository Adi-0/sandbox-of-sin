Every weight you have set so far, you set by hand. That does not scale past about six neurons, and it certainly does not scale to a hundred trillion synapses. So the question is: can a synapse work out its own weight from information available locally at the synapse?

"Locally" is the hard part. A synapse cannot see the network's output. It cannot see the loss function. It has no idea whether the answer was right. All it knows is when its own input spiked, and when the neuron it feeds spiked. Two streams of timestamps. That is the entire budget.

Remarkably, it is enough — because those two timestamps contain a fragment of something very valuable.

Order is evidence

Suppose your input spiked 5 ms before the neuron fired. You cannot be certain you caused it. But the timing is at least consistent with having helped: your bump was still standing when the threshold was crossed, and Part 3 established that "still standing" means "within about one time constant."

Now suppose your input spiked 5 ms after the neuron fired. Then whatever caused that spike, it was not you. You arrived at the scene after the event.

That asymmetry is real information, and it is available at the synapse for free. The rule that exploits it is called spike-timing-dependent plasticity — STDP — and it is one line:

Δw = +A₊ · e−Δt/τ₊ if Δt > 0      Δw = −A₋ · e+Δt/τ₋ if Δt < 0
Why it looks like this. Δt is the output spike time minus the input spike time. Positive means the input came first, so strengthen. Negative means the input came second, so weaken. The exponential is there because evidence decays: an input 2 ms before the spike is strong evidence of contribution, one 60 ms before is nearly none. τ₊ is 20 ms — the same 20 ms as the membrane, which is not a coincidence but a requirement: the synapse is asking exactly the question the membrane is able to answer. τ₋ is 34 ms, deliberately longer, for a reason two paragraphs below.

Figure 11 is that rule, drawn. Drag through zero and watch the sign flip.

The old slogan for this is Hebb's: neurons that fire together, wire together. STDP is the sharpened version, and the sharpening matters. Not "together" — in order. Hebb's rule as usually stated would strengthen a synapse for firing at the same time as its target, which would happily reinforce coincidences that ran backwards in time. STDP will not. It is a causality filter with a 20 millisecond memory.

One detail in Figure 11 that looks cosmetic and is not: the weakening lobe reaches further out in time than the strengthening one — 34 ms against 20. That asymmetry is doing essential work. What decides the fate of a synapse with random timing is not the height of either lobe but the area under it, because random timing samples the whole window evenly. Here the weakening area is about 1.8 times the strengthening area, so random pairings do not cancel; they net out negative. Without that thumb on the scale a synapse fed pure noise drifts upward forever, because every so often it gets lucky. Measured windows in real synapses have the same longer tail on the weakening side.

A synapse fires at completely random times, uncorrelated with its target neuron. Over many hours, what happens to its weight under the rule in Figure 11?
Down to zero. Random timing means roughly equal numbers of "before" and "after" pairings, and because the weakening lobe covers more area, equal numbers do not cancel — they net out negative. This is the mechanism that does the pruning. Nobody decides which synapses are useless; useless synapses starve. You are about to watch it happen.

Watching it find something nobody pointed at

Now the payoff. Figure 12 has twelve input lines feeding one neuron. All twelve fire noisily. Six of them — you are not told which — also fire together at the moments marked by the shaded bands. The weights all start equal.

There is no teacher here. No labels, no target output, no error signal, no backward pass. Just twelve timestamped streams, one neuron, and the rule from Figure 11.

Press play and give it about ten seconds.

Here is what you are watching. When the six pattern lines fire together they contribute 6 × 3.6 = 21.6 mV, just clearing the 20 mV threshold, and the neuron fires. Their spikes therefore land just before an output spike, over and over — the right-hand lobe of the STDP window, every single time the pattern appears. They get stronger.

The six noise lines fire whenever they like. Sometimes just before an output spike, sometimes just after, with no consistency. Their pairings net out negative, so they get weaker. And as they weaken, they contribute less, so the neuron's spikes become even more tightly locked to the pattern, so the pattern lines get an even cleaner signal. The process sharpens itself.

10 s is all it takes for a single neuron to become a detector for a pattern that nobody specified, labelled, or supervised. The shaded bands are drawn for your benefit; the neuron never receives them.

Push the noise slider up while it runs and watch it struggle; push it down and watch it lock on. There is a real limit here, not a demonstration artifact: once the noise can fire the neuron on its own, the output spikes stop being informative about the pattern and the whole mechanism loses its grip.

Two honest caveats. First, the learning rates in Figure 12 are far faster than measured biological STDP so the demo fits in a coffee break, and the starting weights are tuned so that the six coincident inputs only just clear threshold; the outcome is the same shape, just slower and fussier to set up in reality. Second, and more important: this is not how large neuromorphic systems are trained today. STDP is beautiful, local, and cheap, and it reliably finds repeating patterns — but it optimises nothing in particular. There is no objective function it is descending. Ask it to classify handwritten digits at competitive accuracy and it will disappoint you. Part 7 explains what people actually do instead, and why they are not happy about it either.

What STDP buys the hardware

Set aside the accuracy question for a moment and look at what this rule costs to implement, because that is why chip designers keep it.

To apply STDP, a synapse needs: its own weight, a fading memory of when its input last fired, and a fading memory of when its output last fired. Three numbers, all sitting at the synapse. No global state. No stored activations from a forward pass. No error travelling backwards through the network. Nothing needs to be fetched from anywhere.

Compare that to backpropagation, which requires keeping every intermediate activation from the forward pass in memory so the backward pass can use them, then moving all of it — the postage bill from Part 1, paid on every layer, in both directions. STDP updates a weight in the place where the weight already lives, using only signals that were passing through anyway.

That is the whole reason on-chip learning appears on neuromorphic hardware at all. It is not that STDP is better at learning. It is that STDP is the only learning rule that fits inside the architecture without reintroducing the exact problem the architecture exists to avoid.

STDP changes a weight using only two local timestamps: strengthen when the input arrived before the output, weaken when it arrived after, with a 20 ms exponential window and slightly more weakening than strengthening.

That is enough for a neuron to find a repeating pattern in noise with no labels and no error signal — and, crucially, to do it without moving a single number off the synapse.
