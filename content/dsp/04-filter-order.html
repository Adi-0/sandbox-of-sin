Order, Roll-Off and Butterworth

Part 3 built a filter that fell thirty-five decibels short. This part is about closing that gap — what a steeper filter costs, which family to reach for, and the trade that almost every real design makes instead of paying the full price.

4.1 Order is the only lever on steepness

Each pole in a transfer function contributes 20 dB per decade of roll-off once you are well past the corner, and they add. A filter with n poles — an nth-order filter — falls at 20n dB per decade, equivalently 6n dB per octave.

Decades and octaves, once, so they stop colliding

A decade is a factor of ten in frequency; an octave is a factor of two. Since 20 log 2 = 6.02, one pole gives 20 dB per decade and 6 dB per octave — the same slope in two units. Quoting the wrong one gives an answer off by a factor of about 3.3, and it is the most common arithmetic error in filter questions.

4.2 Butterworth: flat where it matters

Knowing you want n poles does not say where to put them. Different placements trade passband flatness against skirt steepness against phase behaviour, and each named family is one choice of that trade. The default — and the one the FE means unless it says otherwise — is Butterworth.

The cast arrives as a near-miss

This compilation's damping ratio has been ζ = 0.6 since Mathematics Part 1 — the 3-4-5 triangle, −3 ± 4j, 9.5% overshoot, 53.13°. Butterworth's second-order section wants ζ = 0.707. So the cast sits just on the peaking side of flat, and you can compute exactly how far: with √(1−ζ²) = 0.8 — the triangle again — the peak is

peak = 1/0.96 = 1.042 → +0.35 dB

A third of a decibel of bulge, at 0.53 fn. That tiny hump is precisely what Butterworth gives up steepness to avoid, and pushing ζ from 0.6 to 0.707 is the whole of the difference. It is the same trade as Control Systems Part 6: damp it more and it stops peaking, at the cost of speed.

4.3 Designing to a specification

Real questions do not say “use six poles”. They give a passband edge, a stopband edge and a required attenuation, and ask what it takes. The formula on the plate above answers it directly, and the picture is a pair of forbidden regions the response must not enter.

Worked · the telephone anti-alias filter, finished

Speech to 3.4 kHz, sampled at 8 kHz, and the 5 kHz interferer must be down 40 dB before the converter. How many poles? Then: what happens if the sample rate is doubled to 16 kHz instead?

  1. Set up the ratio. Passband edge 3.4 kHz, stopband edge 5 kHz: fs/fc = 1.47 That is only 0.168 of a decade — the transition band is desperately narrow.
  2. Apply the order formula. n ≥ 11.9
  3. Twelve poles. That is six cascaded second-order active sections — a serious, expensive, drift-prone piece of hardware, for a telephone channel.
  4. Now double the sample rate to 16 kHz. The fold point moves to 8 kHz, so the interferer no longer has to be killed at 5 kHz — anything landing above 8 kHz is what matters, and the stopband edge moves out: n ≥ 5.4 → 6 poles
  5. Six poles instead of twelve, for twice the sample rate.

That last step is the single most useful thing in this part. Sample rate and filter order buy the same thing, and sample rate is almost always cheaper — it costs memory and arithmetic, both of which have been getting cheaper for fifty years, while poles cost precision components that drift with temperature and age. It is why converters oversample heavily and then do the sharp filtering digitally, where a pole costs nothing but a multiply. Parts 5 and 6 are about those digital filters.

4.4 The other families, briefly

Butterworth is the default, not the only option. Two others are worth recognising by name and by their one distinguishing trait, because that is the depth the exam asks at.

FamilyPassbandSkirtUse it when
Butterworthmaximally flat, no ripplemoderatethe default — nothing about the problem argues otherwise
Chebyshevripples by a chosen amountsteepest for a given orderyou need sharpness and can tolerate ripple
Besselflat, and linear phasegentlestpulse shape matters — linear phase means constant delay, so the wave is not distorted

The pattern is one trade seen three ways: you can buy a steeper skirt with passband ripple, or buy waveform fidelity with a gentler skirt, but you cannot have everything at a fixed order. Chebyshev reaching a given attenuation with fewer poles than Butterworth is not magic — it is the ripple being spent on steepness.

4.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • 20n dB/decade = 6n dB/octave for an n-pole filter. A decade is ×10, an octave is ×2.
  • Butterworth: |H| = 1/√(1 + (f/fc)²ⁿ), and −3 dB at fc for every order.
  • Order needed: n ≥ log(10^(A/10)−1) / 2log(fs/fc) — and always round up.
  • Only the ratio of the band edges matters, never the absolute frequencies.
  • Second-order Butterworth is ζ = 0.707 — exactly the damping at which peaking stops.
  • Butterworth flat, Chebyshev steeper with ripple, Bessel linear phase and gentle.
  • Widening the transition band is cheaper than adding poles. For an anti-alias filter that means sampling faster.

A filter design is two numbers: how far apart the band edges are, and how far down the stopband must be. Their ratio sets the order, and the order sets the cost. Butterworth is the default family because it makes the cutoff mean one thing at every order and leaves the passband flat.

The lesson that outlives the arithmetic is the one the worked example showed: a narrow transition band is ruinously expensive, and the usual fix is not a better filter but a faster sampler. That is why the analog part of a modern signal chain is often a gentle two- or three-pole filter and nothing more, with everything sharp happening after the converter. Which raises the obvious question — what does a filter even look like once the signal is a list of numbers? That is Part 5.
