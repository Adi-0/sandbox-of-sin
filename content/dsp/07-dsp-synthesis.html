Synthesis and the Mixed Bench

One signal chain has been running since Part 2 — speech to 3.4 kHz, an interferer at 5 kHz, a sampler at 8 kHz. Here it is with every control in your hands at once, followed by a set under exam conditions.

7.1 One chain, both knobs

Start where the module started: 8 kHz, two poles. The interferer arrives at 3 kHz, inside the speech band, and nothing after the converter can remove it. Now fix it twice.

  • Add poles. Push the anti-alias filter's order up and the interferer is attenuated before it can fold. It works, and panel three shows the price: twelve poles for 40 dB at this rate.
  • Raise the rate. Above 10 kHz the tone stops folding at all — it stays at 5 kHz, outside the speech band, where a digital filter can take it out for the cost of a few multiplies.

Both defences work and they are not equally priced. That asymmetry is the single most useful thing this module has to give you, and it is why the analog front end of a modern converter is usually a gentle two- or three-pole filter and nothing more.

7.2 How few ideas were doing the work

Seven parts, and almost all of it came from four sentences.

The ideaWhere it did the work
A signal is a set of frequencies, and it has a highest one Part 1 built it; every other part consumed it. Bandwidth, Nyquist rate, filter specifications and the frequency axis of a Bode plot are all the same idea.
Sampling copies the spectrum to every multiple of fs Part 2's whole theorem. Overlap of those copies is aliasing, the copies touching is why the criterion is strict, and the wrap-around is why the z-plane is a circle.
Filtering is multiplying each frequency by a number Parts 3 to 6 without exception. Analog or digital, the only questions are what the numbers are and what it costs to make them change quickly with frequency.
Poles and zeros are places, and their distances give the response Part 3's corner, Part 4's order, Part 6's unit circle. The s-plane and the z-plane are the same picture with the boundary rolled from a line into a circle.

7.3 The six mistakes that cost the most

MistakeThe check that catches it
Swapping Nyquist rate and Nyquist frequencyRate = 2fmax and belongs to the signal; frequency = fs/2 and belongs to the sampler.
An alias answer above fs/2Impossible by construction. You subtracted the wrong multiple.
The 2π in 1/(2πRC)ω has no 2π; f has one. Getting it backwards is a factor of 40.
Decades and octaves20n dB per decade = 6n dB per octave. Mixing them is a factor of 3.3.
Adding RMS values or harmonic amplitudesPowers add, amplitudes do not. 3 and 4 make 5.
Rounding a filter order downOrder is an integer and the requirement is a floor. Always round up.

The classification takes about five seconds and it is most of the battle.

7.5 A set under exam conditions

Everything from all seven parts, mixed, at exam pace.

This module is about one boundary. Below fs/2 a sampled signal is the original signal, exactly and recoverably. Above it, the information is not degraded but gone, and gone in a way no later processing can reach — because sampling maps many input frequencies onto each output frequency, and that map has no inverse.

Everything else here is in service of that boundary. Part 1 gave a signal a highest frequency so the boundary could be stated. Parts 3 and 4 built the filter that makes the highest frequency true, and priced it. Parts 5 and 6 work on the far side of the boundary, where filters are arithmetic and the plane has rolled into a circle — a circle that is finite precisely because the sample rate is.

And one number ran through all of it. A 5 kHz tone at 8 kHz folds to 3. ζ = 0.6 gives √(1 − ζ²) = 0.8. The point 0.6 + 0.8j sits exactly on the unit circle at 53.13°. The same triangle that was an impedance in Circuit Analysis and a pole pair in Linear Systems has been here the whole time, which is the strongest evidence this compilation can offer that these are not seventeen subjects.
