Sampling and Aliasing

Electronics Part 6 gave you the rule: sample faster than twice the highest frequency. This part is where that rule comes from, what exactly breaks when you disobey it, and why no amount of processing afterwards can repair the damage.

2.1 What a converter actually keeps

Sampling is simpler than it sounds. Every Ts seconds, measure the signal and write the number down. Between measurements, keep nothing. The record is a list of numbers and the instants they were taken at, and every later stage — the digital filter, the file, the reconstruction — has access to that list and to nothing else.

The rate is fs = 1/Ts. The question this whole part answers is: when is that list enough to rebuild the signal, and when is it not?

2.2 The failure is total, not gradual

The plate above is worth more than any sentence here, so slide it before reading on. At 3 kHz sampled at 8 kHz, one wave passes through those dots. Push the signal past 4 kHz and a second wave appears that passes through every single one of them — not approximately, exactly.

That is the whole problem, and notice its shape. This is not a signal degraded by noise, where more care recovers more of it. Two different inputs have produced identical outputs, and the converter's output is all that exists downstream. Recovering which one was there is not difficult; it is impossible, in the way that recovering the second addend from a sum is impossible.

And it is not just one impostor

At 8 kHz sampling, a 3 kHz output could have come from 3 kHz — or from 5, 11, 13, 19, 21 kHz, and so on for ever, since every 8k ± 3 kHz lands in the same place. Sampling maps infinitely many input frequencies onto each output frequency. A many-to-one map has no inverse, and that is the formal reason aliasing cannot be undone rather than merely being hard to undo.

2.3 The theorem, and two names that get confused

Say them out loud once

The Nyquist frequency is fs/2 and belongs to the sampler. The Nyquist rate is 2fmax and belongs to the signal. A question that says “sampled at twice the Nyquist frequency” is not the same as one that says “sampled at twice the Nyquist rate”, and the difference is a factor of two in the answer.

2.4 Why the number is two

The time picture shows that aliasing happens. It does not explain why the threshold is exactly twice. For that you need the spectrum, and the reason is almost embarrassingly geometric.

Sampling places a copy of the entire spectrum at every multiple of fs. That is the one fact to carry. The original band occupies −B to +B. The nearest copy is centred at fs, so it reaches down to fs − B. They stay clear of each other exactly when

fs − B > B ⟺ fs > 2B

So the two is not a rule of thumb about needing a couple of points per cycle. It is the condition for two copies of a band of width B to sit side by side without touching. And it explains the strictness: at exactly fs = 2B the copies meet at B, leaving no gap, and separating them would need a filter with infinitely steep sides.

Why 44.1 kHz and not 40

Audio is taken to run to 20 kHz, so the Nyquist rate is 40 kHz. CDs sample at 44.1 kHz. The extra 4.1 kHz is guard band — room for a real anti-alias filter to fall from passband to stopband. Every practical system oversamples, and the amount it oversamples by is set by how sharp a filter you are willing to build. That trade is Part 4.

2.5 Working out where a tone lands

The arithmetic is small: subtract the nearest multiple of fs and take the size of what is left. Equivalently — and this is easier to picture — fold the frequency axis at every multiple of fs/2 and see where the tone comes to rest.

Worked · an interferer that lands inside the band

A telephone channel carries speech to 3.4 kHz and is sampled at 8 kHz. A nearby switching supply injects a 5 kHz tone ahead of the converter. Where does it appear, and does it matter?

  1. Nyquist frequency. fN = 4 kHz. The speech at 3.4 kHz is below it and is safe.
  2. The interferer is not. 5 kHz is above 4 kHz, so it will fold. The nearest multiple of fs is 8 kHz: falias = |5 − 8| = 3 kHz
  3. It lands at 3 kHz — not merely inside the sampled band, but inside the speech band, below the 3.4 kHz edge.
  4. So it matters completely. A 3 kHz tone is an ordinary speech frequency. After sampling there is nothing to distinguish this one from a real one, and no filter — analog or digital — placed after the converter can remove it without also removing real speech at 3 kHz.

Note the numbers, as they will keep coming back: Nyquist frequency 4, tone 5, alias 3 — the same triangle this compilation has followed since Mathematics Part 1. Note also what the fix has to be. The interferer must be removed before the sampler, by an analog filter, because afterwards it is no longer separable from the signal. That filter is Parts 3 and 4.

Worked · a rate below everything in the signal

A 12 kHz sinusoid is sampled at 5 kHz. What frequency is recorded?

  1. Find the nearest multiple of fs. The multiples are 5, 10, 15, … and 12 is nearest to 10.
  2. falias = |12 − 10| = 2 kHz
  3. Check it is in range. The answer must lie between 0 and fs/2 = 2.5 kHz, and 2 kHz does. If your answer exceeds fs/2 you picked the wrong multiple — that check catches almost every arithmetic slip in this topic.
  4. 2 kHz. The record shows a clean 2 kHz sinusoid, with nothing anywhere in it to suggest the input was six times faster.

2.6 The filter comes first

Everything above forces one conclusion, and it is the most practically important sentence in this module.

The anti-alias filter goes before the converter, never after. Its job is to guarantee that fmax is what you believe it is, by removing everything above fs/2 while the signal is still analog and its components are still separable. Once sampling has folded a stray tone into the band, the tone and the signal are the same numbers, and no later stage has anything left to work with.

This is why Part 1 insisted that a signal which has not been band-limited has no fmax at all, and so has no honest sample rate. Real signals always carry something above the band you care about — noise if nothing else — so in practice the filter does not merely protect the sample rate, it creates the fmax that justifies it.

You have seen aliasing outside a laboratory. Wheels that turn backwards in films are sampled at the frame rate; a strobe that appears to freeze a spinning shaft is doing the same arithmetic deliberately. In each case the observation is perfectly consistent with a slower motion, and nothing in the record can distinguish the two.

2.7 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • fs > 2 fmax, strictly. Equality leaves the copies touching and the amplitude unrecoverable.
  • Nyquist frequency = fs/2, a property of the sampler. Nyquist rate = 2fmax, a property of the signal. Do not swap them.
  • falias = |f − k·fs| for the nearest k. The answer always lands between 0 and fs/2 — use that as a check.
  • Sampling copies the spectrum to every multiple of fs. Overlap of those copies is aliasing.
  • Infinitely many inputs share one alias, so the map is many-to-one and has no inverse. Aliasing is permanent.
  • Anti-alias filter goes before the converter. Afterwards there is nothing left to separate.
  • Practical systems oversample for guard band — 44.1 kHz for a 20 kHz band, not 40.

Part 1 said a signal has a highest frequency. Part 2 says that number and the sample rate are in a race, and there is a single line between working perfectly and failing completely. Below fs/2, reconstruction is exact — not approximate, exact. Above it, the information is gone — not degraded, gone.

The rest of this module follows from that. Parts 3 and 4 build the analog filter that keeps you on the right side of the line and quantify what it costs to build a sharp one. Parts 5 and 6 work on the samples once you are safely across it, where the natural frequency variable is no longer hertz but fraction of the sample rate — because after sampling, fs/2 is the only edge the numbers know about.
