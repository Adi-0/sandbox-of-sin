Digital Filters and Difference Equations

Part 4 argued that the sharp filtering should happen after the converter, where a pole costs a multiply instead of a precision component. This part is what that looks like: a filter written as one line of arithmetic, run once per sample.

5.1 The whole idea, in one equation

Once a signal is a list of numbers, filtering it means computing a new list from the old one. Every linear filter you will meet does it the same way: each output is a weighted sum of recent inputs and recent outputs.

Two things on that plate are worth naming. First, the trade is not avoidable: every setting that reduces the noise also slows the edge, and no choice of coefficients separates them — a filter that reacts instantly to a step must also react instantly to a noise spike, because it cannot tell them apart. Second, the DC gain must be 1 for a smoother, and the plate shows the check: for the moving average, L coefficients of 1/L sum to one.

5.2 Finite or infinite: hit it with a single one

Put in a signal that is 1 at n = 0 and zero everywhere else, and what comes out is the impulse response, written h[n]. It is the most informative single test there is, because for a linear filter it is the filter — everything the filter will ever do to anything is encoded in it.

The last row is worth a moment. An IIR filter is the direct descendant of an analog one: feedback, poles, the same stability worry. An FIR filter has no analog counterpart at all — you cannot build a circuit whose impulse response stops dead after eight samples. It is a structure that only exists because the signal became numbers.

Why linear phase is worth having

Part 4 mentioned Bessel filters, which give up steepness to keep phase linear so that a pulse keeps its shape. A symmetric FIR gets exactly linear phase for free, at any steepness, because symmetry forces every frequency to be delayed by the same (L−1)/2 samples. No analog filter can do that — it is one of the genuine advantages of filtering after the converter rather than before it.

5.3 Convolution is just that sum, named

Because the impulse response is the filter, the output for any input can be written using it alone:

y[n] = Σ h[k] x[n−k]

This is convolution, and for an FIR it is literally the difference equation with h in place of b. Read it as a sentence: every input sample sets off a copy of the impulse response, scaled by that sample, and the output is all those copies added up. Nothing more mysterious than that is going on.

Doing one by hand, which the exam may ask

With h = {1, 2, 1} and x = {1, 3, 2}, slide and add: y[0] = 1·1 = 1; y[1] = 1·3 + 2·1 = 5; y[2] = 1·2 + 2·3 + 1·1 = 9; y[3] = 2·2 + 1·3 = 7; y[4] = 1·2 = 2. So y = {1, 5, 9, 7, 2}. Two useful checks: the output length is Lx + Lh − 1 = 3 + 3 − 1 = 5, and the sum of the output equals the product of the two sums, (1+2+1)(1+3+2) = 4 × 6 = 24 = 1+5+9+7+2. If either check fails you have dropped a term.

5.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • y[n] = Σ bk x[n−k] − Σ ak y[n−k]. The b's weight past inputs, the a's past outputs.
  • FIR = no feedback: impulse response is the coefficient list, stops, and is unconditionally stable.
  • IIR = feedback: response decays for ever, far cheaper for a sharp filter, and can be unstable.
  • DC gain = Σb / (1 + Σa), by setting every delay to nothing.
  • A symmetric FIR has exactly linear phase and delays everything by (L−1)/2 samples.
  • Convolution: y[n] = Σ h[k]x[n−k]. Output length Lx + Lh − 1, and the output sums to the product of the input sums.
  • An L-point moving average has nulls at multiples of fs/L — it is a filter, not just an average.

A digital filter is a weighted sum, and the only structural question is whether the sum reaches back into its own output. That one question decides stability, efficiency, phase behaviour and whether an analog counterpart even exists.

What Part 5 cannot yet do is design one. You can run a difference equation and see what it does, but there is no way yet to look at a set of coefficients and say where the filter's corner is, how sharp it is, or — for an IIR — whether it is stable at all, short of running it and watching. Part 6 supplies the missing tool. It does for difference equations exactly what the Laplace transform did for differential equations in Linear Systems Part 3: turns them into algebra, and turns the stability question into a question about where some points sit on a plane.
