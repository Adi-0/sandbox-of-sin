The Z-Transform and the Unit Circle

Part 5 could run a difference equation but not design one, and could not answer “is this stable?” except by running it and watching. This part supplies the missing tool, and it is the same tool Linear Systems used on differential equations — with the half-plane rolled up into a circle.

6.1 One symbol, one meaning: delay

Everything here follows from a single definition. z⁻¹ means one sample of delay. That is the whole intuition, and if you keep hold of it the rest is bookkeeping.

6.2 Difference equation to transfer function, by inspection

Take any difference equation, replace each delay by its power of z⁻¹, and collect. There is no step more clever than that.

Worked · a one-pole filter, completely characterised

Given y[n] = 0.6y[n−1] + 0.4x[n], find H(z), the pole, whether it is stable, the DC gain, and the gain at the Nyquist frequency.

  1. Transform every term. y[n−1] becomes z⁻¹Y(z): Y = 0.6z⁻¹Y + 0.4X
  2. Collect and divide. H(z) = 0.4z/(z − 0.6)
  3. The pole is at z = 0.6. Its magnitude is 0.6, which is less than 1, so it is inside the unit circle: stable.
  4. DC is z = 1. H(1) = 1 — unity, as a smoother should be.
  5. Nyquist is z = −1. H(−1) = 0.25 → −12.0 dB

Five facts from two lines of algebra, and notice that DC and Nyquist need no trigonometry at all — they are z = +1 and z = −1, so you just substitute a real number. Those two points are free on any digital filter question, and they are often enough to eliminate three of the four choices.

6.3 Stability is a distance from the origin

Linear Systems asked whether a pole was to the left of the imaginary axis. The mapping z = e^{sT} answers the same question in the new plane: since |z| = e^{σT}, a negative σ gives a magnitude below 1. The left half-plane rolls up into the unit disc.

6.4 The frequency response is a lap of the circle

Linear Systems Part 5 got a frequency response by walking s up the imaginary axis. Here you walk z around the unit circle, because that is where the imaginary axis went.

The plate draws the lines that make this computable by eye:

|H| = (product of distances to zeros) / (product of distances to poles)

Pass close to a pole and the response peaks. Pass through a zero and it vanishes. That is enough to sketch any of these responses without evaluating anything, and it is the same reasoning the s-plane used — the only change is that the path is now a circle rather than a straight line.

The cast, in its last appearance

The notch on that plate puts its zeros at 0.6 ± 0.8j. That point has magnitude exactly 1 and angle exactly 53.13° — the 3-4-5 triangle, normalised, landing precisely on the unit circle. Which is exactly what a notch needs: a zero on the circle is a response of exactly nothing, so the frequency is not attenuated but annihilated. The same triangle was an impedance in Circuit Analysis, a pole pair at −3 ± 4j in Linear Systems, a phase margin in Control, and an alias in Part 2 of this module. It has been the same triangle every time.

6.5 Why the plane is a circle at all

It is worth seeing where the circle comes from rather than accepting it. The map is z = e^{sT}, and it does two things at once:

In the s-planeBecomes, in the z-plane
left half-plane, σ < 0inside the unit circle
the jω axis, σ = 0the unit circle itself
right half-plane, σ > 0outside the unit circle
the origin, s = 0z = 1 — DC
ω = ±π/T, the Nyquist frequencyz = −1

The last two rows explain the wrap. As ω climbs the imaginary axis, the angle of z is ωT, so at the Nyquist frequency z has gone exactly half way round and reached −1. Keep climbing and it comes back round to where it started. That wrapping is aliasing, seen from a third direction — the same fact Part 2 drew as overlapping spectra and Part 2's other plate drew as an impostor sinusoid.

6.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • z⁻¹ is one sample of delay. Everything else follows.
  • Z{x[n−k]} = z⁻ᵏX(z) — the only property most questions need.
  • H(z) is read straight off the difference equation: b's on top, a's underneath.
  • Stable ⇔ every pole strictly inside the unit circle, |z| < 1.
  • Radius sets decay, angle sets frequency. A pole at r∠θ gives rⁿ times an oscillation at θ rad/sample.
  • An FIR has all its poles at the origin, which is why it is unconditionally stable.
  • DC is z = +1, Nyquist is z = −1. Both need only real arithmetic and are free marks.
  • Ω = 2πf/fs; a full lap of the circle is the whole sample rate.
  • |H| = product of zero distances ÷ product of pole distances, from the point on the circle.
  • s to z is z = e^{sT}: left half-plane → inside, jω axis → the circle.

The z-transform is the Laplace transform with a discrete alphabet. One symbol means delay, difference equations become ratios of polynomials, and the stability question that was “which side of a line?” becomes “inside or outside a circle?”. Nothing conceptual is new — the half-plane has simply been rolled up, and it was sampling that rolled it.

That is the whole module in one image, in fact. The circle is finite because the sample rate is finite, and going round it once and arriving back where you started is aliasing. Part 7 puts every piece of this together on one signal chain and then runs a mixed set under exam conditions.
