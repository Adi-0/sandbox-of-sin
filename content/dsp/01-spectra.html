Signals and Spectra

Nothing in this module works until the frequency axis is a real place rather than a figure of speech. This part builds it: a signal is a sum of sinusoids, the list of their sizes is its spectrum, and the highest one that matters is the single number sampling and filtering both depend on.

1.1 The same signal, written twice

You already have two descriptions of an AC quantity. A waveform is value against time. A phasor is size and angle at one frequency. Circuit Analysis Part 6 could use phasors because there was only ever one frequency in the room, so a single arrow said everything.

Real signals are not that tidy. A square wave, a voice, a switching converter's output — none is a single sinusoid. The fix is not to abandon the phasor but to use more than one: keep the same idea and allow a whole list of frequencies, each with its own amplitude. That list is the spectrum, and the claim that it is always enough is Fourier's.

Why bother, when the waveform is right there

Because most of the questions in this module are invisible in the time domain and obvious in the frequency domain. Will this filter remove the interference? — a filter does one thing to each frequency, so in the spectrum it is a multiplication and in time it is an integral. Is this sample rate fast enough? — the answer depends on one number, the highest frequency present, and the spectrum is where that number is written down. Changing description does not change the signal; it changes which questions are easy.

1.2 The recipe

Any periodic signal with period T is a sum of sinusoids at whole-number multiples of its own repetition rate f₀ = 1/T. Multiples, not arbitrary frequencies — and that is forced, not chosen: any component at a non-multiple would fail to repeat every T, and so would the sum.

The third row deserves a second look, because it is the whole mechanism. Multiplying by cos(2πkf₀t) and averaging over a period gives zero for every component except the one at that exact frequency. Different harmonics are orthogonal — they do not interfere with each other's measurement. That is why a spectrum is well-defined at all, and in a moment it is also why powers add.

1.3 How fast the harmonics die is the whole story

You will not be asked to integrate for these on the exam. You will be asked to recognise them, and what is worth carrying is not the constant out front but the exponent.

The plate above lets you watch what that exponent buys. On the square wave, 25 harmonics still leaves a visible ripple. On the triangle, five terms are already indistinguishable from the real thing at drawing accuracy.

The ripple at the jump never leaves

Add terms to the square wave and the overshoot beside each edge gets narrower but not shorter. It settles at about 9% of the jump height and stays there for ever. This is Gibbs’ phenomenon, and it is not a numerical fault — it is what a sum of continuous functions does when asked to be discontinuous. It matters here for one reason: it is the proof that a band-limited copy of a discontinuous signal is never the signal, no matter how much band you give it.

1.4 Power lives in the spectrum too

Because harmonics are orthogonal, their powers add — the cross terms integrate away. So the RMS of a signal can be assembled from its spectrum without ever drawing the waveform, and this is the most directly examinable thing in this part.

Worked · the RMS of a square wave, from its spectrum alone

A square wave alternates between +1 V and −1 V. Find its RMS value using only the harmonic amplitudes, and check the answer against what you already know.

  1. The amplitudes. Odd harmonics only, at Ak = 4/πk. So the RMS of the kth is Ak/√2.
  2. Add the powers. Xrms² = (8/π²) Σ 1/k² over odd k
  3. The sum is a known one: Σ 1/k² = π²/8 over odd k. Substituting, Xrms² = 1
  4. So the RMS is 1 V — which is obviously right, because a square wave is at |1 V| the entire time. The spectrum reproduces it exactly.

The point is not the answer, which you could have written down. It is that the two descriptions agree to the last digit, and that the fundamental alone contributes 0.81 of it — the 81% on the plate above. Keep the fundamental and the third harmonic and you have 90% of a square wave's power in two sinusoids. That is what makes the next section reasonable rather than reckless.

1.5 Bandwidth, and the number Part 2 needs

Bandwidth is the range of frequencies a signal actually occupies. For a periodic wave in principle that range is infinite — the harmonics never stop. In practice they become negligible, and past some point removing them changes nothing you can measure. The frequency where you draw that line is the signal's bandwidth, and drawing it is called band-limiting.

Two things on that plate are worth being deliberate about. First, the corners round off before anything else does, every time — sharp edges are stored in the high harmonics, so they are the first thing any band limit takes. Second, and more important for what follows: once you have committed to a cutoff, the signal has a highest frequency. It is a definite number. And a signal with a definite highest frequency has a definite minimum honest sample rate.

A signal has two equivalent descriptions and you get to choose. In time it is a value at each instant; in frequency it is an amplitude at each harmonic. Fourier's integrals convert between them, the handbook holds those integrals, and for the three standard waves you should recognise the answer rather than derive it: a jump gives 1/k, a corner gives 1/k².

What this part exists to establish is one number. fmax — the highest frequency a signal contains. Everything in Part 2 is about what happens when you sample a signal faster or slower than twice that number, and everything in Parts 3 and 4 is about building the filter that guarantees fmax is what you think it is. A signal that has not been band-limited does not have an fmax, and cannot be sampled correctly at any rate at all — which is why the filter is not an accessory.

1.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • A periodic signal contains only integer multiples of f₀ = 1/T.
  • Jump ⇒ harmonics fall as 1/k. Corner ⇒ 1/k². Smoother signal, narrower spectrum.
  • Square and triangle: odd harmonics only. Sawtooth: all of them.
  • RMS from a spectrum: Xrms² = X₀² + Σ(Ak/√2)² — powers add, amplitudes do not.
  • THD = √(Σ Ak²)/A₁, everything but the fundamental over the fundamental.
  • A square wave holds 81% of its power in the fundamental, 90% by the third harmonic, and has THD = 48.3%.
  • Gibbs: truncating a series overshoots a jump by ~9% of the jump, and adding terms narrows the ripple without lowering it.
  • Bandwidth = the highest frequency that matters, and it is the input to every sampling question.
