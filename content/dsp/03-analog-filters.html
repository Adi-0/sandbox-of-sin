Analog Filters

Part 2 ended with an obligation: something has to remove everything above fs/2 before the converter sees it. This part builds that something — and finds that the obvious first attempt falls about thirty-five decibels short.

3.1 What a filter is

A filter does one thing to each frequency: multiplies it by a gain and shifts its phase. Nothing more. That is the entire content of H(jω), which Linear Systems Part 4 introduced as a transfer function and Part 5 taught you to sketch.

What makes filtering powerful is exactly what Part 1 set up: since a signal is a collection of frequencies, multiplying each one by a chosen number lets you keep some and discard others. In the frequency domain a filter is a multiplication. In the time domain the same operation is an integral, which is why the frequency description is the one everybody uses.

The whole vocabulary, once

Passband — the frequencies you keep. Stopband — the frequencies you reject. Transition band — the gap between them, which is never zero. Cutoff — the frequency marking the passband edge, and by universal convention it is the −3 dB point, which Linear Systems Part 5 established as the half-power point. Every filter question is about those four words and the trade between them.

3.2 There are only four shapes

Reactance is all you need. A capacitor's reactance falls as frequency rises; an inductor's rises. Combine that with whether the component sits in the signal path or shunts it to ground, and every filter falls out.

3.3 Where the corner is

Worked · the anti-alias filter Part 2 demanded

The telephone channel again: speech to 3.4 kHz, sampled at 8 kHz, with a 5 kHz interferer that folds to 3 kHz. Design a simple RC low-pass at fc = 3.4 kHz and check whether it solves the problem.

  1. Pick components. With R = 4.7 kΩ, C = 9.96 nF so a standard 10 nF part gives fc = 3.39 kHz — close enough.
  2. How much does it reject at 5 kHz? For a single RC section, |H| = 0.562
  3. In decibels: −5.0 dB.
  4. So it does not solve the problem. The interferer arrives at 56% of its original size and still folds to 3 kHz, straight into the speech band. Five decibels is not a filter, it is a gesture.

The reason is visible on plate 106: 5 kHz is only 1.47 times the cutoff, and a first-order section has barely begun to roll off by then. It needs a decade to deliver 20 dB, and it has been given less than a fifth of one. Getting real rejection over such a short frequency span is the entire problem of Part 4, and the answer for this arrangement is uncomfortable: about twelve poles. Part 4 also shows the cheaper way out, which is to sample faster and buy the filter some room.

3.4 Two sections are not one section squared

The obvious response to “one section is not steep enough” is to use two. It is the right instinct and it does not work the obvious way.

Wire two identical RC sections directly together and the second one loads the first: it draws current through the first resistor, so the first section no longer sees the open circuit its formula assumed. The result is

H = 1/(1 + 3ju − u²), not 1/(1 + ju)²

The whole difference is the 3 where you expected a 2, and it is enough to drag the overall corner down to 0.374 fc instead of the predicted 0.644 — a factor of 1.72. The passband droops noticeably earlier than any calculation that multiplied the two responses would suggest.

What the op-amp in an active filter is really for

Put a buffer between the sections and the loading disappears, because a voltage follower presents a near-infinite input impedance to the stage before it and a near-zero output impedance to the stage after. Now the responses genuinely multiply. This is the main reason active filters exist — not gain, which you often do not need, but isolation. It is also why they need inductors far less often: an op-amp can synthesise the resonant behaviour of an LC pair from resistors and capacitors alone, which matters because inductors are bulky, lossy and expensive, and capacitors are none of those things.

Cascading moves the corner even when it works

Even buffered, two sections do not keep the corner at fc. At fc each contributes −3 dB, so the pair delivers −6 dB, and the −3 dB point of the whole thing has moved down to 0.644 fc. Stacking identical sections always pulls the overall cutoff inward, and the more you stack the further it moves. Designing a higher-order filter is therefore not a matter of repeating one design — it is a matter of choosing all the poles together, which is what Butterworth does in Part 4.

3.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Four shapes, each a sentence about reactance: series R shunt C is low-pass; series C shunt R is high-pass; series LC is band-pass; shunt LC is band-stop.
  • fc = 1/(2πRC) and fc = R/(2πL). Watch the 2π — ω = 1/RC has none.
  • f₀ = 1/(2π√(LC)), BW = f₀/Q, and f₀ = √(f₁f₂) — a geometric mean.
  • Cutoff means −3 dB: half power, 0.707 of the amplitude, 45° of phase for a first-order section.
  • A first-order section gives 20 dB per decade and nothing steeper.
  • Cascading passive sections does not multiply their responses — loading changes the answer. Two direct RC sections give 1/(1 + 3ju − u²).
  • An active filter's op-amp is there for isolation, so that stages do multiply.
  • Even buffered, stacking sections pulls the overall corner inward (0.644 fc for two).

Filtering is multiplication in the frequency domain, and there are only four useful shapes to multiply by. The corner of a first-order section is where the resistance and the reactance are equal in magnitude, which is all that 1/(2πRC) says.

What this part establishes for the rest of the module is a limit. One section buys 20 dB per decade, and the anti-alias filter that Part 2 demanded needed roughly 35 dB more than that over less than a fifth of a decade. You cannot get there by repeating a section, both because loading spoils the arithmetic and because even ideal stacking moves the corner. Part 4 is about getting a genuinely steep filter, what it costs, and the trade every designer actually makes instead — sampling faster so the filter has more room to work in.
