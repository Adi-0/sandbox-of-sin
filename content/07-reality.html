Neuromorphic computing has been about to arrive for roughly forty years. It is worth being precise about why, because the reason is not that the physics does not work. Parts 1 through 6 were all true. The reason is that the thing which makes these machines efficient — spikes — is also the thing that makes them nearly impossible to train with the tool that works on everything else.

The derivative that isn't there

Modern machine learning runs on one idea: nudge every parameter in the direction that reduces the error, using the chain rule to work out which direction that is. The chain rule needs derivatives. So: what is the derivative of a spike?

A spike is a step. Below threshold, output zero. Above threshold, output one. The derivative of that function is zero everywhere, and undefined at the single point where it jumps. Backpropagation multiplies gradients along a chain, and a zero anywhere in the chain annihilates everything behind it. Every gradient arrives as zero. No learning happens at all.

The fix is a shrug and a lie. During the backward pass, pretend the spike function was something smooth. Keep the real, hard step for the forward pass — you still want actual spikes and actual sparsity — but when the gradient comes back through, substitute the derivative of a smooth stand-in. This is the surrogate gradient, and essentially every trained spiking network you will read about uses one.

Figure 16 shows the fiction. The dashed grey line is the truth: zero, everywhere it matters. The teal curve is the substitute, and the slider sets how wide it is.

Slide it sharp. Now only neurons sitting within a hair of threshold receive any gradient, the signal is accurate but almost nothing learns, and training stalls. Slide it broad. Now a neuron that never came within 15 mV of firing gets credited for the network's error, which is not true, and training becomes unstable and slow to converge.

Nobody has a principled way to choose. People tune it, the way people tune learning rates — which is to say, empirically, per problem, with a certain amount of superstition. It works well enough to be the standard method, and it is unsatisfying enough that better ones are an active research area.

The main alternative avoids the problem rather than solving it. Train an ordinary network with ordinary backpropagation, then convert it: replace each artificial neuron's continuous output with a spiking neuron whose firing rate matches. This works, and it inherits everything the conventional training pipeline is good at. The cost is that it commits you to rate coding — from Part 3, the slow one — so a converted network typically needs a hundred timesteps per inference to let its rates settle, which spends much of the sparsity advantage you converted in order to get.

The scorecard

With that on the table, here is where things honestly stand.

Event cameras are a genuine, shipping success. They are sold by Sony and Prophesee, deployed in industrial inspection, high-speed tracking and eye tracking. Microsecond latency, dynamic range beyond 120 dB, no motion blur, and data rates proportional to activity. This is the clearest win in the field, and note what it is: sensing, at the edge, where the world genuinely is quiet most of the time.

Always-on edge inference is the strongest processor case. Keyword spotting, vibration monitoring, anomaly detection on a sensor — workloads where a device must listen continuously for something that rarely happens, on a battery, for a year. This is precisely the shape Figure 2 described. Reported energy improvements on such workloads run from single digits to a few orders of magnitude depending on how sparse the task really is, which is a wide range because the workloads vary more than the chips do.

Fast closed-loop control is promising and not yet settled. Drones and robot arms that must react within milliseconds are a natural fit for latency coding and event sensing. There are compelling demonstrations. There is not yet a product category.

Large-scale training and large language models are not the target and will not be. Nothing in this guide helps with a dense matrix multiply over billions of parameters. That workload is bottlenecked on memory bandwidth for weights that are all needed every time — there is no sparsity to exploit, so the central bet of Part 1 does not pay. Anyone selling neuromorphic hardware as an LLM accelerator is describing a different machine than the one you have just learned about.

And the unglamorous blocker, which matters more than any of the above: tooling. A researcher with PyTorch has a decade of accumulated infrastructure. A researcher with a Loihi has Lava, a much younger stack, a much smaller community, and often a waiting list for hardware. Frameworks like snnTorch, Norse and SpikingJelly have narrowed this gap considerably by letting you train spiking networks inside PyTorch — but the gap is still the main thing standing between the field and wider adoption, and it is not a physics problem.

You are choosing hardware for a battery-powered sensor that must detect a rare acoustic event, continuously, for two years. Which fact from this guide decides it?
The second. Everything else is secondary to the fact that the event is rare — that is what makes the ratio in Figure 14 large. Ask "how quiet is this workload?" first. If the answer is "not very", no amount of clever silicon will save the argument, and biological realism has never once appeared on a purchase order.

Synthesis: how few ideas were actually doing the work

Look back at what you have used. There were four ideas, and everything else was a consequence.

A leaky bucket. One state variable, one leak, one threshold. It gave you a memory with a deadline (Part 2), which made it a coincidence detector (Part 3), which made it a decision circuit when you wired several together with inhibition (Part 4).

An identical, all-or-none event. Because spikes carry no magnitude, information moved into their timing — which gave you latency coding, and made a synapse's job so simple that three local numbers were enough to learn (Part 5), and made the wire protocol nothing more than an address (Part 6).

The 20 millisecond time constant. The same number set the memory of the neuron, the width of the coincidence window, the transmission cliff, and the shape of the learning rule. It was never four facts. It was one fact, seen from four directions.

Sparsity. Silence is free only if you have built a machine where silence is free. That is the entire architectural argument, and its size is one division: how often the clock would have ticked, over how often something actually happened.

Where to go next

Five directions, each tied to a part you have just read.

Train one yourself. snnTorch, Norse or SpikingJelly put surrogate-gradient training inside PyTorch, so the loop is familiar. Start with N-MNIST or the Spiking Heidelberg Digits — both are event-based datasets, so you are not converting frames into spikes and pretending. This is Part 7 made practical, and you will meet the β slider within an hour.

Reservoir computing and liquid state machines. Wire a few hundred neurons together at random, do not train them at all, and read the resulting activity with a simple linear layer. It works startlingly well on temporal tasks, and it exists because the circuits in Part 4 already do something useful before anyone tunes them.

Event-based vision. Prophesee's Metavision SDK and the DSEC and DVS-Gesture datasets are the way into the one part of this field with real customers. Everything in Part 6's Figure 15 applies directly.

The biology you were spared. Dale's law, excitatory–inhibitory balance, homeostatic plasticity, dendritic computation. Each is a place where the caricature in Part 2 was flagged as a caricature, and each turns out to matter for stability once networks get large.

Analogue in-memory computing. If Figure 13 is the part that stuck, this is a whole field on its own, and it is not limited to spiking networks — the same crossbars are being built for conventional deep learning, where the device non-idealities in that Honest Note are the entire research programme.

Then go to Part 8 and build something. The bench has every parameter from this guide on a slider, and the fastest way to find out whether you actually understood the transmission cliff is to try to build a relay chain that survives it.

Spiking hardware is efficient for the same reason it is hard to train: an all-or-none event has no derivative. Surrogate gradients paper over that, imperfectly, and remain the standard method.

The field's real wins are where the world is genuinely quiet — event cameras, always-on edge sensing. The first question about any neuromorphic proposal is not "how fast?" but "how sparse?"
