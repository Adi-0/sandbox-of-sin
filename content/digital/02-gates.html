Boolean Logic and Gates

Mathematics Part 8 already built the algebra. This part gives it hardware — and then opens one gate to show that the hardware is Electronics Part 3's transistors, arranged so that exactly one half ever conducts.

2.1 The algebra you already have

Boolean algebra was Part 8 of the Mathematics module: two values, three operators, and a set of identities. Nothing about it changes here, so this is a recap rather than a derivation.

2.2 The gates

Reading a bubble

Every small circle on a logic symbol means exactly one thing: invert here. A NAND is an AND with a bubble on its output; an OR with bubbles on both inputs and its output is also a NAND. Those two drawings are the same circuit, and De Morgan is the statement that they are. Fluency with bubbles is worth more on this exam than the ability to quote the theorem, because schematics are drawn in whichever form makes the signal flow readable.

2.3 Universal gates

NAND and NOR are each universal: any Boolean function whatever can be built from copies of one of them alone. The proof is short — build the three basic operations and you are done:

  • NOT — tie both NAND inputs together. ¬(A·A) = ¬A
  • AND — a NAND followed by that inverter.
  • OR — invert both inputs, then NAND them. That is De Morgan read backwards.

This is not a curiosity. It is why real silicon is mostly NAND, and the reason becomes visible only when you open the gate.

2.4 What a gate actually is

Why NAND is the cheap one

A CMOS NAND is four transistors. A CMOS AND is six — because there is no such thing as an AND in CMOS; you build a NAND and bolt an inverter onto it. The same holds for NOR against OR. Inverting gates are the natural products of the technology and non-inverting ones are the expensive special case, which is exactly backwards from how the symbols are usually taught. It is also why a designer who can read bubbles fluently can save real silicon.

2.5 Combinational building blocks

Above the gate level, a handful of standard blocks appear constantly and are worth recognising by name:

  • Multiplexer — 2ⁿ data inputs, n select lines, one output. "Choose one of these."
  • Decoder — n inputs, 2ⁿ outputs, exactly one active. "Which address is this?"
  • Encoder — the reverse; a priority encoder resolves ties by rank.
  • Half adder — sum is A ⊕ B, carry is A · B. Two gates.
  • Full adder — adds a carry in. Sum is A ⊕ B ⊕ Cin.
Trap · counting inputs and outputs

A multiplexer with 3 select lines has 8 data inputs and 1 output. A decoder with 3 inputs has 8 outputs. They are mirror images, and questions routinely give you one count and ask for the other. The select lines are the exponent, never the base.

2.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • De Morgan both ways, and how to read it off a bubbled symbol.
  • A + 1 = 1 and A + ¬A·B = A + B.
  • NAND and NOR are universal. NOT is a NAND with its inputs tied.
  • CMOS: pull-up and pull-down networks are complements; no static power; dynamic power goes as CV²f.
  • NAND is 4 transistors, AND is 6. Inverting gates are the cheap ones.
  • MUX: n selects → 2ⁿ inputs. Decoder: n inputs → 2ⁿ outputs.
  • Half adder: sum = XOR, carry = AND.

The gates are Mathematics Part 8's algebra given physical form, and the bubbles on their symbols are De Morgan made visible — the same function drawn two ways, chosen for readability rather than meaning.

Underneath, a gate is Electronics Part 3's MOSFETs in two complementary networks. That single arrangement explains why CMOS draws no current at rest, why its power bill goes as the square of the supply voltage, and why the inverting gates — the ones the symbols make look like afterthoughts — are the cheap primitives everything else is assembled from.
