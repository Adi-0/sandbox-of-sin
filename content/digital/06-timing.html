Timing and Hazards

Every part so far has assumed that logic is instantaneous and that a flip-flop samples at a point. Neither is true, and almost every digital failure mode in practice starts from the gap between those assumptions and the silicon.

6.2 The aperture

Setup and hold bracket the clock edge into a window, and the flip-flop's promise is conditional on the data not moving inside it. What happens when it does is the interesting part, and it is not simply "the wrong answer".

Metastability is a probability, not a bug

A flip-flop is two cross-coupled inverters with a pencil balanced on end between them. Push it early or late and it falls one way or the other. Push it at exactly the wrong moment and it stands there — and while the balance decays exponentially, there is no time by which it is guaranteed to have fallen. This is not a defect in a particular part; it is a property of any circuit that must decide between two states in bounded time, and no amount of engineering removes it. What engineering does is make the probability small enough to ignore.

Trap · which signals need synchronising

Any signal that changes without reference to your clock — a button, an interrupt line from another chip, anything from another oscillator, a reset released by hand. Not just slow ones, and not just mechanical ones: what matters is whether the two clocks are related, never how fast they are. Signals already synchronous with your clock do not need a synchroniser; they need timing analysis, which is §6.3.

6.3 How fast can it run?

Every path in a synchronous design starts at one flip-flop's clock edge and ends at another's data input, and must complete before the next edge. Writing that sentence as an inequality gives the whole of static timing analysis:

Worked · a maximum frequency, and why the answer is not the logic delay

A pipeline stage has t_cq = 3 ns, 18 ns of combinational logic, and t_su = 4 ns. How fast can it be clocked?

  1. Follow one path from edge to edge. The launching flip-flop takes 3 ns to put its new value out. The logic takes 18 ns to compute with it. The capturing flip-flop needs the answer steady 4 ns before its own edge.
  2. All three are on the same path, so they add: T_min = 25 ns
  3. Invert: f_max = 40 MHz

Answering 55.6 MHz means using only the logic delay; answering 47.6 MHz means forgetting the setup time. Both appear as distractors because both are what people actually do. The clock period pays for the flip-flops as well as the logic, which is also why pipelining stops helping once the stages get short enough that tcq + tsu dominates.

6.4 Hazards

Between clock edges, combinational logic is free to do whatever the delays make it do — including producing an output the truth table says is impossible.

Where the hazard is, on the map

A static-1 hazard lives wherever two adjacent groups touch without overlapping. Moving between those two cells changes one input, the output hands over from one product term to the other, and if the two paths have unequal delay it drops in between. So the hazard is visible on the Karnaugh map before anything is built: look for a pair of adjacent 1-cells covered by different groups and no group covering both. Draw the group that covers both — it is redundant by construction, which is exactly why Part 3's minimisation deleted it, and it is what removes the glitch.

When a glitch does not matter, and when it does

Into a clocked input, a glitch is invisible: the flip-flop looks only at the edge, by which time the logic has settled. That is the usual case, and it is why synchronous design is worth its overhead. A glitch becomes a fault the moment something level-sensitive is watching: a latch enable, an asynchronous preset or clear, or — worst — a clock derived from gates. Never drive a clock from combinational logic is a rule with exactly this behind it.

6.5 Races

A race is the sequential relative of a hazard. When a transition asks two state flip-flops to change together and one is faster, the machine passes through a state nobody drew — and a critical race is one where it then settles somewhere wrong.

  • The synchronous defence is that every flip-flop is clocked by the same edge, so the intermediate patterns are never sampled. This is most of what synchronous design is for.
  • The assignment defence is Gray coding: if adjacent states differ in one bit, there is nothing to race. Part 5 noted this helps machines that walk their states in order — this is the reason why.
  • Asynchronous designs have neither, which is why they are hard, and why the exam's sequential logic is synchronous throughout.

6.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • f_max = 1/(t_cq + t_logic + t_su). All three terms.
  • Hold check: t_cq + t_logic,min ≥ t_h. No clock period — slowing the clock cannot fix it.
  • Data changing inside the setup/hold window ⇒ metastable, for an unbounded time.
  • Fix: two flip-flops in series, on every asynchronous input. MTBF goes as e^(t_r/τ).
  • Static-1 dips to 0; static-0 pulses to 1; dynamic bounces three times.
  • Remove a static hazard with the redundant consensus term — the one minimisation deleted.
  • Glitches are harmless into clocked inputs and dangerous into level-sensitive ones. Never gate a clock.
  • Races: two state bits changing at once. Gray coding removes them.

Gates take time, and every idea in this part is a consequence. Delays add along a path, so the slowest path sets the clock rate — and the budget pays for the flip-flops at both ends, not just the logic between them. Delays differ between paths, so an output can glitch while the truth table insists it cannot, and the cure is the redundant term minimisation was designed to remove.

The deepest of the three is metastability, because it is not a delay problem at all: it is what happens when a circuit is asked to decide something that has not yet finished happening. There is no fix, only an exponent — and the practical consequence is that two flip-flops on an asynchronous input is not caution, it is arithmetic. That is also a fair summary of why the whole of digital design is synchronous: it is the discipline that turns timing from something you reason about everywhere into something you check in one place.
