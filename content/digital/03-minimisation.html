Minimisation

Any truth table can be written down as an expression mechanically. Making that expression small is a different problem, and the Karnaugh map solves it by turning algebra into geometry.

3.1 From table to expression, mechanically

Two canonical forms exist, and both are read straight off a truth table with no thought at all:

Trap · the complementing rule flips between forms

In SOP, a variable that is 0 in the row appears complemented. In POS, a variable that is 1 in the row appears complemented. The rules are opposite, and writing the SOP rule onto a POS problem is the single most common error in this topic. If it helps: POS terms describe the rows you want to exclude, so everything reverses.

3.2 The map

Boolean algebra simplifies by AB + A¬B = A — two terms that differ in exactly one variable collapse, and that variable disappears. Finding those pairs by staring at an expression is unreliable. Arranging the truth table so that the pairs are physically adjacent makes them impossible to miss, and that is all a Karnaugh map is.

The arrangement is Gray code — 00, 01, 11, 10 — introduced in Part 1. Neighbouring cells differ in exactly one bit, so every adjacency is a legal simplification.

Overlapping groups are correct

Groups may overlap freely, because A + A = A — covering a cell twice costs nothing. What you are minimising is the number of groups and their size, not the tidiness of the picture. Always take the largest legal group, even if it overlaps one you have already drawn. The only rule is that every 1 must end up inside at least one group, and no group may contain a 0.

Worked · the corners

Minimise F = Σm(0, 2, 8, 10).

  1. Canonically, that is four four-literal terms: 16 literals in total
  2. On the map those four land in the four corners. Because the edges wrap, they are mutually adjacent and form a single group of four.
  3. A group of 4 in a 4-variable map leaves 2 literals. Which two? The ones that are constant across all four cells: B is 0 throughout and D is 0 throughout. F = ¬B·¬D

Sixteen literals down to two. Read the plate's outline to see the group; the wrap is the whole trick, and a map drawn without it would have found four groups of one.

3.3 Where the map stops

Two honest limits, both worth knowing before exam day:

  • Above five variables it becomes unusable. Six variables is 64 cells across four maps, and adjacency stops being visual. Real tools use Quine-McCluskey or heuristics; the exam stays at three or four.
  • Some functions do not simplify at all. Parity is a checkerboard — no two on-cells adjacent — so the minimal SOP is as long as the canonical one. Load "Odd parity" on the plate to see it. The right answer there is not a sum of products; it is a tree of XOR gates.

3.4 The expression, as silicon

Having minimised, what do you build it with? Below a certain size, gates. Above it, a programmable logic device — and the commonest kind is a sum-of-products array, which is why minimisation and implementation are the same conversation.

Why the ROM entry is interesting

A ROM's AND array is a full decoder, so it produces every one of the 2ⁿ minterms whether you want them or not. That makes it able to implement any function of its inputs with no minimisation whatever — you just store the truth table. It is the brute-force end of the same spectrum the K-map sits at, and an FPGA's lookup table is exactly the same idea shrunk to four or six inputs.

3.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • SOP: one AND term per 1-row, variable complemented when it is 0. POS reverses that rule.
  • K-map rows and columns are in Gray code, and the edges wrap.
  • Groups are powers of two; a group of size 2ᵏ leaves n − k literals.
  • Take the largest groups, and let them overlap.
  • Don't-cares may be 1 or 0 — use whichever enlarges a group.
  • PLA both arrays programmable; PAL fixed OR; ROM fixed AND; FPGA uses LUTs and needs no minimisation.

Minimisation is the single algebraic identity AB + A¬B = A, applied as often as possible. The Karnaugh map's only contribution is to arrange the truth table in Gray code so that every pair the identity applies to is physically adjacent — and then to wrap the edges, so the adjacency is complete.

The reason to bother is downstream. A sum of products maps directly onto an AND array feeding an OR array, so fewer terms is fewer rows of silicon. That stops being true the moment you reach an FPGA, where the truth table is simply stored — which is a fair summary of why this topic matters less than it used to, and still appears on the exam.
