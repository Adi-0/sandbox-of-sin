Synthesis and the Mixed Bench

Eight subtopics, two symbols. What looked like six unrelated skills was one escalation, each step adding exactly one thing the step before it could not do.

7.1 The module was one escalation

Start with a bit pattern. On its own it means nothing — Part 1 gave it meaning, and made the point that the same pattern is a different number in every interpretation, which is why 11111111 is either 255 or −1 and the question has to say which. Then:

  • Part 2 made patterns computable, and opened one gate to show that the algebra is transistors.
  • Part 3 gave computation a price, and a geometric method for paying as little of it as possible.
  • Part 4 gave the circuit a past, which is the only genuinely new physical idea in the module.
  • Part 5 put logic and memory in a loop, which is a machine — and the general form of every digital system there is.
  • Part 6 is different in kind. It is where the assumption underneath all five — that logic is instant and a flip-flop samples at a point — stops being true.

7.2 What carried over from earlier modules

Three debts collected
  • Mathematics Part 8's Boolean algebra → Part 2 in its entirety. The identities were derived there; this module only gave them symbols and De Morgan a pair of bubbles.
  • Electronics Part 3's MOSFETs → what a gate is. Two complementary networks, exactly one conducting, which is why CMOS burns nothing at rest and CV²f when it switches.
  • Mathematics Part 8's counting → every "how many states" and "how many flip-flops" question in Parts 4 and 5. It is 2ⁿ again, read in both directions.

7.4 The five mistakes that cost the most

Worth one last look before the bench
  1. Signed or unsigned? 11111111 is 255 or −1, and there is no way to tell from the bits. Read the stem for which it is, and remember the range is asymmetric — one more negative than positive.
  2. 2ⁿ against 2n. Counter stages multiply their divisions; they do not add them. A 6-bit counter divides by 64, not 12.
  3. Decoding N−1. A mod-N counter shows 0 through N−1, so it must be cleared on reaching N.
  4. Forgetting tsu. The clock period pays for the flip-flops at both ends of the path, not just the logic between them.
  5. The complementing rule between SOP and POS. SOP complements a variable that is 0 in the row; POS reverses it. This is the single most common error in minimisation.

7.5 Reading a stem quickly

Digital questions announce their method faster than any other area's. The classification worth practising:

  1. A bit pattern and a base, or the word "signed"? Part 1. Pure arithmetic, and the fastest marks in the module.
  2. A gate symbol, a truth table, or a bubbled input? Part 2. Check whether De Morgan turns it into something you recognise on sight.
  3. A Karnaugh map, "minimal", or "sum of products"? Part 3. Group the largest, let them overlap, and wrap the edges.
  4. A clock and a Q? Part 4. Read the present Q first, then the inputs — the characteristic equation needs both.
  5. Circles with arrows, or the words "detect the sequence"? Part 5. A state means how much progress has been made, and that alone answers most of these.
  6. Nanoseconds anywhere in the stem? Part 6. Decide first whether it is a setup question (the period is in it) or a hold question (it is not).
Where the marks actually are

Digital Systems is 8 to 12 questions of the 110, and it is the most recall-heavy area on the exam — which makes it the cheapest to prepare well. Number systems and counter arithmetic are pure recall with no derivation to get wrong; De Morgan and the characteristic equations are four lines each. An hour on those four things is worth more than a week on Karnaugh maps, which take longer per question and appear less often. Timing is the one area where the reasoning is genuinely conceptual, and it rewards knowing which check is being asked for more than it rewards arithmetic.

7.6 A set under exam conditions

Ten questions drawn from all six parts, at the real pace: 110 questions in six hours is 2 minutes 50 seconds each. The clock below runs at that rate. Treat anything you cannot classify within fifteen seconds as a flag — on the day, that is the one you come back to.

Two symbols, one operator set, and one clock. Everything in this module is those three things composed: a pattern given meaning, then computed with, then priced, then remembered, then given a purpose. Each part is the previous one plus exactly one new idea, and none of the ideas is difficult on its own.

What makes the subject work is the abstraction — that a wire is a 0 or a 1 and nothing in between, that logic happens instantly, that a flip-flop samples at a point. Part 6 is the bill for that abstraction, and the reason digital design is built around a clock: a clock does not make the timing problems go away, it collects them into one place where they can be checked. That is the same trade the module makes everywhere — accept a constraint, and get a whole class of problems back as arithmetic.
