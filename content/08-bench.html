Five neurons, labelled A to E. Each has its own input current and its own time constant. Any neuron can connect to any other — including itself — with any weight between −12 and +12 mV. The engine underneath is the same leaky integrate-and-fire model from Part 2, with the same 20 mV threshold and 2 ms refractory period you have been using all along.

Load a preset, press play, then start breaking things.

Six things worth trying

Each of these is a claim from an earlier part. The point is to check it yourself rather than take it on trust.

1 · Find the rheobase

Load One neuron. Drag A's current down through 0.20 nA. It does not fade out — it stops. Part 2.

2 · Break the chain

Load Relay chain, then drag A→B down to 7.5 mV. C goes silent and stays silent, no matter how long you wait. Part 4.

3 · Rig the election

Load Winner take all. Give C the largest current instead of A. Watch the winner change — then set all three equal and see what a tie looks like. Part 4.

4 · Make an oscillator

Load Half-centre oscillator. Two neurons, equal drive, mutual inhibition — and they alternate, with no clock anywhere. Weaken the inhibition to 3 mV and watch the rhythm dissolve into both firing at once.

5 · Widen a window

Load Coincidence detector. D fires only when A, B and C line up. Now raise D's τ to 50 ms and watch it get careless — it starts accepting inputs that are nowhere near simultaneous. Part 3.

6 · Build a memory

From One neuron: connect A to itself with +9 mV, then briefly raise A's current and drop it back to zero. If you tuned it right, A keeps firing on its own. You have built a latch out of one neuron.

Number six is the one worth spending time on, because it is the hardest to get right and the most surprising when it works. Too little self-excitation and the activity dies the moment you remove the drive; too much and A pins itself at its maximum rate forever, which is a latch you cannot switch off. The working band is narrow. Somewhere in there is a circuit that remembers one bit using no memory cell, no clock, and no stored state other than a voltage that keeps re-triggering itself.

That is worth sitting with. Everything conventional computing calls "state" — a register, a flip-flop, a memory cell — is here replaced by activity that sustains itself. Nothing is stored. The circuit remembers by continuing to happen.

The bench runs at half real time so you can see individual spikes, and connection delays are fixed at 2 ms. Weights apply instantly rather than through a synaptic current, exactly as in Figure 8. Nothing here learns — Part 5's plasticity is deliberately switched off, because watching weights move while you are also moving them is a good way to understand neither.

That's the guide

You started with a bucket and a hole. From that, in order: a memory with a deadline, a coincidence detector, a relay with a cliff in it, a circuit that makes decisions, a synapse that teaches itself, an array that multiplies without fetching, and a camera that only speaks when something moves.

None of it needed calculus, and none of it needed a clock.

The four numbers from Part 2 — 20 ms, 20 mV, 100 MΩ, 2 ms — were sufficient to build every circuit in this guide, and they are the same four numbers a neuromorphic chip designer configures per neuron.

If you can predict what the bench will do before you press play, you have understood the field guide.
