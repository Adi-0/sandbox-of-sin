The Mathematics of the FE Electrical and Computer Exam

By the end of these ten parts you will be able to look at any Mathematics question on the FE exam, name the tool it wants inside fifteen seconds, and execute it inside three minutes — because you understand where the tool came from, not because you memorised it.

What this is, and what it assumes

This is not a formula sheet. Formula sheets are what you get on exam day, free, and they do not help you, because a formula you cannot recognise the need for is a formula you do not have.

Instead we build the eight mathematics subtopics in the NCEES specification in an order that makes each one follow from the last. Part 1 establishes the right triangle and the logarithm. Part 2 lets that triangle rotate, which turns out to be what a complex number is. Part 3 gives equations shapes. Parts 4 and 5 handle many quantities at once — first as arrows, then as tables of numbers. Part 6 asks how those shapes change and how much they accumulate. Part 7 turns that into equations about change itself, and there the complex numbers from Part 2 come back and decide whether a circuit rings or dies quietly. Part 8 covers the counting, logic, and graphs that the computer half of this exam is built on. Part 9 shows how few ideas were really doing the work.

No background is assumed beyond high-school algebra: you can solve 2x + 3 = 11, and you know what a right triangle is. Logarithms, complex numbers, vectors, matrices, derivatives, integrals, and differential equations are all built here from the ground up. Nothing is used before it is defined.

The exam, in numbers

110 questions. Six hours. That six hours includes an eight-minute tutorial and an optional twenty-five-minute break, so the working time is about five hours twenty — roughly two minutes fifty per question, including reading the stem. It is multiple choice, four options, no partial credit, and the answers are usually phrased "most nearly," which is a hint: they expect rounding, and they expect you to be quick.

Mathematics is 11 to 17 questions. That ties it with Circuit Analysis as the largest single block on the exam. Plate 1 shows the whole blueprint.

Two things follow from that chart. First, mathematics and circuit analysis together can be a third of your exam, and mathematics is the one that also shows up inside the other sixteen areas — the phasor in Power Systems is a complex number, the Bode plot in Control Systems is a logarithm, the transfer function in Linear Systems is a differential equation wearing a disguise. Time spent here is spent twice.

Second, no single area is worth cramming to perfection at the expense of the rest. Quality over quantity applies to your studying too.

The one distinction that saves you a month

The FE exam is closed book with an electronic reference. During the exam you have the NCEES FE Reference Handbook open in a second window: a few hundred pages of formulas, tables, and constants, fully searchable.

Almost every formula in these ten parts is in it.

Which means the standard approach — flashcard the formulas, drill until they stick — is spending your most expensive resource on the one thing the exam gives away for free. What the handbook cannot give you is the knowledge that a particular formula is the one you need, and the fluency to run it without fumbling. That is the entire game.

So every formula in this guide carries a stamp:

StampMeansWhat to do about it
Handbook It is in the reference on exam day. Know that it exists, what it is called, and what shape of problem calls for it. Do not memorise the algebra.
Know cold It is not in the handbook, or finding it costs more than the question is worth. Memorise it. This list is much shorter than you fear — about twenty-five items across all of mathematics.
Why this is the whole trick

A student who memorises 200 formulas and cannot classify a problem scores worse than a student who memorises 25 and can. The first spends the exam recalling; the second spends it searching, which is faster, because the search box does not get tired.

One object, followed all the way through

Mathematics goes badly when every topic arrives as a stranger. So one object runs through all eight subtopics here, and you will meet it in Part 1 as something you already know: the 3-4-5 right triangle.

It becomes the complex number 3 + 4j in Part 2. It becomes the point (3, 4) on the circle x² + y² = 25 in Part 3, and the vector 3i + 4j + 12k in Part 4. It becomes a matrix whose eigenvalues are ±5 in Part 5. In Part 6 calculus hands back the same tangent line you drew in Part 3. And in Part 7 the numbers −3 ± 4j turn up as the roots that decide how a circuit behaves.

None of that is a coincidence arranged for effect — it is the same structure seen from eight sides, which is what mathematics mostly is. Recognising the object on its sixth appearance is what retention feels like from the inside.

How to use this

  • Read in order. Each part uses the last one. The order here is not the order NCEES prints its subtopics in, because that order is alphabetical, not pedagogical.
  • Turn the knobs. Every plate marked Interactive has exactly one control and one lesson. Ten seconds of play should be enough. If it is not, the plate has failed, not you.
  • Work the bench at the end of each part. The problems are generated fresh from a seed, so "New numbers" gives you a genuinely new set at the same difficulty rather than the same six questions again.
  • Run the Handbook Reflex drill. It gives you a stem and fifteen seconds to name the tool, with no arithmetic at all. It is the cheapest skill on this exam to improve and almost nobody practises it.

Arrow keys move between parts. Progress lives in this browser and nowhere else.

Mathematics is 11–17 of the 110 questions, and it hides inside most of the other areas too. The exam hands you the formulas; it does not hand you the ability to pick one. Train classification and execution, memorise only what is stamped Know cold, and let the handbook carry the rest.
