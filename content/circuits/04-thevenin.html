Thévenin and Norton

However many components are inside a box, if they are all linear then from outside it is one source and one resistor. Not approximately — exactly, and for every load you could ever attach.

4.1 The problem it solves

You have a network of a dozen resistors and two sources, and you want to know what happens when you connect a load. Then a different load. Then eight more, because you are choosing a value.

With Part 3's methods that is ten full solutions of the same network, each differing in one number. That is not hard, it is just wasteful — and on a timed exam, wasteful is the same as wrong.

Thévenin's theorem says you never have to. Solve the network once, with the load removed, and you get a two-component stand-in that behaves identically at those terminals forever after.

Trap · what "killing a source" means

A voltage source becomes a short circuit (0 V across it means a wire). A current source becomes an open circuit (0 A through it means a gap). People reliably get these the wrong way round. Reason it out rather than memorising: a source is killed by setting its own quantity to zero, and then you ask what component behaves that way. And only independent sources are killed — a dependent source stays, which is why that case needs the V_{oc}/I_{sc} route instead.

4.2 Doing it

Worked · a divider, Thévenised

A 100 V source feeds a 20 Ω resistor, and a 5 Ω resistor sits from that junction to ground. The load will hang across the 5 Ω. Find the Thévenin equivalent seen by the load.

  1. Open-circuit voltage. With no load, no current is drawn from the junction, so the 20 Ω and 5 Ω form a plain voltage divider: Vth = 20 V
  2. Thévenin resistance. Kill the 100 V source — replace it with a wire. Now the 20 Ω and the 5 Ω both run from the junction to ground, so from the terminals they are in parallel: Rth = 4 Ω Shorting the source is what turns a series divider into a parallel pair — that reversal is the step to get right.
  3. Check with the short-circuit current. Short the terminals and the 5 Ω is bypassed, so I_sc = 100/20 = 5 A, and 20/5 = 4 Ω ✓ Two independent routes to the same R_th.

So the entire network is 20 V behind 4 Ω. Attach any load you like and one division answers it. Attach 4 Ω and the load gets 10 V; attach 16 Ω and it gets 16 V; attach nothing and it reads 20 V.

4.3 Maximum power transfer

Now a question the equivalent makes easy and the original makes horrible. Which load resistance draws the most power?

Too small a load and it gets almost no voltage, because R_th takes it all. Too large and it gets almost no current. Somewhere between is a peak, and with the Thévenin form it takes one line to find:

On the worked example: R_L = 4 Ω and P_max = 400/16 = 25 W. Drag the load in Plate 31 and watch the curve.

The killer number — and why the grid ignores it

At the matched point the load and the source resistance carry the same current through the same resistance, so they dissipate the same power. Maximum power transfer runs at exactly 50% efficiency. Half of everything generated is burned inside the source.

For a radio receiver extracting a faint signal, that is the right trade. For a power station it would be catastrophic — so transmission systems are deliberately run far from matched, with source resistance as low as it can be made, accepting less than maximum power in exchange for efficiency in the high nineties. "Maximum power" and "maximum efficiency" are different targets, and the exam asks which one you were aiming at.

4.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • V_th is the open-circuit voltage; I_N is the short-circuit current; R_th = R_N = V_oc/I_sc.
  • To find R_th by inspection: voltage sources become shorts, current sources become opens, then look in from the terminals.
  • Only independent sources get killed.
  • Source transformation: V_th = I_N R, both ways, any time.
  • Maximum power at R_L = R_th, giving V_th²/4R_th — and 50% efficiency.

Any linear two-terminal network is one source behind one resistor. Find the source by opening the terminals, find the resistance by killing the independent sources and looking in, and check by shorting the terminals — three measurements, two of which suffice and the third of which verifies.

The equivalent turns "re-solve for every load" into one division, and it makes maximum power transfer a one-line result: match the load, accept 50% efficiency, and know why a power system refuses to.
