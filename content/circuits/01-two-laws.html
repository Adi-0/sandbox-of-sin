The Two Laws

Charge cannot pile up, and energy cannot appear from nowhere. Write those two sentences as equations and you can solve every circuit on this exam. Everything in the next five parts is a shortcut for doing it faster.

1.1 What a circuit question is actually asking

Circuit Analysis is 11 to 17 questions — tied with Mathematics for the largest block on the exam, and unlike Mathematics it is a topic where a methodical person beats a clever one. There is no trick you have to spot. There is a procedure, and the procedure always terminates.

Here is the whole of it. A circuit is a set of components joined by wires. At any instant you want to know two things: how much current is in each branch and what the voltage is at each node. Three facts get you there:

  1. Charge is conserved, so whatever flows into a junction flows out.
  2. Energy is conserved, so a round trip around any loop returns you to the voltage you started at.
  3. Each component has its own rule linking its voltage to its current — for a resistor, that rule is Ohm's law.

That is it. Series and parallel formulas, Thévenin equivalents, node analysis, phasors — all of them are bookkeeping that makes those three facts cheaper to apply to a big network. If you ever forget a shortcut mid-exam, you can still grind out the answer from the two laws. That is worth more than the shortcut.

1.2 Current, voltage, and what they measure

Current is the rate at which charge flows past a point — coulombs per second, which we call amperes. It is a flow, so it makes sense to ask which way it goes, and it makes no sense to ask what the current "at" a node is; current is in a branch.

Voltage is energy per unit charge — joules per coulomb, which we call volts. It is a difference between two points, always. A single point does not have a voltage until you name a reference, which is what a ground symbol does: it declares one node to be zero so every other node has a number.

The plumbing analogy, and where it stops

Current is the flow rate; voltage is the pressure difference driving it; resistance is the narrowness of the pipe. The analogy is genuinely load-bearing and this guide will reuse it — but it fails at capacitors and inductors, which have no clean plumbing counterpart, and it fails at the idea that current is the same all the way round a series loop. Water leaks; charge does not.

1.3 Kirchhoff's Current Law

A junction of wires — a node — has no capacity to store charge. Whatever arrives must leave, immediately and exactly.

1.4 Kirchhoff's Voltage Law

Walk a closed loop around a circuit, adding up every rise and drop, and you arrive back where you started — at the same voltage, necessarily, because it is the same point.

Trap · the passive sign convention

For a resistor, current enters the + terminal — the end it flows into is the higher-voltage end, because a resistor consumes energy. For a source that is delivering, current leaves the + terminal. Get this backwards and your power comes out negative. A negative power is not automatically wrong: it means that element is absorbing when you assumed it was delivering, which a battery being charged really does.

1.5 The circuit this module is built on

One network runs through the whole of Circuit Analysis, the way the 3-4-5 triangle ran through Mathematics. Here it is: a 100 V source, a 2 Ω resistor in series, and then 12 Ω and 4 Ω side by side.

Every number in it is a whole number, which is deliberate — you will be able to check both laws in your head at every step, and any arithmetic slip announces itself immediately.

Worked · the cast circuit, from the two laws alone

No series or parallel formulas yet — those come in Part 2. Just KCL, KVL and Ohm's law.

  1. Call the voltage at the junction V_p, with the bottom rail as our zero. KCL at that junction: the current arriving through the 2 Ω equals the two currents leaving through the 12 Ω and the 4 Ω. (100 − Vp)/2 = Vp/12 + Vp/4
  2. Multiply through by 12 to clear the fractions: Vp = 60 V
  3. Now every branch follows from Ohm's law: I2 = 20 A, I12 = 5 A, I4 = 15 A
  4. Check both laws. KCL: 5 + 15 = 20 ✓. KVL round the left loop: 100 − 40 − 60 = 0 ✓. Two independent checks, ten seconds, and they catch almost every mistake you can make in this topic.

Notice the shape of what we did: one unknown, one equation from KCL, then Ohm's law for everything else. That is node analysis, and Part 3 does nothing more than make it systematic for circuits with more than one unknown node.

The killer habit

Those two checks in step 4 cost ten seconds and are the highest-value seconds on the whole exam. Every branch current and every node voltage you produce can be verified against a law you did not use to derive it. On a multiple-choice exam where a wrong answer scores the same as a blank one, a free correctness check is worth more than a faster method.

1.6 Power, and where it goes

Add up what every resistor dissipates and it must equal what the source delivers — that is conservation of energy again, and it is a third free check.

In the cast circuit: the source delivers 100 × 20 = 2000 W. The 2 Ω dissipates (20)²(2) = 800 W, the 12 Ω dissipates (5)²(12) = 300 W, and the 4 Ω dissipates (15)²(4) = 900 W. Total: 2000 W ✓.

Trap · the bigger resistor is not the hotter one

The 4 Ω dissipates three times what the 12 Ω does, despite being three times smaller. In parallel, both see the same voltage, so P = V²/R makes the smaller resistor the hotter one. In series, both carry the same current, so P = I²R makes the bigger one hotter. Same components, opposite conclusion, depending only on how they are wired. Exam questions are built on exactly this reversal.

1.7 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • V = IR, and the three power forms P = VI = I²R = V²/R.
  • KCL: currents at a node sum to zero. KVL: voltages round a loop sum to zero. Neither is in the handbook in a form that helps you under time pressure.
  • A negative current or power means your assumed arrow was backwards. It is not a mistake and does not need redoing.
  • Current enters a resistor at its + terminal.
  • Always check KCL at a node and KVL round a loop before answering.
  • The cast: 100 V, 2 Ω, then 12 Ω ∥ 4 Ω → 20 A total, splitting 5 A and 15 A, with 60 V across the pair.

Two conservation laws run every circuit on this exam. Charge cannot accumulate at a junction, so branch currents there sum to zero. Energy per charge is single-valued, so voltages round a loop sum to zero.

Every technique in the parts that follow is a way of applying those two faster. None of them can give an answer the two laws would not — which is why checking against them is free, and why you should always do it.
