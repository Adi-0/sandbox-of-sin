Series, Parallel, and Dividers

Two shortcuts that collapse a network down to a single resistor, and two more that split what flows through it. Both pairs fall straight out of the two laws from Part 1 — which is why they are worth deriving once rather than memorising twice.

2.1 Series: the current has nowhere else to go

Components are in series when they are joined end to end with nothing branching off between them. That geometry has one consequence, and everything else follows from it: the same current flows through all of them, because there is no junction where it could divide.

Now apply KVL. The source voltage has to equal the sum of the individual drops, and each drop is IR with the same I:

V = I(R₁+R₂+R₃)

Compare that with V = IR_eq and the rule reads itself off: series resistances add. No memorisation required — it is one line of KVL.

2.2 Parallel: the voltage has nowhere else to be

Components are in parallel when both ends of each are tied to the same two nodes. That geometry also has one consequence: the same voltage appears across all of them, because their ends are literally the same two points.

Now apply KCL. The current arriving splits among the branches, and each branch takes V/R with the same V:

I = V(1/R₁ + 1/R₂)

So it is not the resistances that add in parallel — it is their reciprocals. The reciprocal of resistance is conductance, measured in siemens, and the honest statement of the parallel rule is: conductances add.

2.3 The thing that surprises everyone

A parallel combination is always smaller than the smallest resistor in it. Not the average — smaller than the smallest.

The plumbing picture from Part 1 makes this obvious rather than surprising. Adding a resistor in parallel is adding another lane to the road. Traffic gets easier, never harder, however narrow the new lane is. Turn the knob in Plate 27 and watch the two rules pull in opposite directions.

The killer number

Put a 1 MΩ resistor in parallel with a 10 Ω resistor and the combination is 9.9999 Ω — the megohm might as well not be there. Put the same two in series and you get 1 000 010 Ω, where the 10 Ω might as well not be there. Same two components, and which one matters is decided entirely by the wiring. In parallel the smallest wins; in series the largest does. That single sentence answers a surprising number of exam questions before any arithmetic starts.

2.4 Collapsing a ladder

Real networks are nests of series and parallel groups. The technique is mechanical: start at the end furthest from the source and work back, collapsing whichever group is unambiguous at each step.

  1. The 12 Ω and 4 Ω share both nodes, so they are in parallel: Rp = 3 Ω Note it came out smaller than 4 — as it must.
  2. That 3 Ω is now in series with the 2 Ω, so the source sees R_t = 5 Ω and delivers I = 20 A.
  3. The voltage across the parallel pair is (20)(3) = 60 V, so the branches carry 5 A and 15 A. Identical to Part 1's answer, in a third of the work.

This is why the shortcuts exist. It is also why Part 1 came first: when a network has no clean series or parallel groups — and Part 3 has one — the shortcut simply does not apply, and you fall back on the laws.

2.5 The two dividers

Having collapsed a network you usually need to go back and find one branch's share. Two formulas do that, and they are not symmetric — which is the single most examined trap in this whole topic.

Both are sanity-checkable in two seconds, and you should always do it: on the cast circuit, the 4 Ω branch is the easier path, so it must take more than half the current. The formula gives 20 × 12/16 = 15 A — three quarters. Correct. Had you used the resistor's own value you would have got 5 A, and the sanity check would have caught it.

Trap · "in parallel" is about nodes, not about drawing

Two resistors are in parallel when both ends connect to the same pair of nodes — not when they merely look side by side on the page. Exam figures are drawn to make this hard: a resistor tucked between two others often shares only one node with each, which makes all three a series chain rather than a parallel group. Trace the wire with a finger and label the nodes before you reach for a formula.

2.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Series: same current, resistances add. Parallel: same voltage, conductances add.
  • Product over sum, R₁R₂/(R₁+R₂), for two resistors only.
  • n equal resistors in parallel give R/n.
  • A parallel combination is always smaller than the smallest member.
  • Voltage divider: the resistor's own value on top. Current divider: the other one on top.
  • Check every divider answer against "does the easier path get more current?" before writing it down.

Series means shared current, so voltages and resistances add. Parallel means shared voltage, so currents and conductances add. Both rules are one line of KCL or KVL, which means you can rebuild either one if it slips.

The two dividers differ in exactly one place — which resistance sits on top — and the reason is that resistance opposes current but claims voltage. When in doubt, ask which branch is the easier path and check your answer against the question.
