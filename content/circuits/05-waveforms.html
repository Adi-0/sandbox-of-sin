Waveforms

A quantity that changes every millisecond still has to be quoted as one number. Which number, and why the answer is not the obvious one.

5.1 The vocabulary, in one pass

Everything so far has been DC: constant voltages, constant currents, nothing moving. Now the source is a sinusoid, and before anything can be calculated the terms have to be pinned down.

Phase is a shift along the time axis measured as an angle, because one full cycle is 360°. Two waveforms of the same frequency have a fixed phase difference forever; two of different frequencies do not have a phase relationship at all, which is why the phasor methods in Part 6 insist that everything share one frequency.

5.2 Which number do you quote?

A 60 Hz sinusoid is at its peak for an instant and at zero twice per cycle. There are four honest ways to summarise it, and they disagree.

The average over a whole cycle is zero for any symmetric waveform — the positive half exactly cancels the negative. That is a true fact and a useless summary, so when a problem says "average" for a sinusoid it almost always means the average of the rectified waveform, over a half cycle. Read the stem carefully; this is a deliberate ambiguity that the exam exploits.

5.3 RMS: the number that means something physical

Here is the question that settles it. A resistor does not care about the sign of the voltage — power is v²/R, and squaring kills the sign. So: what DC voltage would heat this resistor at the same rate as the AC waveform does?

That number is the root-mean-square value, and its name is the recipe read backwards: square the waveform, take the mean of that, then take the root.

The killer number

A domestic outlet is quoted as 120 V. That is RMS — the heating-equivalent value. Its actual peak is 170 V, and peak-to-peak it swings 340 V. Insulation, capacitor voltage ratings and semiconductor breakdown all have to survive the 170, while the power calculation uses the 120. Quote the wrong one and you either specify a part that explodes or one that costs three times what it needs to.

Worked · reading a waveform statement

A voltage is v(t) = 170 sin(377t + 30°) volts. Find its frequency, its RMS value, and the power it delivers to a 20 Ω resistor.

  1. The coefficient of t is ω, in rad/s. So f = 60 Hz and the period is 16.7 ms.
  2. 170 is the peak, because that is what multiplies the sine. So Vrms = 120 V
  3. Power always uses RMS — that is what RMS is for: P = 720 W Using the peak instead would have given 1445 W, exactly twice too much.

The 30° phase never entered. For a single source it sets only where the clock starts — it matters the moment there is a second waveform to compare with, which is Part 6.

Trap · which value is the stem giving you?

If a waveform is written as an equation, the number in front is the peak. If it is quoted as a bare voltage — "a 240 V supply" — it is RMS, always, by universal convention. If it says "peak-to-peak", halve it before doing anything else. Getting this wrong is a factor of \sqrt{2} or 2, and both wrong answers will be offered.

5.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • ω = 2πf, and that 60 Hz is 377 rad/s.
  • Vrms = Vm/√2 — for sinusoids only.
  • A square wave's RMS equals its peak. A triangle wave's is Vm/√3.
  • Rectified average is 0.637 Vm; the true average over a full cycle is zero.
  • Power always uses RMS values.
  • A quoted AC voltage is RMS; an equation's coefficient is peak.

A periodic waveform is summarised by its peak, its rectified average, or its RMS, and they are different numbers. RMS is the one that means something physical: it is the DC value that would heat a resistor at the same rate, which is why every power calculation uses it.

For a sinusoid RMS is peak over √2. For anything else it is not, and the exam knows you might not have noticed.
