Digital Communications

Part 4 justified the whole bandwidth expansion on one claim: that a repeater can decide what was sent and send it again exactly. This part looks at the decision — how it is made, what makes it fail, and the single bound that no amount of power, bandwidth or cleverness gets around.

5.1 A receiver only ever does one thing

Strip away the hardware and every digital receiver reduces to the same operation: it measures where the received signal landed, and it picks the nearest legal symbol. That is the decision. Nothing else in the receiver matters if this step is wrong.

So the reliability of a link comes down to a comparison of two distances: how far apart the legal symbols are, and how far the noise moves things. Everything in this part is a way of writing that comparison down.

5.2 Three ways to carry bits, and what each costs

A sinusoid still has only the three properties Part 3 listed, so there are only three things to modulate — and the digital versions are named for which.

  • ASK — amplitude shift keying. Simple, and vulnerable to exactly the noise that AM was.
  • FSK — frequency shift keying. Constant envelope, so a limiter works and a saturating amplifier is fine. Costs bandwidth, as FM did.
  • PSK — phase shift keying. The best of the three for a given power, because for the same average energy the symbols can be put further apart. This is why nearly everything modern is PSK or a descendant.

QAM is the descendant. Vary amplitude and phase and the constellation is free to be a grid rather than a circle, which packs points more efficiently — 16-QAM beats 16-PSK by about 4 dB at the same rate. The price is that the amplifier must stay linear, because the amplitude carries information again.

Why QPSK is the default choice almost everywhere

QPSK sends two bits per symbol and yet has exactly the same bit error rate as BPSK at the same Eb/N0 — not nearly the same, the same. Its two bits ride on cos and sin of the same carrier, which are orthogonal, so they do not interfere and the receiver separates them perfectly. It is genuinely two independent BPSK links in one channel. Half the bandwidth for nothing is not a trade-off, and that is why the burden of proof falls on anyone proposing something else.

5.3 The bound Part 4 deferred

Part 4 finished with M-ary signalling looking like a free lunch: raise the number of levels, divide the bandwidth by log₂M, and nothing appeared to be given up. Something is.

Nyquist's limit constrains the symbol rate and says nothing about noise, which is why it permits an unbounded bit rate as M rises. Shannon's constrains the information rate and does nothing but talk about noise. Put them side by side in the same units and the collision is exact:

Binary needs S/N = 3 (4.8 dB), 4-ary needs 15 (11.8 dB), 16-ary needs 255 (24.1 dB), 64-ary needs 4095 (36.1 dB). Every four-fold rise in M costs about 12 dB, and below that the scheme is not difficult — it is impossible, for any code, any receiver, any budget, for ever.

Worked · why the dial-up modem stopped at 33.6 kbit/s

A telephone line passes 3.4 kHz with a signal-to-noise ratio of about 30 dB. Find its capacity, and compare it with the 64 kbit/s the same line carries once digitised.

  1. Convert the SNR out of decibels first. S/N = 1000
  2. Shannon. C ≈ 33.9 kbit/s
  3. Against the standards. V.34, the last purely analog modem standard, reached 33.6 kbit/s — within one percent of the theoretical ceiling of the channel it ran on.
  4. Against PCM. The same line digitised carries 64 kbit/s, nearly twice as much.

Step 3 is worth sitting with: a commercial product got within 1% of an information-theory bound, which is close to unheard of, and it is why modem speeds stopped climbing rather than continuing to double. Step 4 looks like a contradiction and is not. The 64 kbit/s figure is the rate of a digital link that has replaced the analog channel entirely; the 33.9 kbit/s is what remains possible if you insist on sending sound down a line designed for sound. 56k modems worked by refusing the analog leg altogether — the downstream path was digital end to end, so it never paid the quantisation penalty, and the upstream direction stayed near 33.6. The way past Shannon's bound is always to change the channel, never to beat the bound.

5.4 What the bound does not say

Two misreadings are common enough to be worth naming.

  • Shannon is not a bandwidth limit in disguise. Letting B → ∞ does not give infinite capacity, because the noise power N = N₀B grows with it. The capacity converges, and the floor it converges to is Eb/N₀ = ln 2 = −1.59 dB — below that ratio no communication is possible at any rate, with any bandwidth.
  • Shannon says nothing about how. It is an existence result: codes achieving capacity exist, and it took fifty years to find practical ones (turbo codes in 1993, LDPC's rediscovery soon after). A scheme below the bound is permitted, not achieved.

5.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • The receiver picks the nearest legal symbol. Error rate is set by minimum distance against noise, and nothing else.
  • k = log₂M bits per symbol, and Es/N₀ = k · Eb/N₀. Dropping this factor is the most common error in the part.
  • BPSK and QPSK have the same bit error rate at the same Eb/N₀, and QPSK needs half the bandwidth. 9.6 dB gives 10⁻⁵.
  • PSK beats ASK and FSK for a given power; QAM beats PSK once M is large, at the cost of needing a linear amplifier.
  • Nyquist: C = 2B log₂M — bandwidth only, noise ignored. Shannon: C = B log₂(1 + S/N) — noise only, bandwidth priced.
  • Convert S/N out of decibels before using Shannon. 30 dB is 1000, not 30.
  • Setting them equal gives 1 + S/N = M². Binary 4.8 dB, 4-ary 11.8 dB, 16-ary 24.1 dB — about 12 dB per four-fold rise in M.
  • 3.4 kHz at 30 dB is about 34 kbit/s, which is why dial-up stopped at 33.6.
  • The absolute floor is Eb/N₀ = −1.59 dB. Infinite bandwidth does not give infinite capacity.

A digital link is a distance problem. Symbols sit at points; noise moves the received point; the receiver picks the nearest. Everything else — constellations, error rates, the choice between PSK and QAM — is a way of asking how far apart the points can be kept for a given power, and how often the noise gets across.

Above that sits the one hard wall in this compilation. Every other trade it has shown has an escape: more power, more bandwidth, a better filter, a cleverer code. Shannon's bound has none. It is the reason M-ary signalling is not free, the reason the dial-up modem stopped where it did, and the reason the answer to a channel that cannot carry what you need is always to get a different channel.

What is still missing is how many conversations share one link at once. Part 4 built a T1 out of twenty-four of them without saying how the twenty-four are kept apart. Part 6 is that question, and its answer is the last idea the module needs.
