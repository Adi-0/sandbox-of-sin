Amplitude Modulation

Part 1 ended on one line: multiplying by a carrier copies a spectrum to ±fc at half amplitude. Amplitude modulation is that line and almost nothing else — so this part is mostly about what the copies cost.

2.1 Why modulate at all

A microphone produces something in the range 300 Hz to 3.4 kHz. Radiating it directly is hopeless for two reasons, and they are both about the frequency being low.

  • An antenna must be a useful fraction of a wavelength. At 3 kHz the wavelength is 100 km, so a quarter-wave antenna would be 25 km tall.
  • Everyone would be on top of everyone else. Two stations radiating the same band at the same time are simply added together, and nothing at the receiver can separate them.

Both are fixed by moving the message somewhere else on the dial, and Part 1 already supplied the tool for that. Multiply by a carrier.

2.2 One number describes the whole thing

Why overmodulation is insidious

Past m = 1 the envelope passes through zero, so the measured minimum is 0 whatever the index actually is — and (max − 0)/(max + 0) is exactly 1. The scope formula therefore reports m = 1 for every overmodulated signal, and cannot distinguish 1.1 from 3. Set the plate above to any index over 1 and watch the readout do it. This is why transmitters limit modulation at the source rather than measuring it and hoping.

2.3 Where the power actually goes

Now the uncomfortable part. The carrier is the same sinusoid whatever the message does, so it carries no information at all — and in full-carrier AM it takes most of the power.

Worked · a broadcast transmitter

A 5 kW AM transmitter is modulated to m = 0.6 by a message that runs to 5 kHz. Find the total transmitted power, the power in each sideband, the efficiency, and the bandwidth.

  1. Total power. Pt = 5.90 kW
  2. Sidebands. Together they are the extra 0.90 kW, so each is 0.45 kW.
  3. Efficiency. η = 15.3% — so 5.0 kW of the 5.9 kW is the carrier, doing nothing.
  4. Bandwidth. 2fm = 10 kHz, which is exactly the channel spacing used for broadcast AM.

Step 3 is the one to sit with. Under a sixth of the transmitted power is carrying the programme, and that is a deliberate design choice rather than an oversight — the carrier is what allows a receiver to be a diode, a capacitor and a resistor. One transmitter pays so that millions of receivers can be cheap. Where that trade does not apply, the carrier goes.

2.4 Taking the carrier away

The two sidebands of a real signal are mirror images of each other, so one is redundant — that is what makes SSB possible, and it halves the occupied dial as well as recovering all the power. What both suppressed-carrier schemes give up is the simple receiver. With no carrier riding along, the envelope no longer resembles the message, so the receiver has to manufacture a carrier of its own at the right frequency and the right phase.

2.5 Getting the message back

Demodulation is the same multiplication run again. Multiply the received signal by a carrier a second time and Part 1's frequency-shift property moves each copy by ±fc once more: one copy lands back at baseband, and the others land at ±2fc where a low-pass filter discards them. The mixer that put the message up is the mixer that brings it back down.

And the shortcut, for full-carrier AM only

If the carrier is present and m ≤ 1, the envelope is the message plus a constant — so a diode and a capacitor recover it with no carrier regeneration at all. That is the entire reason full-carrier AM tolerates a 15% efficiency, and the entire reason it cannot tolerate m > 1: overmodulation is exactly the condition under which the envelope stops being the message.

2.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Modulation is Part 1's frequency shift. Sidebands at fc ± fm, each at mAc/2.
  • m = (Amax−Amin)/(Amax+Amin), read off the envelope.
  • BW = 2fm for AM and DSB-SC; fm for SSB.
  • Pt = Pc(1 + m²/2), each sideband m²Pc/4.
  • Efficiency = m²/(2 + m²), which is 1/3 at best, at m = 1.
  • It = Ic√(1 + m²/2) — do not forget the square root.
  • m > 1 is overmodulation: envelope reaches zero, carrier flips phase, and the scope formula uselessly reports exactly 1.
  • The carrier carries no information — it buys a cheap receiver.

Amplitude modulation is one multiplication, and every number in this part falls out of it. The message rides on the carrier's amplitude; the spectrum lands in two copies at fc ± fm; the index says how deeply the envelope swings and cannot exceed 1; and the power split is fixed by the index alone.

What is worth carrying beyond the formulas is the shape of the trade. Full-carrier AM spends most of its power on something that carries no information, in exchange for a receiver made of three components. That is a rational bargain when transmitters are few and receivers are many, and an absurd one otherwise — which is why point-to-point links use SSB and broadcasting does not. Part 3 changes what the message modulates: not the amplitude but the frequency, and it buys something amplitude modulation cannot offer at any efficiency.
