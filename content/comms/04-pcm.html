PCM and the Digital Link

Parts 2 and 3 sent a continuous message on a continuous carrier, and traded power against bandwidth to do it. This part throws the continuous message away first — replaces it with a stream of integers — and the trade changes shape entirely.

4.1 Three operations, two of them already yours

Pulse code modulation is sample, quantise, encode, and this compilation has already built two of the three. Signal Processing Part 2 owns sampling: what fs ≥ 2B means and what happens when it is violated. Electronics Part 6 owns quantisation: the step size, the error it leaves, and the 6.02n + 1.76 dB that follows. Neither is re-derived here. What is new is what happens when you put them in series and then have to transmit the result.

Two theorems, one name

Nyquist has a sampling theorem (fs ≥ 2B, about not losing a waveform) and a signalling theorem (a bandwidth B carries at most 2B symbols per second, about not smearing pulses into each other). They are different results and they appear at different ends of this chain — the first at the source, the second at the channel. Confusing them is the commonest way to get a PCM bandwidth question wrong by a factor of two or four.

4.2 The bill

Chain the four lines together and the cost is stark. A message occupying B hertz as analog, sampled at 2B and coded to n bits, needs

— exactly n times the bandwidth it started with. Eight bits, eight times the spectrum. Sixteen bits, sixteen times. The telephone case is worse than the ideal because of the sampling margin: 3.4 kHz of speech becomes 64 kbit/s, which wants 32 kHz of channel, a 9.4× expansion.

Set against Part 3 this looks terrible. FM spent six times the bandwidth to buy 14 dB. PCM spends eight or nine times to buy 50 dB — but the comparison is not the point, because the two are not buying the same thing.

4.3 What the expansion actually buys

Not signal-to-noise. Something analog modulation cannot offer at any bandwidth, at any power, by any technique.

A repeater that removes noise instead of amplifying it

Every analog link accumulates. An amplifier boosts the noise picked up since the last one along with the signal, so a thousand-mile route arrives with the sum of everything every span collected. Nothing fixes this; it is what amplifying a continuous quantity means.

A digital repeater does not amplify. It decides: the received voltage is nearer a one than a zero, so it transmits a clean, full-amplitude one. The noise from the previous span is not reduced — it is discarded entirely, and the signal leaving the repeater is bit-for-bit what left the transmitter. A thousand miles of regenerated line is as clean as one mile, until the noise gets large enough to flip a decision, at which point it fails abruptly rather than gradually. That cliff is what the bandwidth bought, and it is why the world's trunk networks went digital while the telephones on the ends were still analog.

Worked · a T1 carrier

A T1 line multiplexes 24 telephone channels. Each is sampled at 8 kHz and coded to 8 bits; each frame carries one channel's sample from all 24, plus a single framing bit. Find the frame length, the line rate, and the fraction of it that is overhead.

  1. Bits in a frame. 193 bits
  2. Frames per second. One sample from each channel per frame, and each channel is sampled 8000 times a second, so there are 8000 frames per second — the frame rate is the sample rate.
  3. Line rate. 1.544 Mbit/s
  4. Overhead. The payload is 24 × 64 kbit/s = 1.536 Mbit/s, so the framing costs 8 kbit/s — 0.52%.

Every number in that chain is exact, which is why 1.544 Mbit/s is worth recognising on sight — it identifies the whole system. Step 2 is where the mistakes happen: the frame rate is the sample rate, because a frame is one round of samples. Multiplying by 24 somewhere in there is the standard error. And note that the framing bit is not free but is very nearly so: half a percent buys the receiver the ability to find the frame boundary at all, without which the 24 channels cannot be told apart. Europe's E1 makes the other choice — 32 slots of 8 bits with no odd bit, 2.048 Mbit/s, spending two entire slots on framing and signalling rather than one bit.

4.4 The trouble with even steps

The 6.02n + 1.76 figure has a condition attached that is easy to read past: it is the SNR for a signal at full scale. A uniform quantiser's error is the same size whatever the signal does, so as the signal gets quieter the ratio falls with it, decibel for decibel.

At eight bits, full scale gets 49.9 dB. A talker 30 dB down gets 19.9 dB, which is audibly gritty. And a telephone carries mostly quiet talkers — loud and soft speakers differ by around 30 dB, and almost none of them are at full scale, because a system that clipped the loud ones would be worse than one that hissed at the quiet ones.

The fix is to compress before quantising and expand after — compress + expand = companding. Squash the loud parts of the range so the quantiser's evenly spaced levels land unevenly on the signal, crowded where quiet signals live and spread out where loud ones do. The receiver applies the inverse and gets the amplitudes back.

The plate makes the trade explicit. Companding gives up about 12 dB at full scale — a place the signal essentially never is — to gain about 18 dB thirty decibels down, where it lives. The crossover sits at −11.9 dBFS, and it does not move when the bit count changes: adding a bit lifts both curves by 6.02 dB together. The choice of law and the choice of resolution are independent decisions, which is a convenient thing to notice in a question that changes only one of them.

4.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Rb = n fs — bits per sample times samples per second. This is the most-asked line in the part.
  • Bmin = Rb/2 for binary, and Rb/(2 log₂M) for M levels. This is Nyquist's signalling limit, not his sampling one.
  • Binary PCM costs a factor of n in bandwidth: a Nyquist-sampled B-hertz message needs nB hertz of channel.
  • DS0 = 64 kbit/s (8 kHz × 8 bits). T1 = 24 × 8 + 1 = 193 bits × 8000 = 1.544 Mbit/s. E1 = 32 × 8 × 8000 = 2.048 Mbit/s. The frame rate is the sample rate.
  • SNR = 6.02n + 1.76 dB at full scale, and it falls a decibel per decibel as the signal drops.
  • Companding holds it flat instead: about 38 dB at eight bits, for both µ = 255 and A = 87.6.
  • µ = 255 ⇒ small signals get 1 + µ = 256× the resolution of large ones. µ-law is North America; A-law is Europe.
  • The point of digitising is the regenerative repeater — noise is discarded at every span rather than accumulated. Nothing analog can do this at any bandwidth.

PCM replaces a waveform with numbers, and pays for it in spectrum: a factor of n, straightforwardly, with no clever coding available to avoid it. What it buys is not a better signal-to-noise ratio for the money — FM is a better deal by that measure — but a different kind of link altogether, one where noise stops accumulating because every repeater makes a decision instead of an amplification.

Companding is the second idea, and it is a design decision rather than a law of nature. A uniform quantiser optimises the case that never occurs. Bending the scale so that the levels sit where the signal actually is costs 12 dB at a full scale nothing reaches and returns 18 dB where every conversation lives. That reasoning — optimise the typical case, not the extreme — recurs well beyond communications.

One thing has been quietly assumed throughout: that the repeater's decision is correct. It is not always. Part 5 asks what happens when noise flips a bit, which is where Shannon finally sets a limit that no amount of bandwidth or power can talk its way around.
