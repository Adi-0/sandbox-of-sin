Multiplexing

Part 4 built a T1 out of twenty-four telephone channels and never said how the twenty-four are kept apart. This part answers that — and the answer turns out to be one idea wearing three costumes.

6.1 A channel is a rectangle

Think of what a link actually offers as a rectangle: a range of frequencies, for a stretch of time. Multiplexing is the question of how to hand pieces of that rectangle to several users at once, and there are only three answers.

Worked · a 48 kHz group against a 1.544 Mbit/s T1

Both carry telephone conversations over one link. Compare what each wastes.

  1. FDM, twelve channels. Each 3.4 kHz voice channel is assigned a 4 kHz slot: so 15% is guard band.
  2. TDM, twenty-four channels. A frame is 24 × 8 + 1 = 193 bits, of which one is framing:
  3. The ratio. 15% against 0.52% — a factor of 29.
  4. And they scale differently. Double the FDM channel count and the waste is still 15%, because it is charged per channel. Double the TDM count and the framing bit is spread over twice as much payload, so the waste halves.

Step 4 is the important one and it is easy to read past. FDM's overhead is a percentage; TDM's is a constant divided by the traffic. One of those improves with scale and the other cannot, so as trunk capacity grew the comparison only got more lopsided. This is a rare case of one engineering choice simply dominating another rather than trading against it — which is why the analog hierarchy of groups and supergroups was replaced wholesale by the digital hierarchy of DS0s and T-carriers, and not merely supplemented by it.

6.2 The idea underneath all three

It is tempting to file FDM, TDM and CDMA as three unrelated tricks. They are one trick. Two signals can share a channel without interfering if they are orthogonal — if the integral of their product over a symbol is zero — and each scheme is just a different way of arranging that.

  • TDM makes the product zero by making one signal zero whenever the other is not. Disjoint in time.
  • FDM makes it zero because distinct sinusoids integrate to zero over a whole number of cycles — the same fact Signal Processing Part 1 used to extract Fourier coefficients.
  • CDMA makes it zero with signals that overlap completely in both time and frequency, and cancel anyway.

The third is the surprising one, so it is worth measuring rather than believing.

The cancellation is exact — in integers, not approximately. Walsh codes have inner product exactly N with themselves and exactly zero with each other, so a receiver multiplying the summed wire by one user's code and adding up the chips gets that user's bit while every other user vanishes term by term. The bars at the unused indices are dots on the axis, not small bars.

And then take the codes one chip out of step

The whole thing collapses. A receiver's window that is misaligned catches the tail of an interferer's previous symbol and the head of its current one — two symbols with unrelated bits, which is no longer anybody's code and is orthogonal to nothing. At eight chips, a single misaligned interferer can correlate 8 against a wanted signal of 8: interference exactly as strong as the signal. Worse, where the damage lands is unpredictable — sometimes the wanted user survives untouched and the energy appears on a code nobody is transmitting at all. Orthogonality is a property of aligned codes only, and that one caveat is most of what makes CDMA hard to build. It is why real networks synchronise chips tightly within a cell, and use merely near orthogonal PN codes between cells, where no common clock exists.

6.3 Two refinements worth recognising

  • Statistical TDM. Plain TDM gives every user a slot whether they are talking or not, and a silent user wastes it. Statistical TDM assigns slots on demand, which needs an address in each slot — more overhead per slot, far less waste overall. This is packet switching in embryo, and it is the argument that eventually replaced the T-carrier itself.
  • WDM and OFDM. Wavelength division multiplexing is FDM on a fibre, with colours for channels. OFDM is FDM with the guard bands removed: the subcarriers are spaced so that each one's nulls fall on its neighbours' peaks, so they overlap and remain orthogonal anyway. It recovers FDM's 15% and is the reason it appears in Wi-Fi, LTE and DVB.

6.4 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • FDM shares frequency, TDM shares time, CDMA shares neither — it overlaps and separates by code.
  • All three work by orthogonality: ∫sisj = 0 for i ≠ j. Disjoint slots, disjoint bands, or orthogonal codes.
  • FDM total = N × slot. 3.4 kHz voice in a 4 kHz slot is 15% guard, independent of N. Group = 12 × 4 = 48 kHz; supergroup = 60 × 4 = 240 kHz.
  • TDM rate = fs(Nb + overhead). T1 = 8000(24 × 8 + 1) = 1.544 Mbit/s, framing 1/193 = 0.52% — and it falls as N rises.
  • CDMA processing gain Gp = chip rate / bit rate = N, or 10 log₁₀N in dB.
  • CDMA needs chip synchronisation. Misaligned codes are not orthogonal, and one badly timed interferer can be as loud as the signal.
  • Statistical TDM allocates slots on demand and needs addressing. OFDM is FDM with overlapping but still-orthogonal subcarriers, so no guard bands.

Three schemes, one idea. Orthogonality is what lets signals share a channel, and time slots, frequency bands and spreading codes are three ways of arranging it. Seeing them as one thing is worth more than memorising three definitions, because the exam asks which is which and the answer is always in what is being kept disjoint.

The overheads are where the engineering lives. FDM pays a fixed percentage that never improves; TDM pays a constant that gets cheaper with scale; CDMA pays neither and is charged in synchronisation instead. None of the three is free, and each bill is presented in a different currency.
