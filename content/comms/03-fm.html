Angle Modulation

Amplitude modulation put the message in the carrier's height and paid for it with efficiency. Angle modulation puts it in the carrier's timing instead — and buys something amplitude modulation cannot offer at any price, in exchange for spectrum.

3.1 The other two things a sinusoid has

A sinusoid has an amplitude, a frequency and a phase. AM used the first. The other two are not really separate — phase is the integral of frequency, so nudging one necessarily nudges the other — which is why they are treated together as angle modulation.

3.2 A spectrum you cannot guess

AM's spectrum was a carrier and two sidebands, and you could draw it from memory. FM's cannot be drawn from memory at all. The sideband amplitudes are Bessel functions Jn(β), there are infinitely many of them, and they do not fall off monotonically — a distant sideband can be larger than a near one.

Set the index to 2.405 and the carrier disappears

J₀(2.405) = 0 exactly, so at that index there is no power whatsoever at the carrier frequency — all of it has moved into the sidebands. This is not a rounding effect or an artefact of the drawing; it is the first zero of a Bessel function, and engineers used it to calibrate deviation meters by turning the modulation up until the carrier vanished from a spectrum analyser. There is nothing in AM even loosely like this, and it is the quickest way to see that FM is a different animal rather than the same one with a different knob.

One more thing the plate shows that is easy to miss. The total power never changes. Whatever β does, the sum of all the sideband powers plus the carrier's stays at Ac²/2 — because the amplitude is constant, and power is amplitude squared. FM carves its sidebands out of the carrier; AM added them on top.

3.3 Carson's rule

A spectrum with infinitely many sidebands needs a convention before anyone can be assigned a channel. Carson's rule is that convention.

Worked · a broadcast FM channel

Broadcast FM allows 75 kHz of peak deviation and carries audio to 15 kHz. Find the modulation index and the bandwidth, and compare with the 200 kHz channel spacing actually used.

  1. Index. β = 5
  2. Carson's rule. BW = 180 kHz
  3. Against the allocation. Channels are spaced 200 kHz apart, so there is 20 kHz of guard band between one station's Carson bandwidth and the next channel's.
  4. Against AM. The same 15 kHz of audio by AM would need 30 kHz — so FM is spending six times the spectrum.

Step 4 is the trade stated plainly, and it is worth asking whether it is a good one. Six times the bandwidth buys roughly β² = 25 times the output signal-to-noise, which is about 14 dB — and it is why FM broadcast sounds the way it does and AM broadcast does not. Notice that no extra transmitter power was involved anywhere. The quality was bought with spectrum alone.

3.4 Where the immunity actually comes from

The mechanism is worth understanding rather than accepting, because it explains the limits as well as the benefit.

  • The envelope is constant, so amplitude variation is by definition not signal. A receiver can pass the incoming signal through a limiter that clips it into a square wave, discarding every amplitude change — and most noise arrives as amplitude change.
  • The capture effect. Two AM stations on one frequency both come through, mixed. Two FM stations do not: the stronger one is demodulated and the weaker is almost entirely suppressed, because the demodulator tracks whichever signal dominates the angle.
  • It has a threshold. Below roughly 10 dB of carrier-to-noise the advantage collapses abruptly rather than degrading gracefully — FM goes from clean to unusable over a couple of decibels, where AM fades slowly. This is why weak FM is silent and weak AM is scratchy.

3.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • FM: β = Δf/fm, so β falls as the message frequency rises. PM: β = kpAm, independent of fm. That is the distinguishing question.
  • Carson: BW = 2(Δf + fm) = 2fm(β + 1), holding ~98% of the power.
  • β ≪ 1 ⇒ BW ≈ 2fm (narrowband, same as AM). β ≫ 1 ⇒ BW ≈ 2Δf (wideband).
  • Sideband amplitudes are Jn(β), infinitely many, not monotonic. Carrier null at β = 2.405.
  • Total power is constant at Ac²/2, whatever β is. FM redistributes power; it never adds any.
  • SNR gain ∝ β². Bandwidth buys quiet.
  • Broadcast FM: Δf = 75 kHz, fm = 15 kHz, β = 5, BW = 180 kHz, in a 200 kHz channel.
  • Constant envelope ⇒ a limiter can discard noise, and gives the capture effect. It also gives a hard threshold.

Angle modulation keeps the amplitude constant and puts the message in the timing. Everything follows from that one choice. The spectrum becomes Bessel-valued and unbounded, so bandwidth needs a convention — Carson's. The power stops depending on the message, so modulation redistributes rather than adds. And because amplitude no longer carries anything, a receiver is free to throw amplitude away, which is where the noise immunity comes from.

Set against Part 2, the contrast is clean. AM is cheap in spectrum and expensive in power and quality; FM is the reverse. Neither is better — they sit at opposite ends of one trade, and which end you want depends on whether spectrum or transmitter power is the thing you are short of.

Both, though, are still analog: a continuous message on a continuous carrier. Part 4 takes the same telephone channel this compilation has been carrying since Signal Processing and turns it into numbers, which changes what the whole question is about.
