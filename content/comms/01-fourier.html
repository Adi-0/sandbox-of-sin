Fourier Transforms and Duality

Signal Processing Part 1 gave a repeating signal a spectrum made of spikes. A message does not repeat — that is what makes it a message — so it needs a continuous spectrum instead. This part supplies it, together with the one property that turns the rest of this module into bookkeeping.

1.1 Series to transform, in one step

A Fourier series works because a periodic signal only contains whole multiples of its own repetition rate, so the spectrum is a set of discrete lines spaced f₀ = 1/T apart.

Now stretch T. The lines move closer together. Let T go to infinity — which is what “this signal happens once” means — and the spacing goes to zero: the lines merge into a continuous curve. That curve is the Fourier transform, and nothing else about the idea has changed.

Read it once more. Multiplication by a cosine in time is a shift in frequency. Everything Part 2 says about AM, everything Part 6 says about frequency-division multiplexing, and the entire reason a radio has a tuning knob, is that one line. It is worth knowing before you know anything else in this module.

1.3 Duality is a budget, not a curiosity

Slide the width on the plate above and watch the two panels trade. Narrowing the pulse by a factor of two doubles the width of its spectrum, every time, for every shape. The product does not move:

τ · B ≈ constant

This is the most consequential sentence in the module, because time is what you are trying to save and bandwidth is what you have to buy. A shorter pulse means more bits per second and more hertz of spectrum, in strict proportion, and no cleverness in pulse shaping escapes it — different shapes only reach the limit more or less efficiently. The Gaussian reaches it exactly, which is why it turns up wherever spectrum is expensive.

1.4 What this costs a real link

The plate is worth working through with both patterns, because they say different things.

  • Mixed data survives surprisingly little bandwidth. The waveform stops looking like the data long before any bit is lost — and it does not need to look like the data, because a receiver samples once per bit and asks which side of a threshold it landed on.
  • Alternating 1010 is the honest test. It is the fastest thing a bit stream can do, and as a waveform it is a square wave at exactly half the bit rate. Below B = Rb/2 its fundamental does not get through at all, every sample sits precisely on the threshold, and the receiver has nothing to work with. At exactly Rb/2 the eye snaps open.

That boundary is Nyquist's signalling limit, and Part 5 writes it as a formula. Here it is simply what the arithmetic does. Note also what closed the eye: bandwidth, with no noise anywhere in the picture. Intersymbol interference is a separate failure from noise and it is not fixed by transmitting harder.

1.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Periodic ⇒ discrete spectrum. Aperiodic ⇒ continuous spectrum. Series and transform are the same idea at T → ∞.
  • rect ↔ sinc, with nulls at multiples of 1/τ. tri ↔ sinc². Gaussian ↔ Gaussian.
  • δ(t) ↔ 1 and 1 ↔ δ(f) — an instant holds every frequency, a constant holds one.
  • Scaling: compress in time by a, stretch in frequency by a. τ · B is constant.
  • Frequency shift: multiplying by cos(2πfct) copies the spectrum to ±fc at half amplitude. This is modulation.
  • Time shift changes phase only, never magnitude — delaying a signal cannot change which frequencies are in it.
  • A bit stream at Rb needs at least Rb/2 of bandwidth. Less, and the eye closes with no noise present.

The transform is the series with the repetition removed, and it buys a continuous spectrum in exchange. Two of its properties do nearly all the work in this module: scaling, which says time and bandwidth trade at a fixed exchange rate, and frequency shift, which says multiplying by a carrier moves a spectrum bodily to a new place on the dial.

Those two properties are the two halves of the rest of Communications. Parts 2 and 3 are the frequency shift — how to move a message onto a carrier and what it costs in spectrum. Parts 4 and 5 are the scaling — how fast you can push bits through a given bandwidth. And Part 6 puts many messages on one medium using whichever of the two is cheaper.
