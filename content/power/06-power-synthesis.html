Synthesis and the Mixed Bench

Four subtopics, one physical system. What looked like separate chapters was a single path from a generator to a shaft, and this is the shortest way to hold all of it.

6.1 The module was one plant

The specification lists power theory, transmission, transformers and machines as though they were four subjects. Following one plant through all of them shows what they really are: consecutive stations on the way from generation to torque, each one raising the question the next one answers.

  • Part 3 showed that moving power cheaply demands high voltage — so Part 4 had to explain the device that provides it.
  • Part 2 showed that three phases deliver constant power — which is what makes Part 5's motors turn smoothly.
  • Part 5 explained why the motor draws lagging current — which is the problem Part 1 opened with.

6.3 The five mistakes that cost the most

Worth one last look before the bench
  1. √3 versus 3. Line-to-phase is √3. Delta-to-wye impedance is 3. Three conductors in a loss calculation is 3. They are different threes.
  2. Which θ. In √3 VL IL cos θ it is the impedance angle. The 30° between line and phase voltage is already inside the √3.
  3. Nameplate ratio versus bank ratio. A Δ–Y bank of 20 : 1 transformers is 11.55 : 1 overall.
  4. Tangents, not cosines, in power factor correction.
  5. Poles, not pole pairs, in ns = 120f/p. Three pole pairs is six poles.

6.4 Order of operations, when a question stacks them

Power questions on this exam are often two or three steps wearing one stem. The reliable route is almost always the same:

  1. Get to watts. Horsepower × 746, or read the kW off the stem.
  2. Divide by efficiency if the number given was mechanical output. Electrical input is always the larger figure.
  3. Divide by power factor to reach volt-amperes, which is what the supply, the cable and the transformer are rated for.
  4. Divide by √3 VL to reach line current — the quantity everything physical depends on.
  5. Square it for losses, or use it directly for drop.

Steps 2 and 3 are the ones that get swapped. Efficiency acts on watts; power factor acts on volt-amperes. Keeping the units straight keeps the order straight.

Where the triangle ends up

The 3-4-5 triangle that arrived in Mathematics Part 1 as a trigonometry exercise has now been a complex number, an eigenvalue, a characteristic root, an impedance, a power triangle, and finally a plant drawing 5184 W, 6912 VAR and 8640 VA from a 208 V service. It was never a coincidence and never a teaching device borrowed for the occasion — a right triangle with a 53.13° angle is what a 0.6 power factor is.

6.5 Under exam conditions

Eight questions, drawn across all four subtopics, at the real pace of 2 minutes 50 seconds each. Nothing here needs a formula you have not already written down.

Power Systems is one path: generate, transmit, transform, distribute, correct, convert to torque. Each of its four published subtopics is a station along that path, and the questions the exam asks are almost always about the junction between two stations rather than about either one alone.

Eight expressions cover the area. The arithmetic is short. What the questions actually test is whether you can tell a line quantity from a phase one, a nameplate ratio from a bank ratio, and watts from volt-amperes — which is why reading the stem carefully is worth more here than speed.
