Three-Phase

Almost every power question on this exam is three-phase, and almost every wrong answer is a √3 in the wrong place. This part earns the √3 instead of quoting it, which is the only way it stays put.

2.1 Why three, and not one

A single-phase supply does not deliver power steadily. Voltage and current are both sinusoids, so their product pulsates at twice the line frequency and falls to zero twice per cycle. A single-phase motor is therefore being pushed in gusts, 120 times a second.

Now take three supplies of equal size, 120° apart, each feeding an identical load. Each one still pulsates. But the pulsating parts are 240° apart from one another, and three equal quantities spaced 120° around a circle sum to zero.

The total is constant, at every load angle. That is the reason for three phases — not copper savings, though those are real, and not tradition. Constant power means constant torque, which means a motor that does not shake itself apart. It is also why three, specifically: two phases would not cancel, and four would cost more to achieve nothing extra.

2.2 Two ways to connect three things

Given three impedances and three supply wires, there are exactly two sensible arrangements.

  • Wye (Y, star). One end of each impedance goes to its own line; the other three ends tie together at a neutral.
  • Delta (Δ, mesh). The three impedances form a closed triangle, with a line attached at each corner. There is no neutral.

Two vocabulary words carry the entire topic, and confusing them is the single most common error in FE power questions:

Phase versus line

Phase quantities belong to one of the three components — the voltage across it, the current through it. Line quantities belong to the three wires — the voltage between two of them, the current in one of them. They are not the same thing, and which ones coincide depends entirely on the connection.

2.3 Where the √3 actually comes from

Put a voltmeter between lines a and b. It does not read a phase voltage; it reads the difference of two of them:

Vab = Van − Vbn

Where 208 volts comes from

A standard commercial service is quoted as 208Y/120. Those are not two unrelated numbers: the 120 V is the phase voltage — the same 120 V outlet from Circuit Analysis Part 5 — and 120√3 = 207.85, rounded to 208, is what appears between any two lines. The odd-looking 208 has been √3 the whole time. Likewise 480Y/277, since 277√3 = 480.

2.4 The four relationships

Trap · which angle is θ?

In P = √3 VL IL cos θ it is tempting to read θ as the angle between the line voltage and the line current, because that is what the symbols sit next to. It is not. θ is the angle of the load impedance — the angle between the phase voltage and the phase current. The 30° from the previous section is already baked into the √3, and applying it twice is a wrong answer that will be on offer.

2.5 The plant, in full

Worked · the cast becomes a three-phase load

Three impedances of Z = 3 + j4 Ω, wye-connected, on a 208 V three-phase supply. Find the line current and the total power.

  1. Wye, so each impedance sees the phase voltage: Vφ = 120 V
  2. Each phase current, by Ohm's law with the impedance magnitude: Iφ = 24 A and in a wye the line current is the same 24 A.
  3. Total real power — three phases, each dissipating in its resistance only: P = 5184 W
  4. Cross-check with the line formula, which must agree: √3 (208)(24)(0.6) = 5184 W ✓
  5. And the rest of the triangle: Q = 6912 VAR, S = 8640 VA

5184, 6912, 8640 is 1728 × (3, 4, 5). The plant's power triangle is the triangle from Mathematics Part 1, at three times the scale of the single branch in Part 1 of this module. The power factor is still 0.6 lagging, because connecting three identical loads cannot change the angle of any one of them.

2.6 Converting between the two

A balanced delta and a balanced wye are interchangeable if their impedances are in a ratio of three:

Trap · which way round is the 3?

Half of all students write ZY = 3ZΔ. The check takes two seconds and never fails: a delta on a given line voltage is the heavier load, because every impedance sees the full line voltage instead of 1/√3 of it. Heavier load, more current, so to be equivalent the delta impedances must be the larger ones. ZΔ = 3ZY.

2.7 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Wye: VL = √3 Vφ, IL = Iφ.
  • Delta: VL = Vφ, IL = √3 Iφ.
  • S = √3 VL IL and P = √3 VL IL cos θ, both connections, with θ the impedance angle.
  • ZΔ = 3ZY, and delta draws three times the power of wye on the same line voltage.
  • 208Y/120 and 480Y/277 — recognise them as √3 pairs on sight.
  • The line voltage leads its phase voltage by 30° in a wye.

Three supplies 120° apart deliver constant total power, which is the whole reason the grid is three-phase. A line quantity and a phase quantity differ by √3 and 30°, and which one carries the factor depends on the connection: wye shares a neutral so the current passes through unchanged, delta shares the lines so the voltage does.

Every √3 in this subject is one vector subtraction of two phasors 120° apart. Redrawing that picture is faster and more reliable than recalling which formula the radical belongs in.
