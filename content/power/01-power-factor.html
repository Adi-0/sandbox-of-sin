Power Factor and What It Costs

Circuit Analysis ended with a power triangle. This module begins by attaching money to it. The angle you computed there is the reason electricity bills have a line item most engineers never learn to read.

1.2 The bill has two numbers on it

A utility charges a large customer for two different things.

  • Energy, in kilowatt-hours — that is P integrated over the month. It is what you actually consumed.
  • Demand, in kilovolt-amperes — that is S at its worst moment. It is what the utility had to build for you: conductor cross-section, transformer rating, switchgear interrupting capacity.

Those two are equal only at unity power factor. Everywhere else you are asking for infrastructure you do not use. The current in the cable is set by S, not by P, and the cable heats as the square of that current — so a poor power factor wastes real energy in the wires while consuming none in the load.

The cast arrives, at plant scale

Take one 120 V branch feeding the impedance from Circuit Analysis Part 6, Z = 3 + j4 = 5∠53.13° Ω, and scale the current up to a plant-sized 24 A:

P = 1728 W, Q = 2304 VAR S = 2880 VA

1728, 2304, 2880 is 576 × (3, 4, 5). The power factor is 0.6 lagging, the angle is 53.13°, and it is the same triangle it has been since Mathematics Part 1.

1.3 Why the angle exists at all

Reactive power is not an accounting fiction. An induction motor cannot produce torque without a magnetic field, and establishing that field takes energy that is returned when it collapses. A transformer needs a magnetising current whether or not anything is plugged into it. Both are inductive, both draw lagging current, and together they are most of the load on any real grid.

So Q is unavoidable — but it does not have to travel. That is the entire idea behind correction: supply the VARs locally instead of hauling them from the generating station and back sixty times a second.

1.4 Correcting it with a capacitor

A capacitor's reactive power is negative. Put one in parallel with the load and the two Q values subtract, while P — being in parallel too — is untouched.

Worked · correcting the branch to 0.9

The 1728 W load above runs at 0.6 lagging on 120 V, 60 Hz. Size a capacitor to bring it to 0.9 lagging, and find the new current.

  1. Both angles, from their power factors: θold = 53.13° and θnew = 25.84°.
  2. Their tangents: tan 53.13° = 1.3333, tan 25.84° = 0.4843.
  3. The capacitor supplies the difference: QC = 1467 VAR
  4. In farads, at 120 V and 60 Hz: C = 270 µF
  5. The new current follows from the new apparent power: Snew = 1920 VA, I = 16 A

24 A became 16 A. A third of the current is gone and the load does not know the difference — same 1728 W, same torque, same output. The copper loss in the feeder, which goes as I², is now (16/24)² = 4/9 of what it was: less than half.

Trap · correcting all the way to unity

It looks optimal and it is not what anyone does. Real loads vary through the day, and a bank sized for unity at full load over-corrects at light load — the power factor swings past 1.0 into leading, the current climbs again, and the capacitor can push the local voltage above nominal. Utilities target 0.9 to 0.95 lagging and stop. On the exam, a question asking you to correct "to unity" is legitimate arithmetic; one asking what is good practice wants 0.95.

1.5 Efficiency, and the horsepower conversion

Power questions on this exam very often arrive wrapped in mechanical units, because the load is a motor. Two conversions do all of it:

The order matters, and only one order is right

A 10 hp motor at 85% efficiency and 0.8 power factor needs 7460 W of shaft output, therefore 8776 W of electrical input, therefore 10 970 VA of supply capacity. Efficiency first, then power factor. Doing it in the other order gives the same answer here because both are divisions — but efficiency applies to watts and power factor to volt-amperes, and mixing up which is which is what produces the wrong option.

1.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • QC = P(tan θ₁ − tan θ₂) — the correction formula. Tangents, not cosines.
  • C = QC / (2πfV²).
  • 1 hp = 746 W.
  • Correction changes S, Q, current and losses. It never changes P.
  • Utilities bill energy on P and demand on S. The gap between them is the power factor.
  • Over-correction produces a leading power factor, which is a fault, not a bonus.

Real power is what a load consumes; apparent power is what the system must be built to deliver. A power factor below one means those two differ, and the difference is paid for in conductor size, transformer rating and I²R heating along every metre of the path — none of which shows up as useful output.

A parallel capacitor supplies the reactive power locally so it no longer has to be carried. It is the cheapest intervention in power engineering, and the arithmetic is one subtraction of tangents.
