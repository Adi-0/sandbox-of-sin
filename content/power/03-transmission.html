Transmission and Distribution

Everything so far assumed the supply arrives at the load unchanged. It does not. The wire between them has impedance, and one consequence of that fact decides the shape of every power grid on earth.

3.1 The line is a component

A conductor running any real distance is a resistance in series with an inductive reactance — resistance from the metal, reactance from the magnetic field around it. Both sit between the source and the load, and both are carrying the full load current.

So the voltage at the far end is not the voltage at the near end:

VS = VR + I(Rline + jXline)

Trap · the drop is not I·R

With the plant's 24 A through 0.5 Ω of line resistance, IR = 12 V. The actual drop is 16.8 V. The difference is the reactance, and the reason it matters so much here is the load angle: at 0.6 lagging the current is 53° behind the voltage, which rotates jXI into near-alignment with VR so almost all of it adds to the magnitude. A lagging load makes voltage drop worse than the resistance alone predicts. Correcting the power factor fixes it twice over — less current, and a smaller angle.

3.2 Voltage regulation

Utilities need one number for "how much does the voltage at this point sag when the load comes on." That number is voltage regulation:

For a line with no source impedance of its own, the no-load voltage is just the sending-end voltage — nothing is flowing, so nothing drops. That makes regulation a direct measure of what the line's impedance costs you at full load.

What counts as acceptable

Standards generally hold service voltage within about ±5% of nominal. A regulation figure of 14%, like the plate's one-kilometre case, would be unacceptable in practice — it is drawn that way so the effect is visible. Real feeders are sized so the drop lands near 2–3%, which usually means a fatter conductor, a shorter run, or a higher distribution voltage.

3.3 The fact the grid is built around

Here is the argument in three lines. Deliver a fixed real power P at line voltage VL and power factor cos θ. The current required is

IL = P / (√3 VL cos θ)

and the loss in the conductors is 3 IL² R. Substituting:

Ploss = P²R / (VL² cos²θ)

Loss falls as the square of voltage

Everything in that expression is fixed by the job except VL. Double the transmission voltage and the loss quarters. Multiply it by twenty and the loss falls by four hundred. Nothing else in power engineering offers a lever like that, and it is the entire justification for the grid's structure: generate at medium voltage, step up for the journey, step back down at the end.

Note the cos²θ in the denominator too. Power factor enters squared — the same lever as Part 1, and the reason correction pays for itself on a long feeder.

3.4 What it costs to go high

If high voltage is free efficiency, why is anything distributed at 208 V? Because voltage costs money in every direction other than loss:

  • Insulation thickness scales with voltage, in cables, transformers and switchgear alike.
  • Clearances — the physical air gap to anything else — scale with it, which is why transmission towers are tall and substations are large.
  • Switching and protection get dramatically harder: interrupting a fault at 138 kV is a different engineering problem than at 480 V.
  • Nothing uses it. Motors, lighting and electronics all want hundreds of volts, not thousands.

So the grid steps down in stages, each one trading a little more loss for a lot less apparatus, until the last transformer on the street delivers the 120 V that started this module.

Worked · a feeder, end to end

A three-phase feeder delivers 300 kW at 4160 V and 0.85 power factor lagging. Each conductor has 0.8 Ω of resistance. Find the line loss and the transmission efficiency.

  1. Line current, from the three-phase power relation rearranged: IL = 49.0 A
  2. Loss in all three conductors — the 3 is not optional: Ploss = 5760 W ≈ 5.76 kW
  3. Efficiency: η = 98.1%

Now repeat it mentally at 480 V instead of 4160. The current becomes 425 A, the loss becomes 433 kW — larger than the load. The line would not deliver the power at all; it would melt. That is what the inverse square looks like from the wrong side.

3.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • Ploss = 3 IL² R for three-phase. Three conductors.
  • Ploss ∝ 1/V² at fixed delivered power — and ∝ 1/cos²θ as well.
  • VR% = (Vnl − Vfl)/Vfl × 100 — divide by the full-load value.
  • VS = VR + IZ, as phasors. The drop is not I·R.
  • Service voltage is normally held within about ±5%.

A transmission line is a series impedance carrying the load current, so the far end always sits lower than the near end, and the shortfall depends on the load's angle as much as on the line's resistance.

Delivering fixed power means current is inversely proportional to voltage, and loss is proportional to current squared — so loss is inversely proportional to voltage squared. Every structural decision in a power system, from the height of a tower to the location of a substation, follows from that one exponent.
