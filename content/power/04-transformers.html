Transformers

Part 3 ended with a problem: transmission wants high voltage and everything else wants low voltage. The transformer is the entire answer, and it is the reason alternating current won.

4.1 Why this device decided the argument

There is no simple, efficient, static way to change a DC voltage. There is for AC: wind two coils on a shared magnetic core, and a changing current in one induces a voltage in the other, in proportion to the turns. No moving parts, and efficiencies above 98% in any decent unit.

That single fact settled the nineteenth-century argument over which kind of current the grid would use. Without a transformer, Part 3's inverse square law is unusable — you would be stuck generating, transmitting and consuming at the same voltage.

4.2 The ideal transformer

Reflected impedance, with the cast

The plant's phase impedance is 5 Ω. Behind a 4160 : 208 transformer — a turns ratio of 20 — the source does not see 5 Ω. It sees

Z1 = 400 × 5 = 2000 Ω

Check it the long way: 4160 V produces 2.08 A on the primary, and 4160/2.08 = 2000 Ω. The transformer has not changed the load; it has changed what the load looks like from the other side. Electronics uses the identical trick to match a loudspeaker to an amplifier, and Part 5 uses it to refer a motor's rotor circuit to its stator.

Trap · which way is a?

A "480 : 120 transformer" has a = 4 with the 480 V side as the primary — but nothing stops you energising the 120 V side instead, and then it is a step-up with a = 1/4. Read which side the source is on before writing anything. The physical check never fails: the high-voltage winding always has more turns and carries less current.

4.3 Real transformers

Two loss mechanisms, and they behave completely differently with load:

  • Core loss (iron loss) — hysteresis and eddy currents in the steel. It depends on voltage and frequency, so it is essentially constant whenever the transformer is energised, load or no load. It is what an open-circuit test measures.
  • Copper loss — I²R in the windings. It goes as the square of load. It is what a short-circuit test measures.
Why efficiency peaks partway up

One loss is fixed and the other grows as load squared, so efficiency is maximised where copper loss equals core loss — typically at 40–70% of rated load, not at full load. Distribution transformers are deliberately designed for peak efficiency well below their rating, because that is where they actually spend their lives.

Transformers are rated in kVA, not kW, and the reason is now obvious: the two loss mechanisms are driven by voltage and by current, and neither cares about the angle between them. A 100 kVA transformer can deliver 100 kW at unity power factor or 60 kW at 0.6 — the winding is at its limit either way.

4.4 Three-phase connections

Three single-phase transformers (or one three-limbed core) make a three-phase bank, and each side can be wired wye or delta independently. That gives four combinations, and they do not all have the same voltage ratio.

Worked · sizing a bank

Three single-phase transformers, each rated 25 kVA at 2400 : 240 V, are connected delta–wye. What is the bank's rating and its secondary line voltage?

  1. Bank rating is just the sum — the connection does not change how much each transformer can carry: S = 75 kVA
  2. Primary is delta, so each transformer's primary winding sees the full line voltage, 2400 V. Its secondary therefore produces its rated 240 V — as a phase voltage, because the secondary is wye.
  3. Secondary line voltage picks up the √3: VL2 = 416 V

The overall ratio is 2400/416 = 5.77 : 1, not the 10 : 1 printed on each nameplate. That gap is the √3, and reading past it is the single most common transformer error on this exam.

4.5 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • a = N1/N2 = V1/V2 = I2/I1 — voltage follows turns, current inverts.
  • Z1 = a²Z2. Squared.
  • V1I1 = V2I2 for an ideal transformer — the cross-check on every answer.
  • Δ–Y multiplies the ratio by 1/√3; Y–Δ multiplies it by √3; matched connections leave it alone.
  • Core loss is constant, copper loss goes as load squared, and efficiency peaks where they are equal.
  • Transformers are rated in kVA.

A transformer trades voltage for current at constant apparent power, which is what makes Part 3's inverse square law usable and what made alternating current the winner. Because it changes voltage and current in opposite directions, it changes impedance by the square of the ratio — the most useful consequence of the device and the one most often left out.

In three-phase banks the nameplate ratio belongs to one transformer, not to the bank. Mismatched connections leave a √3 uncancelled, and delta–wye is the workhorse precisely because that extra √3 and the neutral it provides are both wanted.
