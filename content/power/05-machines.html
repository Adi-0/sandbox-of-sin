Motors and Generators

Where the power has been going the whole time. Motors are roughly half of all electricity consumed, and the plant this module has followed since Part 1 is one — which is also the answer to a question left open there.

5.1 One idea, three machines

Every rotating machine here works the same way: a magnetic field rotates, and something on the rotor is dragged along by it. What separates the three families is how the rotor gets its own field.

  • Synchronous — the rotor is fed DC and becomes a magnet. It locks to the rotating field and turns at exactly its speed. Every large generator on the grid is one.
  • Induction — the rotor has no supply at all; the rotating field induces current in it. Cheap, rugged, and the overwhelming majority of motors in service.
  • DC — the field is stationary and a commutator switches the armature current so the torque stays in one direction.

5.2 Synchronous speed

The rotating field's speed is set by two things only: the supply frequency, and how many magnetic poles the stator is wound for.

The four numbers to have by heart

At 60 Hz: 2 poles → 3600 rpm, 4 poles → 1800, 6 poles → 1200, 8 poles → 900. At 50 Hz they become 3000, 1500, 1000 and 750. A nameplate reading 1750 rpm is a four-pole induction motor at about 2.8% slip — recognising that on sight saves a calculation on exam day. And an induction motor's nameplate speed is never a round number, precisely because it is never synchronous.

5.3 Why an induction motor cannot reach synchronous speed

The rotor of an induction motor is a short-circuited cage with no connection to anything. Its current exists only because the stator's rotating field sweeps past it and induces one. If the rotor ever caught up, the field would be stationary relative to it, nothing would be induced, no current would flow — and there would be no torque at all.

So the motor must slip. It settles at whatever speed produces exactly the torque the load demands, and heavier loads settle further below synchronous.

5.4 What slip costs

Slip is not merely a speed error. It is a fixed fraction of the power thrown away as heat, and the relationship is exact:

Trap · a stalled motor

At standstill s = 1, so Protor = Pag — every watt crossing the air gap becomes heat in the rotor, and none of it becomes shaft power. Meanwhile the stator draws its full locked-rotor current, typically six times rated. That is why a jammed motor destroys itself in seconds, and why overload protection exists.

5.5 The DC machine

DC machines are simpler to describe and now much rarer, but they remain on the specification and the arithmetic is short.

Worked · the plant's motor

The plant is a four-pole, 60 Hz induction motor drawing 5184 W with a nameplate speed of 1764 rpm. Stator losses are 250 W and windage 100 W. Find the slip, the rotor loss, the shaft output and the efficiency.

  1. Synchronous speed, from poles and frequency: ns = 1800 rpm
  2. Slip: s = 2%
  3. Air-gap power is the input less the stator's share: Pag = 4934 W
  4. The rotor loses exactly the slip fraction of it: Protor = 98.7 W
  5. Shaft output is what remains after windage: Pout = 4735 W = 6.35 hp
  6. Efficiency: η = 91.3%
The question Part 1 left open

This module began with a plant running at 0.6 power factor lagging and treated that as a given. Here is why. An induction motor must magnetise its air gap before it can produce any torque at all, and that magnetising current is almost purely reactive — it is drawn whether the motor is loaded or not. A fully loaded motor might reach 0.85; a lightly loaded one falls to 0.5 or below, because the real component shrinks with the load while the magnetising component does not.

So the reactive power that Part 1's capacitor was buying back was the air gap of this motor, being charged and discharged sixty times a second. Oversized motors running light are the single largest cause of poor industrial power factor — and the cheapest fix is often to fit a smaller motor rather than a capacitor.

5.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • ns = 120f/p, with p poles, not pole pairs. 60 Hz: 3600 / 1800 / 1200 / 900 for 2 / 4 / 6 / 8 poles.
  • s = (ns − n)/ns and n = (1 − s)ns.
  • fr = sf — the rotor sees the difference frequency.
  • Protor = s·Pag, so Pmech = (1 − s)Pag.
  • T = P/ω, with ω in rad/s (ω = 2πn/60).
  • DC: Ea = kφω and T = kφIa, same k.
  • An induction motor never reaches synchronous speed. A synchronous machine runs at nothing else.

A rotating field's speed is fixed by frequency and pole count. A synchronous machine locks to it; an induction machine must lag it, because induction requires relative motion and torque requires induced current.

That lag is slip, and slip is expensive in an exact way: the rotor dissipates precisely the slip fraction of everything crossing the air gap. It is also why induction motors draw lagging current — the air gap has to be magnetised before any of this can happen, and that magnetising current is reactive whether the motor is working or idling.
