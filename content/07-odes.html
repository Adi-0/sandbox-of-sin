Differential Equations

What you write down when you know the rule for how something changes but not the thing itself. And the part where the complex numbers from Part 2 come back and decide whether a circuit rings.

7.1 Why engineering produces these by accident

You rarely set out to write a differential equation. You write down physics and one appears.

The current through a capacitor is i = C dv/dt. The voltage across an inductor is v = L di/dt. Both of those relate a quantity to its own rate of change, so the moment you apply Kirchhoff's laws to a circuit containing either one, you have a differential equation: an equation whose unknown is a function, and which involves that function's derivatives.

Its order is the highest derivative that appears. One capacitor or one inductor gives first order. One of each — an RLC circuit — gives second order, and that is where all the interesting behaviour lives.

7.4 The cast, one last time

Take y″ + 6y′ + 25y = 0. The characteristic equation is r² + 6r + 25 = 0, and:

r = −3 ± 4j

There it is. The discriminant went negative, the square root of a negative number was not a failure but an answer, and out came −3 ± 4j — the cast, in the complex plane where Part 2 left it.

Read the two parts off directly. The solution is

y = e^(−3t)(C₁cos4t + C₂sin4t)

The magnitude of −3 + 4j is 5 — the hypotenuse of the 3-4-5 triangle you drew in Part 1. Its angle from the negative real axis is 53.13°, and cos 53.13° = 0.6 is the damping ratio.

The triangle from Part 1 is the thing that decides whether this circuit rings. Not a metaphor for it, not an analogy — the same three numbers, doing the work.

The killer comparison

Set the damping to 10 and the response never crosses zero — it eases back to rest from one side and stops. Set it to 6 and the same circuit swings straight past zero and back again before it dies. One coefficient moved by four; nothing in the equation looks meaningfully different. But the discriminant changed sign, the roots left the real axis, and the behaviour changed category. That sign is the design decision behind every damper, every filter and every control loop you will ever tune.

Honest simplification

Everything here assumes constant coefficients and a linear equation. Real components drift with temperature and saturate at high current, and neither is linear. The FE exam stays inside the linear constant-coefficient world, and so does this part — but the assumption is an assumption, not a law.

7.6 What to memorise from this part

Know cold

  • y′ = ky ⇒ y = Ce^{kt}. Every RC and RL transient is this.
  • To get the characteristic equation, replace each derivative with a power of r. Second derivative becomes r².
  • The three cases, by discriminant: two real roots → overdamped, repeated → critically damped, complex pair → underdamped and it rings.
  • For complex roots α ± jβ: the real part is the decay, the imaginary part is the ringing frequency.
  • The repeated-root case needs the extra factor of t in the second term.
  • y = y_c + y_p — and check whether the question wants both halves or only the complementary one.

A differential equation is a rule for change; solving it means finding the function the rule describes. For constant coefficients, guessing an exponential turns the whole problem into a quadratic — so the characteristic roots are the answer in compressed form.

Where those roots sit is the behaviour. On the real axis the system crawls back to rest; off it, the imaginary part is a ringing frequency and the real part is how fast the ringing dies. The 3-4-5 triangle from Part 1 turns out to be exactly the root that separates the two.
