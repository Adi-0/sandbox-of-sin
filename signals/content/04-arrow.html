Part 3 left you with a machine that always works and never explains: flip, slide, multiply, add, and no way to see what the answer will look like before you compute it. This part starts the escape, and the escape begins with an odd question.

Is there a signal a room cannot mangle? Something you could put in and get back, changed only in the two most boring ways a signal can change — a bit bigger or smaller, a bit earlier or later — but never reshaped?

There is exactly one family, and you have already met it.

Figure 10 puts a pure tone into the same room from Figure 6 and plots what comes out. Drag the frequency anywhere between 20 Hz and 1600 Hz. The output gets smaller and slides later — and it is still a sine wave at the same frequency. It never develops a kink, never grows a wobble, never becomes anything else.

The grey dashed trace makes the claim testable. It is not the output; it is the input, multiplied by one number and slid along by another. The orange curve is the room's differential equation, integrated step by step from a standing start. Two completely different calculations, and one curve on the screen.

Compare that with Figure 9, where a square wave went in and something visibly different came out. A sinusoid is the exception, and the exception turns out to be the whole subject.

Why it has to be true

The reason is a shape fact, not an algebra fact.

Press play on Figure 11. An arrow of fixed length turns at a steady rate; a horizontal line runs from its tip to the graph on the right; the graph traces a sine wave. That is not an analogy. A sine wave is the height of something going round in a circle. All the rise and fall you see on the right is produced by a thing that never changes size and never changes speed — it merely points in a different direction as time goes on.

Now recall Part 2. A time-invariant system does the same thing at every instant; it has no calendar. Feed it something whose only change over time is which direction it points, and there is nothing for the system to get hold of. It cannot treat one moment differently from another, because from the signal's point of view every moment is the same moment, rotated. So the only freedoms left are: shrink the arrow, and turn it. Which is exactly what the readouts in Figure 10 report.

A signal that comes out of a system as a scaled copy of itself is called an eigenfunction of that system — from the German for "own" or "characteristic". Sinusoids are the eigenfunctions of every LTI system there is. Not of some systems: of all of them, always, as a direct consequence of the two promises.

You put a 400 Hz tone into an unknown LTI box and a 400 Hz tone comes out, twice as tall. What can you conclude about a 900 Hz tone?
It will come out at 900 Hz, unchanged in shape, because every sinusoid is an eigenfunction of every LTI system. But how much it shrinks or grows is a different number for every frequency — the room in Figure 10 passes 20 Hz almost untouched and cuts 1600 Hz to a fifth. That table of numbers-versus-frequency is the object Part 6 is built on.

Two numbers, and why we glue them together

So a system's whole effect on a tone is two numbers: how much it shrinks it, and how far it slides it. Those get names.

The gain is the ratio of output height to input height — 0.847 at 100 Hz for our room. The phase is how far the output has slid, measured in degrees of a cycle, so that −45° means "a whole cycle is 360°, and this arrived an eighth of a cycle late". Phase is quoted in degrees rather than milliseconds for a good reason: a fixed time delay is a different fraction of a cycle at every frequency, and the fraction is what determines whether two tones add up or cancel out.

Now, carrying two numbers around separately is tolerable for one tone. In Part 5 you will be carrying them for hundreds at once, and multiplying them together, and the bookkeeping becomes unbearable. So they get glued into one object.

A complex number is an arrow: a length and a direction. That is all it is, and Figure 12's left panel is the whole definition. The length is the gain, the angle is the phase, and the standard way to write it is M·ejφ — length M, angle φ.

The middle panel of Figure 12 is the only reason the notation is worth learning. Multiply two arrows and the lengths multiply while the angles add. That is precisely what happens when a signal goes through two rooms in a row: the gains multiply, the delays add. An operation that takes two sentences to describe in words becomes one multiplication sign.

ejωt = cos(ωt) + j·sin(ωt)
Why it looks like this. It is Figure 11, written down. The left side is "an arrow of length 1, turning at rate ω". The right side is its two shadows: how far across it reaches, and how far up. Nothing mystical is being claimed — the equals sign is saying that a rotation and a pair of shadows are two descriptions of one thing. The letter j is the electrical engineer's i, borrowed because i was already spoken for by electric current.

The symbol ω is the rotation rate in radians per second, and it is worth thirty seconds of care because it is the unit this subject actually runs on. One full turn is 2π radians, so a tone at f cycles per second turns at ω = 2πf radians per second. Our 100 Hz square wave has a fundamental at ω = 628 rad/s. Our room's corner sits at exactly ω = 1000 rad/s, which is f = 159 Hz — a round number in the honest unit and an awkward one in hertz, which tells you which unit the physics prefers.

Strictly, the eigenfunction of an LTI system is the complex exponential ejωt — the whole spinning arrow — not the real sine wave, which is only its shadow. Everything in this guide works with real signals and real measurements, and the shadow behaves exactly as advertised because a real sine is the sum of two arrows spinning in opposite directions. That is a detail you can safely carry as a footnote rather than a burden.

A sinusoid is the height of a fixed-length arrow turning at a steady rate — which is why a system with no calendar cannot reshape one. Sinusoids are the eigenfunctions of every LTI system.

All a system can do to a tone is scale it and slide it: a gain and a phase. Glued together they make one complex number, and complex numbers multiply by multiplying lengths and adding angles — which is exactly how systems in series combine.
