Two industries, no new mathematics. Everything in this part is a rearrangement of things you already have.

Why a radio station has a frequency

A voice occupies roughly 0 to 5 kHz. Two people speaking into two microphones produce two signals in the same band, and adding them together gives a mess that no filter can separate — the same permanent overlap as Figure 27.

So the problem is: how do a thousand stations share one sky?

The answer is one line of trigonometry that you can also read straight off Figure 12. Multiplying a signal by a cosine at frequency fc takes its whole spectrum and sets it down at ±fc. Nothing is distorted, nothing is lost. The spectrum is simply moved.

x(t) · cos(2π fc t) ⟷ ½X(f − fc) + ½X(f + fc)
Why it looks like this. A cosine is two arrows spinning in opposite directions at fc. Multiplying by a spinning arrow adds its rate to everything in the signal — angles add, from Figure 12 — so one copy of the spectrum slides up by fc and the other slides down. This is modulation, and it is the entire reason wireless communication is possible.

Figure 29 shows the AM band with four fixed stations and one you can move. Every hump is the same voice spectrum from the left-hand edge, relocated. Slide yours onto a neighbour and the readout turns red — that is a collision, and it is a real one: the two signals become inseparable in exactly the way Part 9's overlapping copies did.

Give each station a 10 kHz channel and the licensed AM band from 530 to 1700 kHz holds 117 of them simultaneously, with the same aerial, in the same air, at the same instant. The receiver's job is then just Part 8: a bandpass filter tuned to one carrier, and everything else falls away. The tuning knob on an old radio was moving a pole.

Press the listen button for the shape of it. You hear a plain 300 Hz tone, then the same tone riding on a carrier — the pitch you hear is the carrier, and the message is the wobble in its loudness. A real AM station uses a carrier a thousand times higher and utterly inaudible; the demo brings it down into the audio band so the shape is hearable, which is a distortion of scale and not of principle.

Why does an AM station occupy 10 kHz of band when the voice only reaches 5 kHz?
Both sidebands. The equation above puts a copy at fc + f and another at fc − f, so a 5 kHz message occupies 5 kHz either side. The two carry identical information, which is wasteful — and noticing that gives you single-sideband, which throws one away and fits twice as many stations in the same sky. Amateur and marine radio have used it for decades.

Why your car holds its speed on a hill

Now the other half, and it is Figure 23 wearing different clothes.

A car with cruise control is three lags in series. The engine takes about ten seconds to deliver a change in torque. The car's mass takes a second or so to respond to that torque. The speed sensor and its filtering lag by another half second. Three lags — the exact plant from Part 7, with names on it.

Figure 30 sets the target at 100 km/h and puts a hill at sixty seconds. One slider: how hard the controller reacts to being wrong.

At K = 1 the car cruises at 50 km/h — half the target — and sags badly on the hill. At K = 10 it manages 91 km/h and holds most of its speed on the climb. At K = 30 it is within 3 km/h but visibly wallows, hunting up and down for half a minute after every disturbance. Past about K = 35 the poles cross the line, and the car surges violently between speeds forever. Exactly Figure 23, exactly the same cliff, at exactly the same gain — because it is the same three poles.

Now look at what the car never does: reach 100. A controller that acts only on the present error must keep an error in order to keep pushing. Zero error would mean zero throttle, and zero throttle on a level road means slowing down.

K = 1

50% error. Placid, useless.

K = 10

9% error. Sensible.

K = 30

3% error. Wallowing.

K = 40

0% error, briefly, on the way past.

The cure is to add a term that accumulates the error over time, so that any persistent shortfall keeps building throttle until it is gone. That is the I in a PID controller, and a real cruise control has one. Adding an integrator is adding a pole at s = 0 — which you can do on the Bench in one drag, and watch the steady-state error vanish while the stability margin gets worse. Nothing here is a special case; it is all the same map.

Both examples are simplified in the same honest direction: they assume linearity over the whole operating range. A real engine's torque is nowhere near proportional to throttle at the extremes, and a real transmitter clips. Both designs work anyway because they are kept in the region where Part 2's first promise holds — and both fail in exactly the way Part 2 predicted when they are not. An overdriven transmitter splatters energy into its neighbours' channels, which regulators fine people for.

Multiplying by a cosine moves a spectrum to the carrier and nowhere else. That is modulation, and it is the whole reason a thousand stations fit in one sky — with a Part 8 bandpass filter at the far end to pick one out.

Feedback is Part 7's map with a purpose: more gain means less error and less margin. A proportional controller must keep an error to keep pushing, and curing that means adding another pole.
